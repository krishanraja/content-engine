I've been following how money is changing because of AI. I'm Krish.

I've been digging into Suno, the app that makes songs with AI. Ask it for a song about your dog's birthday. Its AI writes one and sings it back to you. The record labels say Suno learned to do that by copying their recordings without asking. So when a fan makes a song with AI, who gets paid and who is left waiting?

That matters beyond music, because right now the price of training AI is being set in public. So I wanted to break it down for you.

More than 100 million people have used Suno, it says. The labels sued. Then one of them changed sides.

First, the engine. The AI behind Suno is like the engine under the bonnet of a car. Suno trains that engine on songs. That's where the money fight starts.

Universal and Sony sued Suno in June 2024. They went to a federal court in Massachusetts, over 560 recordings. Warner had its own case against Suno too.

Then, in November 2025, Warner settled. A settlement is when two sides agree to end a court fight. Warner went further, though. It became Suno's partner. The deal was new engines in 2026, built with permission. The old ones get switched off.

On 9 September 2026, Suno launched its new engine, called v6. Suno says it was made with its partners, including Warner and two other music firms, BMG and Believe. Since that day, those three get a share of Suno's money.

The partners even chose which of their music the new engine studied. Jack Brody runs product at Suno. He told the news site Music Business Worldwide that v6 was trained "entirely from scratch". He said its data doesn't include Universal or Sony.

Universal and Sony kept going. In August 2026, Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV said no to adding 61,026 more recordings to their first case. He wants that case decided on one big question, which I'll come to. But he noted they could sue again, separately. So on 18 September, they did. It's a new case in Boston, over 60,202 recordings. Warner isn't on it.

Same app. Same songs. Two opposite bets.

So let's follow the money from one fan's song. I count five stops, and the trail gets fainter at every one.

Stop one: the fan pays Suno. Under Warner's deal, free songs can be played and shared. But to download a song, you need a paid account, and paid plans have a monthly cap. Pay, and Brody says "you own the music". Lovely.

Stop two: Suno collects. By its own count, Suno had 2 million paying subscribers in February. It put its yearly pace of subscription money at $300 million. I'd treat both as Suno's claim, until it shows audited accounts. On 1 October, its boss, Mikey Shulman, said Suno is now "far beyond" both. He gave no new numbers. A growth claim with no number attached is just a feeling.

Stop three: Suno passes on "a portion". That's Brody's phrase. From launch day, a portion of Suno's money goes to its partners. Then those partners decide how to share it out. Two small words, carrying a lot of weight.

Stop four: the labels decide what the artists get. Suno pays the label. My read is the label then pays the artist, under the artist's own contract. After Universal's AI deals in 2025, a law firm called Manatt listed what artists still didn't know. When the money reaches them. How they could check it. Whether they'd be told how often their music gets used. And whether it just pays off what they already owe their label.

Stop five is everyone else. Warner's deal promises artists and songwriters "full control" over the use of their names and voices in new AI music. Believe says artists who agree to take part will be paid. Sony and Universal are still suing. Their road to Suno's money runs through a courtroom, or a deal. And investors get paid if Suno grows. A round led by Bond Capital put over $400 million into Suno. That was at a $5.4 billion price for the whole business. Suno says some musicians invested too. So a few of them could get paid as owners.

Think of a wedding band booked through an agency. The couple pays the venue. The venue pays the agency. What reaches the drummer depends on a contract the couple never sees. Suno is the venue. The label is the agency. And the artist is the drummer.

Each side moved for its own reason. A licence is a paid permission to use someone's music. Suno pays for one because its old engines got it sued. My read: a licence swaps a court risk for a running cost. Warner's boss, Robert Kyncl, said Suno is growing fast, so Warner grabbed the chance to help shape its engines. Warner also sold Suno a concert-finding site called Songkick.

And Universal and Sony keep suing. I think a lawsuit is also a way to set a price. Universal settled a similar fight with Udio, another AI music app, in October 2025. Part of that deal was licences. So Universal sells AI licences to Udio, while it sues Suno. Funny old world.

Now to the ruling everyone in music is reading. On 29 September 2026, a US appeals court ruled for Thomson Reuters against ROSS Intelligence, a legal research firm. ROSS had taken short summaries of court rulings from Thomson Reuters. It used them to train a rival legal search engine.

ROSS argued fair use, which is a US rule. It lets you copy someone's work without asking, in some cases. It's how a film critic can quote a line from a film. The court said ROSS's use "was not fair". It also said the market for selling training rights to AI is "rapidly developing". On 5 October, Music Business Worldwide tied that ruling to Suno.

There are two catches, though. The first catch is that this ruling doesn't bind the judge in the Suno cases. He sits in Massachusetts, under a different appeals court. He can find it persuasive. He doesn't have to follow it.

The second catch is in a footnote. The court said the ROSS AI "cannot generate original expression". Music Business Worldwide put it simply. ROSS didn't make new things, and Suno's engines do. So the two cases aren't twins.

And the fair use question isn't expected to reach Judge Saylor until the second half of 2027. That's according to Music Business Worldwide.

This next bit is my favourite. In paragraph 97 of their new case, Universal and Sony turn Suno's deals against it. They say Suno takes its partners' recordings to train on, and pays the people who supply them. They call that "a functioning licensing market".

Suno sees it differently. Brody has said the money isn't in exchange for training, and that the deals aren't really about the data.

So why would the labels want to prove a market exists? My bet is that if paying for training is normal, then copying without paying looks like skipping the bill.

Every payment Suno makes to Warner does two jobs. It makes Suno look legit. It also hands Sony and Universal a receipt to wave in court. It's like settling a fence row with one neighbour, then watching the other two bring your cheque to court.

Then there's the $9 billion figure you might have seen. Copyright is the law on who owns a work. In the US, it lets a judge award between $750 and $30,000 for each work copied. If the copying was deliberate, it can go up to $150,000 a work. At that top rate, Music Business Worldwide worked out a "theoretical maximum" for the new case. It came to just over $9 billion. At the bottom rate, it's a small slice of that. The judge picks where to land.

The labels also say a new engine doesn't wipe the slate clean. They claim training v6 on the output of Suno's older engines "launders" the copying. They call v6 the fruit of "the same poisoned tree". That's only their claim so far. But if you built a business on Suno, you'd still have to answer it.

So who gets paid?

Here's where I land. The money is real as far as the labels. Past the labels, it goes dark. The artist's share is set by label contracts nobody has published. Suno's deals are a pipe to the labels. What comes out of the label's tap is the label's business.

And the plans for artists are still fuzzy. Suno talks about products where artists can choose to take part, and get paid when they do. On the fan remix plans, the tech news site The Rundown AI reports no firm launch date yet. There's no price for fans, and no formula for paying artists.

If you make songs on Suno, check who owns the result before you use it in anything you sell. Brody says the rights differ once you remix a real artist. If you're an artist on a label, Manatt's questions are yours to ask. Does this just pay off what you already owe?

That $5.4 billion price for Suno needs one more thing to come true. The rest of these court fights have to end in licences. Warner's ended in a partnership. Universal's fight with Udio ended in licences. My bet is Suno's fight with Universal and Sony ends the same way. Both sides have a reason to want it. Suno faces a ceiling of just over $9 billion in the new case alone. The labels carry a risk too. A judge could call training fair use. And a licence nobody needs is hard to sell.

So here's our prediction. By 31 December 2027, Universal Music Group or Sony Music Entertainment will have announced a deal with Suno. That's a settlement or a licensing deal.
