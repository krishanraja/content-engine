I've been following how money is changing because of AI. I'm Krish.

So I've been having a little play around with Muse, Meta's new AI agent. An agent is an AI that does jobs for you, and one of Muse's jobs is shopping. Within two weeks of its launch, Amazon shut the door on it. Shopify threw its door wide open. So when an AI agent does your shopping, who actually gets paid? That question matters to anyone who shops online, so I wanted to break it down for you.

The dates first, because this moved fast. Meta launched Muse on 8 September 2026. Meta says that when it's time to pay, "Muse can checkout" for you. By the night of Sunday 20 September, people using Muse on Amazon hit a warning popup. Amazon called it an "unauthorized AI agent".

The very next day, Shopify went the other way. Its boss, Tobi Lütke, posted on X that Shopify was "partnering deeply with Muse". So Muse can now pay with Shop Pay, Shopify's one-tap checkout, on every Shopify store.

One agent. Two opposite answers. I think the reason is simple. It comes down to what each company gets paid for.

Amazon first. Amazon took in 716.9 billion dollars in 2025. It earns from its online stores, and from fees on the sellers who use its site. It earns from Prime, and from AWS, its cloud business.

Then there are the adverts. Those brought in 68.6 billion dollars in 2025. Brands bid to have their products shown in your search results. Amazon gets paid when someone clicks an advert, or simply sees one.

I find it easiest to picture Amazon as a giant supermarket. Brands pay to sit on the eye-level shelf, the one you see first. But that only pays off if a person walks down the aisle and looks.

Shopify works differently. It gives shops the tools to run their own online store. Then it takes a fee when they make a sale. In 2025, Shopify brought in 11.6 billion dollars. And 76% of that came from what it calls merchant solutions, which are mostly fees on payments. On the basic plan in the US, a shop pays from 2.9% plus 30 cents on each online card payment.

In supermarket terms, Shopify is the till. And the till doesn't care which shelf you walked past. It only cares that you paid.

Now think about an AI agent as a shopper. Honestly, it's the dullest shopper you've ever met. It has a list and it sticks to it. It never gets tempted by a sale on garden furniture. It does the browsing for you, and you only see what it picks.

For Amazon, that's a problem, because those listings are the shelf money. Amazon even said so, in a court case against another AI company, Perplexity. It said its advertisers pay for their ads to be "shown to humans".

Here's the bit I find most telling. Amazon builds AI shopping agents itself. Its own one is now called Alexa for Shopping. Amazon says shoppers can use it to "ask questions", hunt for deals, fill a basket and make regular buys. It tells advertisers they can join in through sponsored ads. Their adverts are "automatically eligible" to show up there. Amazon even says shoppers who click a Sponsored Prompt buy 48% more often. They spend 21% more, too.

So here's how I read it. Amazon likes AI shopping agents when they're its own, with its shelf still inside. Muse walks straight round the shelf.

Amazon's boss, Andy Jassy, has said he expects Amazon to work with outside agents over time. He's also said what he thinks of them today. Their delivery dates are often wrong, he says, and so are their prices.

For Shopify, the sums run the other way. An order an agent places through Shop Pay earns Shopify the same fee as any other online order. Shopify's president, Harley Finkelstein, told investors this in August. He said the money works exactly like a normal online order. Then he added: "There's no new fees." More agent orders mean more money for Shopify. No wonder Shopify now lets its shops sell through ChatGPT, Google's Gemini and Meta's checkout.

And one more twist, which I love. Meta is itself a Shopify customer. It's named in Shopify's own yearly report.

Of course, Amazon gives a different reason. It says this is about its rules. The popup said an unauthorised AI agent breaks Amazon's Conditions of Use. And it said Amazon's customers have agreed to those rules.

Since 30 May 2025, those rules have included "Agent Terms". They say no AI agent may use Amazon unless it says what it is, at all times. In plain English, I'd put it like this. Tell us you're an AI agent, and ask first.

Amazon also told GeekWire that Muse doesn't say it's an agent while it browses. And The Next Web reported that Muse appears to capture and store customer logins. Meta says Muse can't see people's passwords or how they pay. It says any login you share goes into "secure storage", so Muse can use it without seeing it. I think both can be true at once. The login is stored, and Meta says the AI can't see it.

Then there's a court fight, and it matters here. Perplexity also has a shopping agent, and Amazon took it to court. Early on, Amazon won an order against Perplexity. On 4 August 2026, a US appeals court cancelled it. The judges said Amazon was unlikely to win its main claim. Their reason was that the user, not Perplexity, got into Amazon, using the AI as a tool. The case goes on. Amazon sent the court an updated version of its case on 21 September.

So here's my guess, and it is a guess. The rules are Amazon's tool. The advert money is what the tool protects.

So who carries the risk when this goes wrong? The answer depends on who you are, and on which side of the till you stand (buying, or selling).

If you shop with Muse, read the small print. Muse's terms say you're "solely responsible" for what it does. You carry all the risk of losing money. And some purchases "may be irreversible", which means you can't undo them. If something goes wrong, the most Meta has to pay you is 250 dollars. Or whatever you paid Meta in the last 12 months, if that's bigger.

There is a safety net. If you pay with Stripe's Link, Meta says Muse is the first AI agent covered by Link's purchase protections. Those pay up to 500 dollars a claim for items that are damaged, lost or stolen.

Watch for paid influence, too. Meta says Muse shows no adverts today, Digiday reports. Amazon's own assistant does show them. And a UK government paper on AI agents and shoppers gives a warning. People may be steered towards things that are more profitable, but less suited to what they need.

If you sell on Shopify, selling through Meta's checkout is switched on from the start for eligible stores. When someone buys that way, your Google Analytics and tracking pixels don't fire. You can't ask the customer to sign in first. And the order is still yours, so returns are still your job. Shopify says there are no extra fees beyond the normal cost of taking a payment. That could change. Mark Zuckerberg has said Meta plans to charge a commission in the end, CNN reported. That's a cut of sales made through Muse. Digiday reports the shop would pay it.

If you sell on Amazon, remember who the adverts are for. They're paid to be "shown to humans". An agent that does the browsing skips your advert too. And the price of those adverts is in court as well. On 31 August 2026, the US Federal Trade Commission and 22 states sued Amazon. They say it secretly raised what brands and sellers paid to advertise. Amazon called the case "misguided".

The article sets out three guesses for where this lands, and I'll go through each one.

Guess one. Amazon wins in court, and keeps every agent that doesn't say what it is off its site. Amazon keeps all its shelf money. Every company building shopping agents loses.

Guess two. Amazon opens a proper front door instead. Agents are welcome, as long as they say what they are and behave. Amazon would still show them sponsored ads on the way through. It keeps most of its shelf money, and the companies building agents get a way in.

Guess three. The courts side with the shopper, and agents simply go round Amazon. They go straight to the shops that welcome them. Shopify wins twice. It keeps the till fee, and it gets the shoppers Amazon turned away.

I'm betting on guess two, and the reasons are already on the record. Amazon already runs sponsored ads inside its own agent. Jassy says he expects to work with outside agents. And the appeals court's reading makes a total ban harder to hold.

If you're building in this space, that's where I'd look. The strongest business is the front door that shops can choose to open to agents. The one paying is likely the shop, or the platform it sells through. And the trap is borrowed access, because a marketplace can change its rules and close the door.

So here's my call. By 30 June 2027, Amazon opens an authorised route for shopping agents, and that route still shows them sponsored listings. On the day, I'll mark it held, broke or unclear. Misses go up as big as hits.
