Here's how it works. You type an idea or upload a photo. You pick a camera move or a style, and it makes the clip. It said it had 30 million users in August. That was the month investors valued it at 5.4 billion dollars.

Now a twist. OpenAI used to have its own video engine, called Sora. In AI, a model is just an engine like that, the thing that makes the pictures. Before OpenAI shut Sora down, Higgsfield was its biggest customer by spend and use. That's according to Tech Times.

OpenAI switched off its Sora 2 models on 24 September 2026. It closed its Videos service for app makers the same day. And that same month, Higgsfield's founder, Alex Mashrabov, told a podcast they'd "crossed $1 billion". He meant a year's worth of sales, at the current pace.

That needs explaining. So I took it apart.

Why did Sora close in the first place? OpenAI said its Sora team would now work on "world simulation research", to move robotics forward. It also said demand for computing power was growing. The Wall Street Journal reported that Sora was costing OpenAI about 1 million dollars a day to run. Its active users had dropped below 500,000, down from around one million. OpenAI gave app makers six months' notice. It told them on 24 March 2026.

The article has a picture of Higgsfield as a stack of five layers, each stamped real or theatre. At the top are the tricks people pay for. Under that is the chooser, which picks the engine in over 40% of jobs. Then the engines, mostly other companies' models. Then the billion. And at the bottom, the claim of no paid ads, stamped theatre. I'll go through each one.

The billion first. I'd call it real, with small print. On the same podcast, Mashrabov explained the sum. He takes the last 4 weeks of sales and multiplies by 13. So it's a pace for the year ahead, worked out from four weeks. Still, a billion-a-year pace buys a lot of popcorn.

Next, the engines. Real, and mostly rented. Higgsfield's own list of video models for app makers reads like a who's who of other people's AI. Seedance belongs to ByteDance. Wan is Alibaba's. Hailuo is MiniMax's, and Grok Imagine is xAI's. Kling and Google's Veo 3.1 are on the menu too.

Higgsfield does build a few models of its own. There's DoP, which turns a still photo into a moving shot. And there's Soul, for fashion-style pictures. But most of the heavy machinery belongs to someone else.

Now the chooser. For me, this is the clever bit, and it's real. Mashrabov told the podcast that Higgsfield picks the model itself in over 40% of cases.

Think of a travel agent choosing your airline. You still get to Lisbon. They just pick the plane that suits them.

That matters because of what each engine leaves behind for Higgsfield. On its own models and open ones, Mashrabov says it keeps over 80% of each sale. On closed models, he says it's probably between 20 and 30%. You can see why the choosing is where the money sits.

Then the tricks on top. Real, and in my view this is what people actually pay for. Mashrabov says the team spoke to eight creative directors. Every one of them said camera control was what was missing. There's also an AI influencer builder. There's Genjutsu, which redraws a video in a new style but keeps the original motion, timing and camera work. And there's Ads Studio, which turns a link to your website into ads.

And finally, the bit I'd stamp theatre, the claim of no paid advertising. On the podcast, he was asked, "You don't do paid?" Mashrabov answered: "We don't do paid." Then came the follow-up. "Are influencers not paid?" With influencers, he said, there's usually a fee for making the video, and then a payment tied to clicks. I'd call that paid marketing in a nicer jacket.

A few things in the interview surprised me. Here are three.

First, its own staff spend over 4 million dollars a month on AI. That's Mashrabov's figure for close to 400 people, so more than 10,000 dollars each. One of them spent over 30,000 dollars in a single week, on one model. Even Mashrabov called that "quite a lot". I agree.

Second, the footage. For 90 minutes of TV-quality film, Mashrabov says, it took over 100 hours of AI-made footage. My take is that AI made footage cheap, but taste is still expensive.

Third, it's part tech company, part TV studio. Over 150 creative people make its launch videos and tutorials, he says. That's almost half the whole workforce. Well over 70% of its money comes from the West. Yet the largest city by usage is Seoul.

That taste point is where the old way of making film starts to change. A film used to pass down a long line of people. Mashrabov lists them. There are scriptwriters, then screenwriters. Then people who break the story down "shot by shot". Then people who do the storyboards, and a director over the lot.

His bet is that in five years, especially in his space, creative directors will be "talking to computers". They'll be making stories as video, in real time. The line gets shorter. The choosing gets harder. In the end, he says, taste will matter a lot more than just having stories to tell.

The money side is shifting too, as I see it. Video used to be paid for in days, with a crew, a camera and a set. Now it's paid for by the clip, in rented computing power. So the money follows whoever owns the customer and picks the engine. That's why the chooser matters so much.

There's an old story that fits. Netflix started out mailing DVDs. Then it began releasing its own shows, starting with House of Cards in 2013. I think Higgsfield is running the same play with engines. Sell other companies' first, then build a few of your own.

So what does all this mean for you? I've got two answers, for two kinds of people, starting with the people who watch.

If you watch or make videos, know this. Anyone can now invent a person. Higgsfield's AI influencer tool lets you describe one, right down to their age, skin, body and face. Then you make them copy a move from a trending video, or from a clip you upload. It copies the "movement, camera and timing" frame for frame. It's free to try, and it works inside ChatGPT. Honestly, the next influencer doing this week's dance in your feed may never have danced at all.

Does it make its users sharper, or more dependent? In my view, it does a bit of both, and I keep going back and forth on it. It hands anyone camera moves that used to need a film crew. But when Higgsfield picks the engine, you may not know whose AI made your video. You also may not know whose rules on faces apply, or on copyright, meaning who owns the work. If that matters to you, ask which model made each clip, and keep the answer.

If you're starting a business and you just need adverts, you no longer need a film crew. Ads Studio turns a link to your website into ads.

If you're building one of these businesses, I think the money follows whoever owns the job. The engine maker comes second. There are three moves I'd copy.

Sell the result. People come to make an advert or a music video, and few of them care which model does it.

Choose for them. A good-enough engine at a good price, picked for the customer, beats a menu with dozens of names on it.

Keep a spare engine. Back in June, Tech Times reported that Sora's service for app makers was due to close on 24 September 2026. It closed on the day. If one supplier can switch you off, line up a second before you need it.

There's one thing I'd build differently, though. Show customers which engine made each clip, and whose rules apply to it. Higgsfield picks the engine in over 40% of cases. A choice the customer can see is a choice they can trust.
