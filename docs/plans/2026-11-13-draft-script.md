Is your company running a rat farm? If it counts how much AI your staff use, it probably is. Let me explain. I've been digging into how companies measure AI at work. And if you run a team that's been told to use more AI, this one's for you. There are three parts. A rat story from 1902. The companies that ranked their staff by AI use, and then backed off. And one firm that asked for less.

So, part one. In 1902, the city of Hanoi was scared of plague, and it had a rat problem. So it paid people for every rat tail they handed in, as proof of a dead rat. What could possibly go wrong? Well, people handed in loads of tails. But then rats with no tails started running around the streets, very much alive. People were catching rats, cutting off the tails and letting them go. That way the rats could breed and make more tails. Some even opened rat farms. More rats, more tails, more money. Hanoi was counting tails, when what it actually wanted was dead rats.

Part two. Now swap the rat tails for AI use. In 2025, bosses started telling staff that using AI wasn't optional any more. Take Shopify, the online shop company. Its boss told staff to show AI couldn't do a job before asking for more people. And he said AI use would count in their performance reviews. Duolingo, the language app, did the same. So using AI became part of how people were judged at work. And once you're judging people on something, someone builds a leaderboard, a chart that ranks everyone from top to bottom.

At Meta, AI use counted in performance reviews too. And one member of staff built a leaderboard that ranked people by how much AI they used. AI use is counted in tokens, which are basically little chunks of the words the AI reads and writes. And every one of them costs money. The top spot on the chart was called Token Legend. That's a real title. Amazon had its own leaderboard too, for its AI coding tool. It was called KiroRank.

You can guess what happened next. People wanted to climb. Now, there are bits of AI called agents, which keep working on a task by themselves. So some people set agents running on pointless jobs, just to use up more AI. That's rat farming with a laptop. One engineer even joked about these leaderboards online. Every time his manager said something daft, he'd set ten AIs to work on it, just to climb the chart. Ten! It was a joke. But the leaderboards were real, and burning AI on nonsense really did climb them.

And then came the U-turns. Meta's chart came down within days of the press finding out about it. Then Meta said it would stop counting AI use in people's performance reviews. Amazon took KiroRank offline too, after one of its bosses said the AI bills were too high. And in October, Amazon told its engineers that counting AI use was a bad way to measure people.

Part three, and my favourite bit. Researchers looked at a big firm that makes medical equipment. It told its staff to ask its AI a set number of questions every month. Most people asked just enough to hit the number, and then stopped. About a third of all those questions were junk. The same question twice, or stuff that had nothing to do with work. So the firm cut the target in half, one branch at a time. That let researchers compare branches that had changed with ones that hadn't. Most of the junk vanished, and the useful stuff stayed. And sales staff in the branches that changed sold 7% more than in branches that hadn't changed yet. They asked for less AI, and they sold more. The study doesn't say exactly why, but my guess is people got their time back for real work. And think about that. The firm didn't ban AI, and it didn't drop the target altogether. It just stopped asking for so much of it. And even then, most people still asked just enough to hit the new number.

So let me sum it up. Hanoi counted tails and got tails. Companies counted AI use and got AI use. And when one firm asked for less AI, it sold more. Whatever you count, people learn to make more of it.

So I reckon it's simple. If you're counting how much AI your people use, you're counting rat tails.

If I ran a team, I'd go one step further than that firm and scrap that number. I'd watch what brings money in instead, like sales, or whatever your team is there to deliver. Those are your dead rats, the thing you actually want.
