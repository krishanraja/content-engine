Why does counting how much AI your people use make things worse? Because whatever you count, people learn to make more of it. I've been digging into how companies measure AI at work. And I want to break it down for you in three parts. A rat story from 1902. The companies that ranked their staff by AI use. And one firm that did the opposite. And if you run a team that's been told to use more AI, this one's for you.

Let's start in Hanoi, in 1902. The city had a rat problem. So it paid people for every rat tail they handed in, as proof of a dead rat. What could possibly go wrong? And people did hand in tails, loads of them. Then rats with no tails started turning up in the streets, very much alive. People were snipping off the tails and letting the rats go, to breed more tails. Some even set up rat farms. So Hanoi was counting tails, when what it actually wanted was dead rats.

Now jump to 2025. Bosses started telling staff that using AI wasn't optional any more. Shopify's boss told his people to reach for AI first, as a habit. And he said it would come up in their reviews. Duolingo said much the same. So using AI became something you got marked on at work. And once you're marking something, someone builds a leaderboard, right?

Then came 2026. At Meta, one member of staff built a leaderboard that ranked people by how much AI they used. AI use is counted in tokens, which are basically small chunks of the words the AI reads and writes. And every one of them costs money. So the more you asked the AI to do, the higher you climbed. And the board gave out titles. The top users were called things like Token Legend. Amazon had its own version for its coding tool, Kiro. It was called KiroRank.

You can guess what happened next. People wanted to climb. Now, there are bits of AI called agents, which keep working on a task by themselves. So some people set agents running on pointless busywork, just to burn more tokens. That helps no one. But it does climb the board. So the rat tails were back. Loads of AI use, and not much to show for it.

And then the boards came down. Meta's came down within days of the press finding out about it. Then Meta said it would stop using AI counts to judge people in their reviews. Amazon took KiroRank offline too, after one of its bosses said the AI bills were too high. And this autumn, Amazon told its engineers that counting AI use was deeply flawed. Go figure.

Now here's the bit I like most. Researchers looked at a big firm that makes medical equipment. It told its staff to ask its AI a set number of questions every month. And most people used it just enough to hit the target, and then stopped. About a third of all those questions were junk. The same question twice, or stuff that had nothing to do with work. So the firm cut the target in half. And people's use dropped, right down to the new target. The junk dropped away, and the real use stayed. And its sales staff sold 7% more than branches that hadn't cut it yet. They asked for less AI, and they sold more.

So now imagine ranking your own team by the work that actually got done instead. I reckon the person at the top for AI use isn't at the top any more.

There's one more twist. The most famous story about this mistake is about cobras. The British paid for dead cobras, so people bred cobras. Everyone tells it. But historians can't find good proof that it ever happened. So even the warning about counting the wrong thing got believed without anyone checking it. The rats in Hanoi, though, are in the records.

So let me sum it up. Hanoi counted tails and got tails. Companies counted AI use and got AI use. And when one firm asked for less AI, it sold more. Whatever you count, people learn to make more of it.

If you're counting how much AI your people use, you're counting rat tails.

If I ran a team, I'd go further than that firm and delete any target for AI use. And I'd check if the AI is moving a number I already track, like sales or invoices sent. Those are the dead rats.
