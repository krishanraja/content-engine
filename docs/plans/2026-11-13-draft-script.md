Is your company running a rat farm with AI? Probably, so let me explain. I've been digging into how companies measure AI at work. And if you run a team that's been told to use more AI, this one's for you. There are three parts. A rat story from 1902. The companies that ranked their staff by AI use. And one firm that did the opposite.

So, part one. In 1902, the city of Hanoi had a rat problem. So it paid people for every rat tail they handed in, as proof of a dead rat. What could possibly go wrong? Well, people handed in loads of tails. But then rats with no tails started running around the streets, very much alive. People were catching rats, cutting off the tails and letting them go, so they could breed more tails. Some even opened rat farms. More rats, more tails, more money. Hanoi was counting tails, when what it actually wanted was dead rats.

Part two. Now swap the rats for AI. In 2025, bosses started telling staff that using AI wasn't optional any more. Shopify's boss told his people to reach for AI first, and said it would come up in their reviews. Duolingo said much the same. So AI use became something you got marked on. And once you're marking something, someone builds a leaderboard, right?

At Meta, one member of staff built a leaderboard that ranked people by how much AI they used. AI use is counted in tokens, which are basically little chunks of the words the AI reads and writes. And every one of them costs money. So the top spot on the chart was called Token Legend. That's a real title. Amazon had its own version for its coding tool, Kiro. It was called KiroRank.

You can guess what happened next. People wanted to climb. Now, there are bits of AI called agents, which keep working on a task by themselves. So some people set agents running on pointless jobs, just to burn more tokens. That's rat farming with a laptop. It helps no one, but it does climb the chart.

Part three, the U-turns. Meta's chart came down within days of the press finding out about it. Then Meta said it would stop using AI counts to judge people in their reviews. Amazon took KiroRank offline too, after one of its bosses said the AI bills were too high. And this autumn, Amazon told its engineers that counting AI use was deeply flawed. Go figure.

Now, my favourite bit. Researchers looked at a big firm that makes medical equipment. It told its staff to ask its AI a set number of questions every month. And guess what? Most people asked just enough to hit the number, and then stopped. About a third of all those questions were junk. The same question twice, or stuff that had nothing to do with work. So the firm cut the target in half. Most of the junk vanished, and the useful stuff stayed. And its sales staff sold 7% more than branches that hadn't cut it yet. They asked for less AI, and they sold more.

There's one more twist. The most famous story about this mistake is about cobras. The British paid for dead cobras, so people bred cobras. Everyone tells it. But historians can't find good proof that it ever happened. So even the warning story got believed without anyone checking it. The rats in Hanoi, though, are in the records.

So let me sum it up. Hanoi counted tails and got tails. Companies counted AI use and got AI use. And when one firm asked for less AI, it sold more. Whatever you count, people learn to make more of it.

So I reckon it's simple. If you're counting how much AI your people use, you're counting rat tails.

If I ran a team, I'd scrap that number. I'd watch the ones that pay the bills instead, like sales or invoices sent. Those are the dead rats.
