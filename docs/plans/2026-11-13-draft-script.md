In 1902, Hanoi paid people to get rid of rats, and ended up with rat farms. The city was scared of plague. So it paid people for every rat tail they handed in, as proof of a dead rat. What could possibly go wrong? Well, people handed in loads of tails. But then rats with no tails started running around the streets, very much alive. People were catching rats, cutting off the tails and letting them go. That way the rats could breed and make more tails. Some even opened rat farms. More rats, more tails, more money. In the end, the city gave up and stopped paying. It had been counting tails, when what it actually wanted was dead rats.

Sounds daft, right? But if your company counts how much AI its staff use, it might be running a rat farm too.

In 2025, bosses started telling staff that using AI wasn't optional any more. Take Shopify, the online shop company. Its boss said AI use would count in staff performance reviews. Duolingo, the language app, said AI use would count in reviews too. So using AI became part of how people were judged at work. And once you're judging people on something, someone builds a leaderboard.

At Meta, the owner of Facebook, AI use counted in performance reviews too. And one member of staff built a leaderboard that ranked people by how much AI they used. AI use is counted in tokens, which are basically little chunks of the words the AI reads and writes. And every one of them costs money. The top spot on the chart was called Token Legend. That's a real title. Amazon had one too, for Kiro, its AI tool that writes code. It was called KiroRank.

You can guess what happened next. No one wants to be bottom of a chart at work. Now, there are bits of AI called agents, which keep working on a task by themselves. So some people set agents running on pointless jobs, just to use up more AI. That's rat farming with a laptop. One engineer even joked online that whenever his manager said something daft, he'd set ten agents loose on it. Not to fix anything, just to climb the chart. He was joking. But burning AI on nonsense really did climb those leaderboards.

Then the leaderboards came down. Meta's chart came down within days of the press finding out about it. Then Meta said it would stop counting AI use in people's performance reviews. Amazon took KiroRank offline too, after one of its bosses said the AI bills were too high. And last month, Amazon told its engineers that counting AI use was a bad way to measure people.

But my favourite bit is a firm that kept counting, and simply asked for less. Researchers looked at a big firm that makes medical equipment. It told its staff to ask its AI chatbot a set number of questions every month. Most people asked just enough to hit the number, and then stopped. About a third of all those questions were junk. The same question twice, or stuff that had nothing to do with work. So the firm cut the target in half, in some of its sales offices first. Most of the junk vanished, and the useful stuff stayed. And sales staff in offices with the lower target sold 7% more than in offices still on the old one. The firm asked for less AI, and its staff sold more. The study doesn't say exactly why, but my guess is people got their time back for real work. And notice, the firm didn't ban AI. It just stopped asking for so much of it.

Hanoi counted tails and got tails. Companies counted AI use and got AI use. And when one firm asked for less AI, it sold more. Whatever you count, people learn to make more of it.

So I reckon it's simple. If you're counting how much AI your people use, you're counting rat tails.

If I ran a team, I'd scrap that number, because the target is what made the junk. I'd watch what brings money in instead, like sales, or whatever your team is there to deliver. Those are your dead rats.
