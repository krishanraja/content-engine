I've been watching what's coming with AI, before everyone notices. I'm Krish.

I've been using AI apps every day for years now. And I've noticed something odd about how they're sold. Every launch is about a name. A new number, a livestream, a chart. Claude Opus 4.8. Then Opus 5. Then Opus 5.5. At this rate, when's Opus 5.56 coming out? So I wanted to break it down. Does the name you pick actually change the answer you get?

First, some of the new AIs really are better. The people building them aren't lying to you. But I think the name is the least important thing on your screen.

There's a setting that changes your answer much more. It decides how hard the AI thinks before it answers. The app usually starts it low, and almost nobody changes it. There are a few reasons the AI companies would rather you look at the name. Let's unpack them.

And I think I can see where this ends up. The AI itself will decide how hard it thinks. That's good news and bad news.

First, the names. Anthropic makes Claude. It launched Opus 4.8 in May, Opus 5 in July, and Opus 5.5 in September. OpenAI makes ChatGPT. It went from GPT 5.1 last November to GPT 6.1 by the end of September.

Why so many? The companies don't say it's to get in the news. So this next bit is my own opinion. Look at what a launch gets them.

In March 2025, OpenAI added picture making to ChatGPT. Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, then said a million new people joined in one hour. That same day, OpenAI said investors were giving it forty billion dollars. That money had been planned for months. But launching on the same day made OpenAI look even more exciting.

Here's a detail I love. The page for Opus 5.5 has a small note at the bottom. It says the scores in its charts come from the AI thinking as hard as it possibly can. But for people building apps, Anthropic starts Opus 5.5 at medium thinking. So the chart shows it trying its hardest. You get the middle, unless you ask for more.

Here's my favourite example. In September, Anthropic's boss, Dario Amodei, wrote that AI companies should slow down how fast AI gets smarter. In the next few weeks, Anthropic brought out three new Claude names. OpenAI brought out two. To be fair, he was talking about making AI smarter. And that's my point. You can get a new name without getting a smarter AI.

So what does matter? Let me explain with one person.

Picture a woman called Sally. She runs a small shop. She's about to sign a deal with a company that supplies the things she sells. She pastes the deal into her AI app and asks what she should worry about. In four seconds, it gives her a tidy answer. It sounds sure. She signs.

Six months later, she finds one small rule hidden in the deal. It costs her a lot of money. A careful reader would have spotted it.

Nothing was wrong with the name on her screen. The AI just never stopped to think.

ChatGPT and Claude both let you choose how hard the AI thinks. On low, it answers straight away. On high, it works through the problem and checks itself first.

Think of a bike. The name is the sticker on the frame. The thinking setting is the gear. In an easy gear, it barely tries. In a hard gear, it really works. We argue about stickers. And most of us ride everywhere in the easiest gear.

How big is the difference? A group called Artificial Analysis tests these AIs. They ran the very same Claude, Opus 5.5, in different gears. In an easy gear, it scored 42 on their test. In its hardest gear, 58. The older Opus 5, trying its hardest, only got 51. So changing gear helped more than getting the newer version.

But more thinking isn't always better. Each extra bit helps a little less than the last. And sometimes it's a total waste. Researchers asked thinking AIs a really simple question. What is two plus three? They used about twenty times more words than normal AIs to get there. One of them worked out the answer thirteen times.

So the gear matters most on hard jobs, like Sally's deal. On easy ones, extra thinking is wasted money. Whose money?

When an AI thinks, it writes out its working behind the scenes. Every word of that uses computer time, and computer time costs money. For Opus 5.5, thinking as hard as it can cost about eleven times as much as thinking a little.

Who pays for that thinking depends on how you pay.

Some people pay by the word. Usually that's businesses building apps. For them, more thinking means a bigger bill. So when businesses turn up the thinking, OpenAI and Anthropic earn more.

But most of us pay the same amount every month, however much we use. Then every extra thought costs OpenAI or Anthropic, and we pay nothing extra. In January 2025, Altman said OpenAI was losing money on its most expensive plan. It cost two hundred dollars a month, and people used it far more than expected.

Anthropic had the same problem. One person on a two hundred dollar plan used tens of thousands of dollars' worth of AI. So Anthropic added weekly limits.

Picture a café where some customers pay one price and eat as much as they like. They pay one in every ten coins the café takes. But they eat more than four in every ten plates. A research firm called SemiAnalysis says Anthropic's monthly plans work a bit like that.

I wanted to know how much they make on one hard-thinking answer, compared with a quick one. Neither company tells us. I looked.

Across the whole company, it looks better. If you pay OpenAI a dollar, about seventy cents is left after it pays for the computers. That's what news reports say.

But on a monthly plan, the hard-thinking answer is the expensive one. That, I think, is why the app starts you in an easy gear. That's my opinion. Nobody has said it out loud.

So who decides how hard your AI thinks? The AI already decides more than you'd guess. Even inside one gear, it chooses how much to think. OpenAI's own instructions say its AIs think less on simple jobs, and harder on tricky ones.

The next step is the AI choosing the gear for you. Google has just started doing that. Since the ninth of October, the free Gemini app starts on a setting called Auto. Google says most questions go to its fastest brain. Harder ones may go to a deeper one.

OpenAI has done the opposite. Twice. Last December, it turned off its automatic gear picker for free users. A magazine called WIRED said the picker was sending lots more free users to the slower, thinking answers. And those cost OpenAI more. Then in September, OpenAI stopped doing it for paying users too. It didn't say why. Now, if you pay, you pick the gear.

I don't think that lasts. Most of us would rather the AI picked the gear for us. So would the companies, to save money.

So here's my bet. By the end of June 2027, ChatGPT will let the AI pick the gear for people who pay. They won't have to switch anything on. I'd be wrong if, on that date, you still have to pick the gear yourself.

Is that good? Some of both. The good first. People don't change settings. Back in 2025, ChatGPT tried picking the gear for people. Far more of them ended up with careful, thinking answers. Before, fewer than one free user in a hundred used thinking each day. After, seven in a hundred did.

Anthropic learned this too. In March, it moved its coding app down to the middle gear. Users said it felt less smart, but most never changed it back. Anthropic said that was a mistake, and changed it back. So whoever picks the starting gear decides how hard most people's AI thinks. An AI that picks for each question would at least think hard when it matters.

Now the bad. You can't see it. In January, OpenAI made ChatGPT think for less time, because people like faster answers. By mistake, it also cut the longest thinking setting. It took weeks to fix. Nobody looking at their screen would have known.

And whatever does the deciding has two jobs. Give you a good answer, and keep OpenAI's costs down. Those don't always agree. When OpenAI's chooser broke, right after a launch, Altman said the AI seemed way dumber.

So the big question is this. When the AI picks your gear, is it thinking about you, or about the bill? And can you see what it picked?

So what should you actually do?

For quick, everyday stuff, an easy gear is fine.

For anything you'll act on, change the gear yourself. A deal you're signing. A health question. Money. A choice you can't take back. On ChatGPT, pick Medium or High. On Claude, turn the effort up. On Gemini, set the effort to high. It takes a few more seconds, and uses up your limit sooner. For the questions that matter, it's worth it.

If you build things with AI, test your hardest questions in each gear. And check what the thinking costs you.

There's a better way. Let the AI pick the gear, if it must. But show it. A small label under every answer saying how hard it thought. Then you'd know when to ask again.

We've spent two years learning the stickers. By the time we learn the gears, the bike will be changing them for us. I'd just like to see which gear it picked.
