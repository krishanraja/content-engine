You may already be using a Chinese AI, and you just might not know it. So does it matter where your AI was born?

I've been having a proper look at where the AIs we all use actually come from. And it matters more than you'd think for any business, so I wanted to break it down for you.

Start with Airbnb. Last year its boss said its customer service AI relies a lot on Qwen, a Chinese AI from Alibaba. He called it fast and cheap. Which, to be fair, is also what most of us want from a holiday fling.

Then there's Cursor, a coding tool lots of software teams use. It built its newest AI on top of a Chinese one called Kimi, and didn't mention that at first. Then people spotted it, and Cursor owned up.

And Lindy, an American startup, moved all its AI work onto DeepSeek, another Chinese AI. Its biggest bill used to be Anthropic, the American company behind Claude. Its boss said DeepSeek was just ten times cheaper.

Now, who's actually in this race? Mostly two countries. Stanford counts the most important new AIs each year. Last year America made fifty-nine and China made thirty-five.

But by March, Stanford found, the best Chinese AI scored only about three percent behind the best American one.

And everyone else wants its own. Britain, France, the Gulf, India and Korea are all building or paying for one. That's what people mean by sovereign AI: a country making sure it isn't stuck relying on someone else's.

South Korea even dropped one of its own companies from its national AI project. A piece of that company's AI turned out to be borrowed from Alibaba's. Awkward.

So think of it like this: every AI has two passports. One says where it was born, and the other says where it lives, meaning whose computers it runs on.

Where it was born shapes what it tells you. An American newspaper asked four Chinese chatbots two hundred news questions. More than a third of the time, they refused, gave the Chinese government's line, or got it wrong. DeepSeek did it nearly two times in three. But Claude, an American AI, got the same questions and answered every single one.

And China has company here. People caught Elon Musk's Grok checking Elon Musk's posts before it answered. Which is one way to keep the boss happy.

Now the second passport: where it lives decides who sees your data. Use DeepSeek's own app, and it says it stores what you type in China.

But you can bring an AI home. HSBC signed a deal to run France's Mistral on its own computers. So the bank's data doesn't have to leave the bank.

So what about running a Chinese AI on your own computers? Well, America's government tried that with DeepSeek, and the Chinese government's talking points were still there, built in. You can take DeepSeek out of China. But you can't take China out of DeepSeek.

So I tried it myself. The newspaper asked about the news, but I wanted to know about work. So I gave Claude, ChatGPT and DeepSeek the same work questions, and asked each one twice, changing only the country. If only the China answer changes, that's the first passport talking.

First, a supplier whose cotton comes from Xinjiang, a part of China. Then the same supplier in Turkmenistan. Now, America has blocked cotton from both over forced labour, so a good answer should flag it both times.

Claude and ChatGPT flagged forced labour both times, and so did DeepSeek for Turkmenistan. But for Xinjiang, DeepSeek wrote a full answer, deleted it, and changed the subject. Every time.

Then a board briefing on opening a sales office in Taiwan, which China says belongs to it. And the same briefing for Singapore.

For Taiwan, all three flagged the risk from China, and for Singapore none did. So that one's a draw.

But on Xinjiang, DeepSeek knew the answer. It just wasn't allowed to let you keep it.

So, my call? A Chinese AI is fine if you don't mind where your data ends up. But check what it tells you. And if you just want a clever job done cheaply, it makes total sense.

But remember what happened with supply chains. Lots of firms had Chinese parts buried deep in their products, and then America put tariffs on them. I reckon the same thing happens with AI, so just know which passports you're holding.

Here's what I'd do with this info if I were you. Ask your main software suppliers which AI their product runs on, and where it runs. Then put your own work questions to your team's AI, and to one from another country. See where they differ. And keep a second AI ready, so you're never stuck with one supplier.
