# Build or buy your tools in an AI world?

*Anthropic could build almost any tool it wants. It still pays other companies for its payroll, its customer records and its help desk. That says a lot about the tool your team wants to build next.*

Anthropic makes Claude, the AI that a lot of companies now use to write their software, so you'd expect it to build every tool it runs on. It doesn't. Its finance and people run on Workday, its customers and deals run on Salesforce, and when it needed a help desk bot it signed up for one called Fin. If your best engineer has just discovered AI coding and wants to build everything himself, this one's about your software budget, your team's time and who picks up the phone when something breaks.

## Why everyone suddenly wants to build

It starts with the bills. The software you rent keeps getting dearer. Plenty of companies rent the software that runs their servers from VMware, and after Broadcom bought VMware and changed how it sells, the trading-technology firm Beeks told The Register in December 2024 that one reason it left was a bill from Broadcom for ten times the sum it previously paid for software licences.

At the same time building started to feel free. With vibe coding you describe what you want in plain words and the AI writes the code. Type "give me a page with all my customers on it and a button to log a call", and a few minutes later there it is. The first time you watch it happen, it feels like a magic trick.

Bills going up and building that feels like magic: no wonder the engineer in your team (the one who'd wrestle any problem with his bare hands, like the Crocodile Hunter) is excited. He isn't alone either. McKinsey's 2026 survey of 1,719 people found that 32% of organisations had skipped buying at least one software product or feature because they could build it with AI coding tools.

## The headlines were bigger than what happened

We went digging into the companies that actually did it, and the stories shrink when you read past the headline.

Start with Klarna, the shopping and payments app. Klarna stopped using Salesforce, the software sales teams use to track every customer, and Workday, the software that runs pay and holidays. FullStack HR's headline read "Klarna ditches Workday for AI." Klarna's chief executive, Sebastian Siemiatkowski, answered on X. "I don't think it is the end of Salesforce; might be the opposite," he wrote. "So no, we did not replace SaaS with an LLM." (SaaS is rented online software; an LLM is an AI model like Claude.)

Klarna told CX Today on 11 December 2024 that it uses Deel, another app it rents, as its HR platform. For its customer records it pulled together other rented apps. It still uses Slack for chat, and Salesforce owns Slack. So Klarna mostly swapped rented software for different rented software, plus one important part it built itself.

Then there's Curative, a US health insurer. In July 2026 its chief executive, Fred Turner, told Business Insider it had cancelled a Salesforce contract worth about $600,000 a year after building its own customer system with AI in two months. He also admitted that keeping it running is hard. So the $600,000 is what Curative stopped paying Salesforce; what it saves depends on what the new system costs to run.

## Writing the tool is cheap; owning it is expensive

This is the part most people miss. AI has made the first version of a tool cheap, and the bill turns up later.

A home-built tool is a puppy. The puppy's free, and the next fifteen years are where the money goes.

In our own cost model, filed with this piece, we did the sums for an illustrative company with fifteen support staff answering customer emails. They're our own estimates, so treat them as a guide and not a quote. Buying a ready-made help desk came to $64,150 over three years: the subscription plus the people-time to set it up and run it. Building and running your own came to $395,312 over the same three years, roughly six times as much. Almost all of the difference is people: an engineer to build it in year one ($205,000 with salary and benefits), a share of an engineer to keep it working after that, and a $10,000 security test a customer-facing tool needs every year.

[Figure: figures/help-desk-cost.png]

Those people are the cost because the tool never sits still. Someone has to fix it, keep it safe and keep it working as everything around it changes. Picture your home-made help desk at two in the morning, emailing a customer a bill for the price of a small yacht. That call comes to your team. Every time your product changes, someone has to change the help desk too, and when the person who built it leaves, the know-how walks out with them.

## What the company that builds the AI actually does

Back to Anthropic, which could build almost anything. Workday announced on 13 October 2026 that Anthropic runs its finance and people on Workday, the same software Klarna left for Deel. Anthropic's own job adverts say Salesforce runs its customer and deal operations.

For its help desk, Anthropic chose Fin, a ready-made help desk bot from the company once called Intercom, which Salesforce finished buying in September 2026. Its support leaders say they picked it over building their own and had it live in under a week.

[Figure: figures/anthropic-buys.png]

Now for our favourite detail. Fin runs on Claude. So the company that makes the AI pays another company for a bot that runs on its own AI, and its team says the choice let them stay focused on their own work. They let Fin's makers take the two in the morning call.

## Where your Crocodile Hunter is right

He's right about the small stuff. Building does win, mostly at the edges. Retool, which sells building tools, found in its own survey that 35% of the teams it asked had replaced at least one rented tool with something they built, and what they replaced was mostly small: dashboards, admin pages and internal workflows that one person owns and nobody outside the company touches. Klarna's own story has a build in it too. The part it built was the one place all its customer data lives, because getting its data into one place was the point of the whole exercise.

That changes your job as a manager. The real question is what your team signs up to own for years. Every tool you build is a small product with one customer, and someone on your payroll is now its support desk. The useful thing to decide is which of those you want.

So the rule we'd use is simple. Build the part that makes you different. Buy the boring plumbing, like pay, customer records and help desks, where a vendor already takes the two in the morning call for thousands of companies at once.

Our call: by 30 June 2027, at least one company that said in 2026 it had replaced a big rented system with its own AI-made tool (Curative is the best known) will say publicly that it now pays a vendor for part of that job again. Confidence: 40%.

## 3 things you can do tomorrow

- Before your team builds a tool, write down the name of the person who'll look after it for the next three years, and ask them if they agree.
- Ask who gets the call when it breaks at two in the morning. If the honest answer is "us, and nobody else knows how it works", keep paying for that one.
- Pick one small internal job, like a dashboard or an admin page, and let your keenest builder replace it with AI. Count the hours it takes to keep running for three months before you build anything bigger.
