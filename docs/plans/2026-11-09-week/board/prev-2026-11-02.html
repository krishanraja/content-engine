Article

1,094 words

Anthropic, the company behind Claude, has just published a study of its own staff, and one line stopped me. Since early 2025, the share of its new technical hires who are juniors has fallen from 27% to 9%.

That's roughly one new technical hire in four becoming fewer than one in ten. And it comes from the company that knows better than anyone what its own tools can do.

So, one question. If AI takes the work juniors used to learn on, who trains the seniors you'll need in 2030?

THE BOTTOM RUNG IS GOING

"How Employment is Changing at Anthropic" came out on 16 October, and it's unusually frank (it's not every day a company publishes the bit that makes it look awkward). Juniors' share of its technical teams slipped from 21% to 14% over the same stretch. Postings for junior roles "have fallen particularly sharply since December 2025". By April, its engineering and research teams had cut back on hiring for junior technical roles, and around a third of its machine learning managers said they were hiring less because of AI.

Why? The answer sits in the same report. By August, the hours its AI agents worked outnumbered its employees' hours six to one. When the agents write that much of the first draft, the first-draft job gets thin.

Outside Anthropic, the picture looks much the same. Seyed Hosseini Maasoum and Guy Lichtinger studied US CVs and job postings, and found that from early 2023, junior employment at firms that adopted generative AI "declined sharply relative to non-adopters, while senior employment continued to rise". The drop came mainly from slower hiring. Fewer juniors were taken on in the first place.

WE'VE BEEN HERE BEFORE

Before anyone reaches for the panic button, it's worth remembering that machines have eaten the bottom of a job before. Twice, quite famously.

You'd expect cash machines to have finished off bank tellers. James Bessen looked at what actually happened, in a piece for the IMF. Between 1988 and 2004, the number of tellers an average city branch needed fell from 20 to 13. But banks spent the savings on more branches, 43% more in cities, and the number of teller jobs held up. Tellers spent less time counting cash and more time talking to customers.

Then came spreadsheets. Since 1980, as NPR's Planet Money put it, "400,000 bookkeeping and accounting clerk jobs have gone away. But 600,000 accounting jobs have been added." Planet Money's explanation is lovely in its simplicity: accounting got cheaper, and "when something gets cheaper, people buy a lot more of that thing."

My read of both: each tool took the dull bottom of a job, the work got cheaper, people wanted more of it, and the job came back bigger.

WHAT'S DIFFERENT THIS TIME

Here's the bit that worries me. In both of those stories, the people at the bottom still had somewhere to learn. A teller who stopped counting notes spent more time with customers. As clerks' jobs went, accountants' jobs grew.

So is this just another cash-machine moment? I don't think so, and Anthropic's own report hints at why. It says that in some roles, its staff are "increasingly removed from the object-level work that originally built their skills". In plain English, the hands-on work that taught people the job is the work AI now does first. My read is that juniors feel that most, because that work was their whole training.

And the cut is quiet. Nobody gets a dramatic letter. Anthropic's researchers put it bluntly: "Employment protections for incumbents will not benefit those not hired to begin with." It hits the people who never get the job, so they never show up in anyone's numbers.

WHO TRAINS YOUR 2030 SENIORS

Hiring managers, the same report notes, "increasingly emphasize applicants' autonomy and judgment relative to specific technical expertise". That makes sense. If AI writes the first draft, you need people who can tell a good draft from a bad one.

But judgement is exactly what people used to pick up in their junior years, by doing the dull work, getting it wrong and being put right. Take away the junior years and you still need the judgement. You just have nowhere left to grow it.

That's why this is every business's problem, and Anthropic's staff are only early to it. They expect AI to be able to do 75% of their own work by 2030. Whatever the right number is for your business, the seniors you'll need in 2030 are juniors somewhere today. If nobody hires them, nobody trains them.

MY FIRST RUNG

Here's why this one matters to me. In 2008 I was a junior at Microsoft. Yes, the people who make Excel. I was asked to reformat a spreadsheet with half a million rows, by hand. Same clicks, over and over. I had a hunch it wasn't meant to be done that way, so I looked online and found Excel macros, which record a simple action once so you can repeat it. Building one took me a few days, and I knew I might be throwing those days away. Then three weeks of work became a few hours.

Building the macro was the easy part. What I actually learned was spotting the boring job that repeats, and sticking with the fix when the first version breaks, rather than blaming the technology. I only learned that because someone gave me the dull job.

So, a question to take into your next hiring meeting. Before you cut a junior role, ask who on this team will be a senior in 2030, and what they're learning on today.

And if you're early in your career, ask in your next interview what real work you'll learn on. When you find a boring job that repeats, learn how it works before you hand it to AI, then build the fix.

3 things you can do tomorrow

  1. Name your 2030 seniors. For each team, write down who you expect to be senior in four years' time, and the real work they're learning on now.
  2. Look at your last three hiring decisions. If AI was the reason a junior role went unfilled, write down who now does the learning that role used to do.
  3. Give one junior one dull job to own. Pick a task that repeats every week. Have them do it by hand first, then build the fix with AI, so they learn how the work works before the tool does it for them.

Article

1,417 words

Claude Duet comes to you before you go to it.

Anthropic opened it on 21 October as a research preview, off a waitlist, for Pro and Max users in the US. Its own page says it "comes to you with what needs your attention, completes tasks you ask of it, keeps working even when you step away, and checks with you before it acts on your behalf." The reviews so far tell you whether it's any good. Most of them skip the question that matters if you already run half your working week through Claude.

So, one question. What does Claude Duet actually change for someone who already works in Claude?

WHAT DUET DOES ON ITS OWN

Duet runs on its own computer in the cloud, with its own browser, so your laptop can be shut. Every few hours it checks the apps you have connected: Gmail, Slack, your calendar, HubSpot, or any of the "over 6,000 connectors and plugins" Anthropic counts. Each morning it sends you a short brief on what matters. In between, in Anthropic's words, it "reads, drafts, and prepares work on its own".

Then it stops. Before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document or deletes anything, it shows you exactly what it's about to do and waits for your yes. A few things stay off the table even with your yes, such as trading securities or moving money between accounts.

For a Claude user, the interesting part is what comes along for the ride. Anthropic says Duet "carries over your memory, skills, and connected apps from your Claude account", and it can search your past chats so you don't have to repeat yourself. That means no awkward first date.

WHAT PEOPLE THINK OF DUET

In its first week, the mood is curious and mostly warm about the idea and the design, and sceptical about cost, speed and access. Most people talking about it haven't got in yet. On Reddit, getting in is the single biggest topic: the waitlist, the US-only rule, and one plaintive "You know, us Teams users need love, too :)".

The people who have used it agree it's polished. Very polished. Every's review calls it "the first personal agent I'd call a pro tool". Claire Vo of How I AI put it to work on an inbox, Slack, a sales pipeline and a sales deck, and the episode on Lenny's Newsletter is called "Claude Duet is beautiful. I still forgot to use it". Vo's write-up found it slow: "The work behind the interface was the slow part."

The sharpest worry is your plan limits. Duet's background checks come out of the same allowance as the rest of your Claude work, and Anthropic says so. One Reddit user reports it used half their usage limit in a single morning, while they weren't using Claude at all. That's one report and nobody has checked it. Still, if you already hit your limit on heavy days, you'll feel it.

FROM ASKING TO APPROVING

Here's what neither the launch page nor the reviews say outright. My read: for someone who already lives in Claude, Duet adds few new abilities. What it changes is who starts the work.

Today you open Claude, paste in the email thread, the calendar invite and the notes from your sales system, and ask. With Duet, the work turns up first: a brief in the morning, a running list of "what it's working on and what it needs from me", as Every's review puts it, and drafts waiting for a yes or a no. Your job stops being the asking. It becomes checking its work and saying yes, no or not today (which, if you manage people, will sound familiar).

A small-business owner on Reddit, u/freakyslug, put it best: "I'm not doing anything that I don't think a normal version of Claude could do. I just hadn't thought to use it this way." They also say they had been falling behind, and caught up in a day and a half.

So what's the catch? Habits. A habit only forms around something that keeps coming back. Vo's test is the warning: after the first few tasks, nothing "would pull me back in". My read: Duet earns its place only when you give it a job that comes round every week. One job. Every week.

WHAT COMES WITH YOU AND WHAT STAYS BEHIND

Power users have built a lot around Claude, and not all of it makes the trip.

What comes: your memory, the skills and connectors on your account, and the apps you connect. The sales pipeline in Vo's test is the good example. Duet already had Slack, email and the CRM, so there was no need "to assemble and paste all the inputs myself".

What stays behind: files on your computer, and some of your Claude projects. Duet can't reach either. Some skills that lived on Vo's own computer didn't come across. Every's reviewer found that its scheduled runs can't reach custom connectors, and that it can see the Claude sessions on the reviewer's computers but can't direct them. Brian Govanlu asked it to work with the Claude Code sessions on a laptop; it couldn't, so it filed a feature request with Anthropic instead, which is at least very polite. Microsoft 365 isn't connected yet, and Slack has limits.

And if your company pays for Claude Team or Enterprise, you don't get it yet. Anthropic says it plans to bring Duet to those plans later.

WHERE TO HOOK IT IN FIRST

So start with one weekly job whose inputs already live in the apps Duet can reach, and that you now do by copying and pasting into Claude. Three good places to start:

  • Prep for a client meeting: the calendar invite, the last email thread and the proposal in Google Docs, pulled into one brief with what you need to settle before the call.
  • A weekly pipeline review: your CRM, inbox and Slack, turned into a short note on which deals moved and which went quiet.
  • Your follow-ups: the threads waiting on you, drafted and lined up for your yes.

For the first week, let it draft and check every draft before you say yes. Run it beside the way you do it now, and compare what it caught with what it missed. Then look at your usage at the end of the week, because the same allowance pays for everything else you do in Claude. On a Team or Enterprise account, this is the job to have ready for when it arrives.

THE JOB I'D HAND IT FIRST

If you're wondering what I'd hand it first, it's the dullest job I have: keeping my documents, my Google Drive and the notes that explain how my systems work up to date. My other AI tools read those notes before they help me, so when they go stale, every tool works from an old picture of what's going on. And I only fix that when I remember to. So every week I'd ask Duet to update them from my email and calendar, and show me the changes before it saves them. Every's review says it can edit docs and "keep them current".
