The First AI-Animated Feature Film In Theaters: ‘Gods Don’t Give Gifts’ Director Zack London Talks About The Future of Cinema

By

 Christopher Moonlight
 | November 5, 2026 

 Share

On December 4th, an independent artist named Zack London, who is more widely known across the web by his handle, Gossip Goblin, is set to walk straight into global movie theaters and break new ground with his debut AI-animated feature, Gods Don’t Give Gifts.

Partnering with AGC Studios for international theatrical distribution and self-distributing domestically through a passionate, direct-to-audience grassroots network, London has pulled off what the legacy studio system insisted was impossible: mounting an idiosyncratic, deeply personal sci-fi vision for the big screen without waiting for Hollywood’s permission.

To understand why London’s achievement is such an existential threat to the online status quo, you have to look at the approved script dominating the cultural discourse around generative technology in cinema. The prevailing narrative across timeline mobs and trade comment sections insists that anyone utilizing AI tools in a creative capacity is an unskilled charlatan looking to destroy the craft of filmmaking, the soul of art, and stand, in director Guillermo del Toro’s words, as an “insult to humanity.”

Yet if you examine where that fury actually lands, a revealing hypocrisy emerges. The timeline puritans rarely picket the glass spires of Burbank or Silicon Valley conglomerates signing multi-million-dollar tech licensing deals. Instead, their vitriol is almost exclusively aimed downward, at independent animators, garage tinkerers, and low-budget filmmakers daring to build outside legacy control.

Within the more extreme corners of legacy guild culture, a bitter protectionism has taken root: the reactionary belief that unless an idea can command a $150 million budget with full institutional scale, it has no moral right to exist at all. It is an ethos that views filmmaking not as a universal artistic impulse, but as an exclusive guild concession. If you cannot afford their tollbooth, they would rather your story remain unborn.

London, however, is ignoring that tollbooth and dismantling the very premise it stands on.

The Traditional Hand Behind the Machine

The easiest way to dismiss London’s achievement is to reduce him to the online stereotype of an opportunistic “prompt bro.” But London’s roots are firmly planted in traditional physical craft. Long before he ever touched a neural network, he spent over a decade working as an illustrator and sculptor:

“I did super meticulous micron pen drawings. The fidelity is something I don’t think AI can capture even yet… I spent like a decade cultivating a fanbase online by just posting my illustrations and writing short stories to accompany them. I would self-publish books of short stories and illustrations. I was already doing a lot of storytelling without any real end goal in mind.”

When early text-to-image and diffusion video models began trickling into public view three and a half years ago, London didn’t see a shortcut to displace work; he saw an untamed creative canvas:

“At first, the tools weren’t that good… but I was like, okay, well, I’m not going to try and recreate the stuff I’m already doing. That doesn’t feel exciting, and I don’t want to replace what I was doing. But this allows me to explore all this new kind of stuff… To me, it was just a really fun outlet. I didn’t have any real big goal in mind like, ‘I’m going to make a movie studio and take over the world.’ It was just super fun.”

As London began developing narrative shorts, he encountered the predictable online backlash demanding he spend money he didn’t have on traditional pipelines:

“I remember seeing comments like, ‘You’re so lazy. Why don’t you just hire an animator to do this?’ Because this is not a commercial. I don’t want to spend money that I don’t have to take a two-minute Instagram story and spend three months making it. This is a creative exercise where I can come up with the story on the spot and spend half a day bringing it to fruition in the way that I pictured it in my head.”

The Viability Gap and the Human Crew

The foundational lie of the anti-AI campaign is that these tools eliminate human jobs. In independent cinema, the exact opposite is true. Uncompromising, bizarre, world-heavy scripts cannot clear the focus groups of modern corporate studios. The options are not “hire 500 union animators or use AI.” The options are: make it with a hybrid pipeline, or let the project die in a desk drawer.

As London explains:

“Like you said, this film wouldn’t exist otherwise. It’s not that this film is getting made in lieu of a film that would have employed ten times the number of people. This film just wouldn’t exist. It wouldn’t get greenlit. It’s too idiosyncratic and weird. It doesn’t meet the benchmarks to get the $150 million greenlight that would be necessary to execute it the normal way. And now we get to make it on my terms without having to answer to committees or executives or budgetary constraints.”

Far from being a sterile, solo software execution, mounting Gods Don’t Give Gifts as a theatrical feature required assembling a dedicated team of flesh-and-blood creators. London brought on a co-writer, contracted a post-production crew of traditional editors and colorists, and cast a dedicated ensemble of professional voice actors.

Just a week before our conversation, London’s entire sound team flew to Stockholm, locking themselves in a cinema soundstage for four days to hand-craft an intricate, physical auditory landscape for theatrical DCP projection:

“The sound team came to Stockholm, and we spent four days in a sound studio finalizing everything for DCP and theatrical… Our Foley artist has created so many insane sounds for little creatures that we throw in the world… Someone described my work in a critical way: ‘You need to take the greeble level down by ten,’ because everything is so hyper-maximalist and insane. And what I love too is that our sound guys really lean into that. There’s just so much texture and richness to the world. It’s such a different experience seeing this in theaters when you’re immersed in the soundscape, and the visuals are blown up.”

The Post-Studio Playbook: Following the Trailblazers

London did not build his distribution path in a vacuum. The blueprint for independent creator-led theatrical distribution has been rapidly evolving, sparked by creators who realized they didn’t need legacy gatekeepers to put people into theater seats.

A major inflection point came when London connected with producer Edward Saatchi, an Emmy-winning virtual reality pioneer who previously co-founded Oculus Story Studio and Fable. Saatchi immediately recognized that London wasn’t just generating novel internet clips; he was sitting on an expansive, auteur-driven transhumanist universe titled The Second Cycle of Humanity:

“Ed really persuaded me that what you’re sitting on here is really, really good. And it is art… And the team had been working crazy hours, and we’d been doing so many unnecessary steps with Foley artists and musicians and voice actors and just a level of detail that was so extreme for something to just end up on YouTube… He gave me that little pep talk: ‘No, let’s make it happen. Let’s put this on a big screen because this deserves it.’”

During that formative period, London and Saatchi watched Markiplier’s Iron Lung, the self-financed, independently produced adaptation of David Szymanski’s indie horror game. Mark Fischbach (Markiplier) took full sovereignty over his own production, wrote, directed, financed, and worked directly with theater chains and alternative platforms to bring a claustrophobic, uncompromising horror chamber piece directly to ticket-buying audiences without studio interference.

Seeing that direct line from independent creator to the cinema screen proved that the gatekeepers no longer owned the projectors:

“We hung out and watched Iron Lung, which is kind of foreshadowing in a way, because now we’re undergoing kind of a similar bootstrapping self-distribution, ‘let’s do it ourselves’ kind of thing without studio involvement.”

To drive that release, London has activated an ambassador program and street team directly through his community at godsdontgivegifts.com, turning his audience into active partners in the rollout.

Redefining Success on Indie Terms

The fatal mistake modern creators make is accepting Hollywood’s metrics for success. A studio that spends $200 million on production and another $100 million on marketing needs to conquer the global box office just to stave off bankruptcy. An independent production built on sweat equity, precision software, and a tight human crew plays by an entirely different mathematical law:

“In our case, the success metric for the film is: we had an absurdly small budget to make this. So unfortunately for the haters, the criteria to break even is woefully low. So, sorry, but by that metric, it should be okay.”

For London, the real victory isn’t pleasing the internet’s permanent outrage brigade, nor is it winning the approval of corporate legacy institutions. It is proving that personal, weird, and visually arresting storytelling can cut through the noise, stand on its own two feet, and belong to the people who made it:

“My metric for success is really: does this have some actual cultural impact? Does this enter the zeitgeist as more than just a flicker of ‘here’s some novel experiment with AI’? Because I’m not doing this to be contrarian or controversial or to make some statements. I’m not out there trying to evangelize AI or tear down Hollywood. We wanted to tell the story at scale, and that’s what we’re doing. And hopefully the story, the characters, or insane parts of the script are what become resonant and lasting, and less so like, ‘Oh, this is just some elaborate tech demo we forget about the next day.’”

When Gods Don’t Give Gifts lights up cinema screens on December 4th, it won’t just mark a milestone for Gossip Goblin. It will signal to every independent creator with a keyboard, a microphone, and an uncompromised vision that the studio walls have cracked. You don’t have to wait for an institution to validate you, you don’t have to water down your vision to satisfy a focus group, and you certainly don’t need a hundred million dollars to take the big screen.

The tools are here. The projectors are waiting. All that remains is the courage to build.

Check out the Gods Don’t Give Gifts Official Trailer to see how Zack London is bringing his independent, high-concept world to cinema screens this December.
