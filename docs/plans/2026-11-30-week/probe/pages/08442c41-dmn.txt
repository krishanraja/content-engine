TikTok Rolls Out a Manual AI Detection Tool for Users, Officially Launches ‘Likeness Detection’ for Global Creators

 Dylan Smith

November 23, 2026

Photo Credit: Zulfugar Karimov

TikTok has officially launched two new AI detection tools, one of which is designed to help creators find and remove videos “that may use their likeness without their consent.” 

The short-form mainstay just recently rolled out the fresh AI identification features, which arrive amid seemingly intensifying pushback against machine-generated slop. In the music world, over 80% of U.S. fans reportedly want an AI audio “kill switch” on DSPs, for instance.

Meanwhile, AI (and especially deepfake) concerns on the visual media side aren’t a secret; TikTok says it’s labeled a staggering five billion or so artificial intelligence uploads to date.

Evidently, though, the app’s AI response is evolving. Enter the first of the initially noted tools, described as enabling “anyone” to determine whether content “was made or edited with TikTok’s AI creation features.”
This content includes videos, images, and audio alike, which individuals, whether signed up for TikTok or not, can upload and check. Of course, the tool aims to detect AI from across the TikTok/ByteDance product suite – referring to the video-sharing giant itself as well as CapCut, Dreamina, Hypic, and Pippit.

Regarding the specific process at hand, the feature is said to zero in on “Content Credentials” metadata and “invisible watermarks” – meaning the physical watermarks needn’t be present – en route to identifying the use of the relevant models.

As for the second and more interesting of TikTok’s new tools, the platform is making “Likeness Detection” globally available to eligible creators and public officials. 
The offering, framed as building on an extensive collection of existing deepfake-reporting and -takedown options, appears straightforward enough. Creators can “sign up by verifying their ID with a trusted third-party partner” and will then be notified of potentially unauthorized likeness usages.

(During Likeness Detection’s stateside test, TikTok confirmed Jumio as that partner, we reported over the summer. Headquartered in Sunnyvale, the company is said to count as clients Uber and Airbnb, to name a couple.)

Because it wasn’t too long ago that Rihanna and Taylor Swift deepfake scams were making headlines, it’ll be worth tracking Likeness Detection’s impact moving forward. Furthermore, as demonstrated by the episodes, brazen schemes involving the likenesses of billionaire celebrities sometimes slip by the appropriate teams and find victims.

And if impressionable fans can be convinced that these high-profile figures are urging them to sign up for fake rewards programs, it stands to reason that certain content creators’ followers, already quite accustomed to seeing sponsored messages, may be particularly susceptible to AI scams.
