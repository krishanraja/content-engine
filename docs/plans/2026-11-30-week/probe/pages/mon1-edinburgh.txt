Wizards of the Coast Is Giving Its Entire AI Settlement Cut to D&D Writers

 22 November 2026
 ·
 4 min read
 ·
 News

One and a half billion dollars. That is the size of the Anthropic copyright settlement — the largest in the history of copyright law — and Dungeons & Dragons publisher Wizards of the Coast just decided it does not want its half.

Wizards was one of the claimants in the Bartz v. Anthropic case, filed as the registered copyright owner of D&D novels, modules and stories whose texts ended up in pirate libraries used to train the Claude AI model. Under the standard arrangement, settlement money for each covered work is split 50/50 between publisher and author. Wizards will instead hand its entire share to the writers.

Key point: The settlement pays roughly $3,000 per eligible work, with around 500,000 books covered. Wizards’ share of the D&D claims is estimated by author James Lowder at hundreds of thousands of dollars — all of it now destined for freelance writers.

What the letter to authors said

The news surfaced through James Lowder, a freelance writer and editor who has contracted with Wizards on D&D books including Forgotten Realms and Ravenloft titles. He shared the letter the company is sending to writers on Facebook and LinkedIn.

“While Wizards is the copyright holder, our priority here is to recognize the creators who built these amazing worlds. Dungeons & Dragons became what it is because of the people who wrote for it. So as a thank-you to you and the other authors who helped build this world, Wizards has decided to voluntarily forgo its share of the proceeds and pass its share of the Settlement payments for the Works on to you and the other authors.”

A Wizards representative confirmed the decision to Wargamer: “D&D wouldn’t be D&D without the creators who built its worlds.” The beneficiaries appear to be primarily — perhaps exclusively — independent contractors, up to and including the biggest name on the D&D masthead, R.A. Salvatore.

How the Anthropic settlement works

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI assistant, agreed to the $1.5 billion settlement in September 2025; a judge approved it in July 2026. The case hinged on a distinction the court drew sharply: training AI models on legally obtained books was deemed fair use, but Anthropic’s use of pirated libraries containing hundreds of thousands of unauthorised copies was not.

Payments work out at roughly $3,000 per eligible work before fees, distributed among rights holders of some half a million books. Because publishers often hold the registered copyright on work-for-hire tie-in fiction, they filed the claims in their own names — and the default split gave them half.

Authors could petition for a different split if their publisher agreed. Very few publishers have. Which makes Wizards’ blanket decision to surrender its half an outlier.

Why writers credit R.A. Salvatore

This was not charity that fell from the sky. According to Lowder, conversations between D&D authors and Wizards about the settlement ran for months.

“This all came about because some of the writers used what influence they possessed to raise the issue,” Lowder wrote, “and because the people inside Wizards, up to the highest levels, were willing to consider the situation from more than a financial perspective.”

He singled out Salvatore — the Drizzt Do’Urden novelist and arguably the most commercially successful writer ever to work in the Forgotten Realms — for the heavy lifting, and credited Dan Ayoub and others inside Wizards for doing “the generous (and right) thing”.

Lowder’s own verdict was blunt: “This is remarkable, and amounts to hundreds of thousands of dollars that WotC could have collected going to the writers. And Wizards is doing this at a time when the larger publishers are scrambling to grab as much settlement money as possible from authors.”

Generosity against a rocky backdrop

The cynics — and there are plenty — will note the timing. Wizards of the Coast has spent years rebuilding trust it spent freely.

Its 2023 attempt to rework D&D’s Open Gaming License met ferocious fan and industry backlash and an almost immediate backpedal. AI-generated art surfaced in marketing material and a D&D sourcebook, both greeted sourly. The company laid off around 1,100 people in 2023, including much of the publishing team that liaised with Larian Studios on Baldur’s Gate 3. More recently, Wizards twice refused to voluntarily recognise an employee union, forcing a staff vote whose results are not yet public.

Against that record, several hundred thousand dollars — real money for the freelancers receiving it, pocket change for a subsidiary of Hasbro — reads easily as a PR win.

Both things can be true. The writers getting paid were not obliged to get the publisher’s half, and Lowder’s framing is hard to argue with: for the freelancers who actually build D&D’s worlds, this is meaningful, deserved money arriving at a moment when their industry is contracting.

Exact per-author payouts and a payment timetable have not been published. But for once, the default split in publishing’s favourite legal windfall ran in the writers’ favour — and it took authors like Salvatore asking for months to make it happen.
