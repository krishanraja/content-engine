Web designers share how they’ve adapted to AI in 2026

 By Stacker

 Published

 November 25, 2026 

 5:15 PM 

By Sharon Hafuta for Wix Studio

AI has changed the way designers work, and most would say there’s been a learning curve. But as the tools get better, designers aren’t being replaced; they’re finding their footing and leaning into a new world of web design.

“I hear more and more people say: ‘I enjoy designing. I’m not doing it because I have to. It’s what I love to do,’” says Gali Erez, VP and GM of Wix Studio. “Explaining your design thinking to AI has become a skill people enjoy developing, too.”

The shift shows up in the data: 91% of designers use AI at least weekly, up from 54% a year earlier, according to a 2026 Designer Fund and Foundation Capital survey of more than 900 designers.

The question is: how, exactly, have designers adapted to a new way of working? In this article, freelancers, agency owners and designers share what’s changed in their day-to-day work, and what they’re still figuring out in the age of AI.

Erez says designers love using AI for complex things that would take a long time to build, but there are other things they can do faster themselves. Her team, she says, is finding new ways to let designers edit AI-generated work by hand.

Saleena Aggarwal, senior brand designer at Wix, has seen the difference firsthand. While she was on the social team for just one brand, she built 19 strategy documents. When she moved into a broader social role covering five brands, that system stopped working: There were too many documents for AI to read, and it hurt the results. With Claude’s help, she consolidated them into three layers: general frameworks, brand-specific documents and project instructions, an approach often called context engineering. She says the new system works better than the old one.

She takes a different approach to visual work. “The visual direction always starts on the canvas in Figma, beginning with inspiration and my own ideas,” she says. “AI comes in when it’s useful.”

That doesn’t mean AI stays out of the creative process. She uses it to draft hooks, captions, storyboards and carousels. “I can ask for a lot of versions, pick the one with potential and shape it from there,” she says.

Or Pines, UX designer at Wix, prefers to start with AI. “AI is always my first tool, before I touch code or build a screen in a design tool,” she says. But the final decisions still come down to her. “I do the last mile by hand, in a game of ping-pong. The LLM builds the frame for me, and I fix it, nudge it, try to explain myself, and hand the direction I believe in back to it as a prompt. And around we go again.”

Noam Ramati, brand design team lead at Wix, takes a different route again, starting with the visual concept rather than AI. On her most recent project, she says, “I gathered references and developed the concept and visual direction myself, then went straight to the canvas and designed freely.” AI came later, “once the vision was clear and it was time to turn it into a system that could scale across different screens and use cases.”

Her view: “For me, AI doesn’t have to be the first tab I open on every project.”

Train AI to work the way you work

Aggarwal used to give AI loose instructions and expect a finished product. That didn’t get her very far. “To get good results, I have to be methodical, very clear about the task, and thorough with my changes,” she says. “That actually made me better at my job.”

Pines had her own learning curve. “At first there was frustration and a feeling of not being understood,” she says. The tool had hard-coded every color and font, so she had to get it to follow the brand’s design system. Now, she saves what she and the AI learn at the end of each session in a small cheat sheet inside a skill. That way, the tool has something to refer back to the next time they work together. “What didn’t work yesterday works today, and what doesn’t work today will work tomorrow.”

Freelance web designer Lara Rossi manages over 100 websites and uses AI tools nearly every day. She builds designs with AI tools, but kept running into the same problem: “The design looks great, but what can I do with it?” she says. “I need a CMS.” It’s a common gap with AI-built sites, and it’s why many builders want to know whether an AI-built website will actually work before they launch. She connected the AI-built front end to a CMS and now updates text and pictures by chatting with AI, which points her to the right CMS screen.

Take on projects that used to take months

Freelance agency builder Shahkam Abdul Aziz pairs the Cursor coding agent with the  command-line tools of the platform he builds on. “I don’t have a deep understanding of code, but this setup lets me move so much faster,” he says. “The AI handles things like responsive design, so I can focus on the project itself.” Projects that once took him months now take weeks, sometimes less than two, he says.

Pines sees the difference in smaller tasks, too. “When it’s something small, a fix or QA, I don’t need to wait for anyone,” she says. “I go into the code, fix it, open a PR, and it goes live.” A PR, or pull request, is how developers submit a code change for review. What used to be a ticket waiting in a queue, she can now handle herself.

Ramati has also handed some of the production work over to AI. She used to set breakpoints, which control how a layout adapts in responsive web design, and clean up layers and names, organize frames and prepare files for handoff herself. Now AI takes the first pass. “It helps build and organize the foundation, and I step in afterward to review, refine and make sure everything actually works,” she says. “That frees up time I can spend on more creative decisions.”

Give clients what they couldn’t get before

Erez says AI has changed what clients can do on their own, but that doesn’t mean agencies have less to offer. “A client can go and generate a site on their own,” she says. “But what an agency can do for that client has gotten huge. You’re bringing a much bigger package to the table, and the competition between your client and their competitors is more intense, so there’s more edge to bring.”

Claudio Joao, who runs the design studio Felicé Studios, spent  years building client sites. Then he started exploring AI coding tools like Claude Code and Codex, which let people make a website in an AI coding tool by describing what they want in plain language. He now builds using that approach, which has opened up new possibilities for animation. “I can build every animation and everything I want,” he says. “For me, everything is possible now.”

Deyvison Dionisio runs NoCorp, a web agency in Minas Gerais, Brazil. One fitness client came in with a highly detailed vision for menu behavior and photo layouts. To bring it to life, the team built a custom-coded front end connected to a separate backend platform, a setup known as a headless website. The client signed off within about a week. With the new setup, Dionisio says, “we can build a lot of the things our client wanted that we couldn’t build before.”

Stay the creative director

AI-generated designs can also start looking a little too familiar. In one designers’ group, someone recently asked how to stop Claude from designing like Claude, because everything was starting to look the same. The group’s answer: “Just tell Claude not to design like Claude.” It quickly became a running joke, Erez says.

Ramati saw that problem herself this year. She brought AI into a project early and asked it to develop a visual direction from a relatively open brief. It helped her explore ideas, but the designs didn’t feel distinctive enough. “The design felt generic and predictable.” She gets better results when she sets the direction first and uses AI to push her thinking further. “I get much stronger results when I define the principles and direction first, then use AI to challenge, develop, and expand them.”

Aggarwal says her own role has changed, too. “I feel more like a creative director of my own process than I used to,” she says. “AI can draft and iterate, but someone still has to set the vision, make the calls, and decide what’s good.” With so much generic, generated content around, she thinks design led by people matters more than ever. “Audiences can feel the difference when there’s a real point of view behind the work.”

Pines spends less time building screens and more time talking through ideas. She records voice notes on walks, in the kitchen and at red lights, and sketches in a notebook she photographs afterward. “In the end it all comes back as a prompt, better than anything I’d reach by rushing to build screens, and in far less time,” she says. Juggling several projects at once has taken some getting used to. “Sometimes I’m playing five pianos at once, but little by little, I’m learning to love it, and I’m even starting to make harmonies.”
