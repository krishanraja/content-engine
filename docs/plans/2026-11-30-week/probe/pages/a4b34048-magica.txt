Fable is taking Gossip Goblin creator Zack London’s AI-assisted sci-fi anthology to U.S. and UK cinemas on December 4. Its awards campaign will require evidence of human authorship, qualifying animation and theatrical bookings; online popularity alone cannot establish commercial demand.

Fable, the AI entertainment studio behind the Showrunner television-generation technology, has moved Gods Don’t Give Gifts to December 4, 2026, as the filmmakers prepare a Best Animated Feature Oscar campaign. Director Zack London, known online as Gossip Goblin, is bringing the science-fiction world of his popular shorts to a feature-length horror anthology. The release-date report says Fable shifted the U.S. opening from October 30 and had not disclosed a distributor.

The trailer launch puts a familiar commercial challenge behind the technological pitch: turning an online following into paying cinema audiences. The awards push adds a separate test of the finished film’s authorship, animation and release history. Neither a large view count nor an announced campaign resolves those questions.

Character imagery and production credits on the official Gods Don’t Give Gifts website. Credit: Gossip Goblin. Source: Film Factual.

An online audience and a studio with an AI stake

Gossip Goblin’s science-fiction shorts have accumulated more than one billion views and the project has more than three million followers across platforms, according to the production coverage. Those totals describe the online property’s reach; they do not measure unique potential ticket buyers or advance sales.

London co-produces with Edward Saatchi, Fable Studio’s CEO, whose earlier work includes an executive-producer credit on Wolves in the Walls: It’s All Over, a 2019 Emmy winner for Outstanding Innovation in Interactive Media.

Fable also has a commercial interest in the wider adoption of AI entertainment. Its Showrunner technology generated the South Park episodes that circulated during the 2023 Hollywood strikes. A July 2025 interview with Saatchi reported an Amazon-led funding round for Fable. In that interview, Saatchi described plans for a new AI content platform while acknowledging that he did not know whether people wanted it. The funding report concerns the studio, not a disclosed budget for Gods Don’t Give Gifts.

For this film, the planned promotion includes a limited U.S. and UK theatrical engagement, trailers and lobby posters in cinemas, and billboard advertising beginning in November. The filmmakers say it is the first AI film to receive an MPA rating—R—and to be campaigned for Best Animated Feature.

The release reports disclose no production budget, marketing spend or confirmed screen count. They therefore provide no basis for calculating a break-even audience or measuring a cost saving against conventional animation.

People behind the generated images

The anthology follows four connected stories on NiiroCradle, a world where consciousness can be traded and physical bodies replaced. London directed and co-wrote the screenplay with Jacob Batchelor. In the account of the production, the filmmakers emphasize human voice performances and an international team of artists, performers and technical contributors making the creative decisions.

That description qualifies the shorthand that the film was made entirely with AI. Generative tools are extensively involved, but writing, direction, performance and artistic judgment remain human contributions in the reported process. The accounts do not give a detailed breakdown of the tools or their use across the finished film.

The broad publicity claim that this is the first AI-generated feature to reach theaters has also been challenged in coverage of the release. A specific precedent comes from Başka Sinema, a Turkish independent and arthouse distributor. In a July 2025 announcement, the distributor said the documentary Post Truth opened on July 11 at 58 locations, with AI-generated visuals, music and voice. That production also had human co-writers.

The distributor’s account does not independently settle who was first under every definition of AI filmmaking. It does make an unqualified claim to the first global theatrical release untenable as an established fact. Nor does that earlier release resolve the separate claims about an MPA rating or an animated-feature campaign.

The Academy’s animated-feature eligibility rules, printed page 9, specify qualifying screenings and restrictions on previously exhibited material. Source: Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences.

What the Oscar bid must establish before December

The Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, which administers the Oscars, does not treat generative AI use alone as a barrier to nomination. Its 99th Academy Awards rules say the tools themselves neither help nor hurt nomination chances; judging takes human creative authorship into account, and the Academy may request more information about it.

The animation category has a more specific definition: movement must be created by human means, and animation of the primary subjects must occupy at least 75% of the running time, excluding opening and end credits. Filmmakers must supply evidence that their work qualifies. Human writing and voice acting are relevant context, but do not by themselves establish that the images meet that definition.

Release history matters too. The category excludes episodes extracted from a larger series. For the theatrical qualification route, no more than 15% of a film’s running time may have appeared in nontheatrical media beforehand. The reports establish a connection to London’s online universe, but do not establish how much previously shown footage, if any, appears in the feature. Sharing a fictional world is not evidence that this limit has been breached.

The theatrical route requires seven consecutive days in the same commercial cinema in one of six qualifying U.S. metropolitan areas, with at least three paid-admission screenings daily, including one beginning between 6 p.m. and 10 p.m. The animated-feature submission deadline for films qualifying in the second half of 2026 is November 12 at 5 p.m. PT. Films qualifying afterward must supply theater documentation by that deadline confirming they will meet the release requirements.

The next evidence needed is therefore concrete: qualifying bookings, the submitted film and supporting production information. The Academy’s Animation Branch Executive Committee resolves category eligibility. Its determination would settle whether the film can compete; ticket sales after the planned December 4 opening would begin to show whether London’s online audience will follow him into cinemas.
