“Maharaja in Denims,” author Khushwant Singh’s novel about a young man who relives the life of 19th-century Sikh ruler Maharaja Ranjit Singh, is nearing the finish line as a feature-length generative AI film – one its makers say they effectively had to make three times.
The team hopes to release the film by April 2027, in time for Baisakhi, the Punjabi harvest festival. Ahmedabad-based Twenty-Twenty One Studios has come aboard as co-producer and is weighing a theatrical run followed by non-theatrical distribution. The book was optioned in July 2020 by producer Guneet Monga Kapoor, but that version never got made. Singh, who is also CEO of Intelliflicks Studios, the company producing the AI film, tells Variety the timing worked against it. The option landed in the middle of the COVID pandemic, cinemas were slow to recover, and a story spanning Ranjit Singh’s reign as well as 20th- and 21st-century India was always going to be a costly gamble for a producer.
The AI route came from Gurdeep Pall, then corporate VP of incubations at Microsoft. During a visit to Chandigarh in 2023, Pall asked Singh why the book had never reached the screen and proposed trying it with generative AI. His work with OpenAI’s models had convinced him the technology would mature within a couple of years to the point where a realistic feature was possible.
“It has unfolded exactly as predicted,” Singh says.
The pair co-founded Intelliflicks that year and unveiled the project with a short clip in 2024.
Choosing realism over mythology or sci-fi made consistency the central problem. Production began in 2024 with Midjourney and Runway, shifted to Google Veo, with Nano Banana handling stills, then moved through Kling AI and Seedance 2.0 before wrapping on tools available on Higgsfield. ChatGPT and Google Imagen helped build early character references, and keeping faces, costumes and sets in line took heavy manual fixes with face-swapping software, Photoshop and Adobe Firefly.
Nano Banana proved the turning point, letting the team generate new camera angles and compositions from reference images with far fewer manual corrections. Even so, Singh says near-perfect shot-to-shot consistency only became achievable four or five months ago. The team rebuilt the stills for the entire film three times before locking a look. When Higgsfield began allowing moving shots to be generated straight from character sheets, switching over would have added time and money, so the team animated the stills it already had.
Performances went through a similar evolution. Early on, real actors guided them via Runway Act-One. A single person created most of the motion shots so that each character would speak, move and react the same way throughout. The short clips also gave the editor less room to shape scenes than conventional footage allows.
“For example, if a lampshade is placed at a particular spot in a two-minute scene, it must remain in exactly the same position across every generated shot, irrespective of the camera angle. Even the slightest inconsistency can disrupt the cinematic experience,” Singh says.
That learning curve drove up the budget. Singh says new technology usually cuts costs to around a tenth, but the team redid close to an hour of the film three times as the tools improved. He puts the cost at more than $500,000, excluding marketing, which has yet to begin.
“Throughout this technological evolution, the creative process has remained entirely human-led. Screenwriting, direction, storyboarding, shot composition, performance evaluation and final creative decisions remain with our filmmaking team. AI is the technology that helps us translate that vision onto the screen,” Singh says.
Set in 2008, the film follows Hari Sandhu, who has flashbacks to three earlier lives: as Ranjit Singh, as a victim of Partition violence in 1947 and as a victim of the 1984 anti-Sikh violence. Kaur chose pivotal episodes from Ranjit Singh’s life and added material from outside the novel to give his story more screen heft. The team stuck closely to historical sources, Singh says, but took liberties where the tools couldn’t render period costumes accurately. Keeping faces and headgear consistent was also a major hurdle when production began, and some early imagery still bears the marks. Singh vetted the screenplay for accuracy with input from historians, but leaves day-to-day filmmaking to Kaur.
The AI film joins a long line of Ranjit Singh projects. Last week, Katha Studios and B4 Makers Studios unveiled a big-screen biopic of the ruler, set to shoot in 2027. Actor-politician Raj Babbar’s series “Maharaja Ranjit Singh” ran on public broadcaster Doordarshan from 2010 to 2011, while Contiloe Pictures’ “Sher-e-Punjab: Maharaja Ranjit Singh,” with Damanpreet Singh in the title role, aired on Life OK in 2017. Feature versions have proved harder to get off the ground. Babbar’s planned film “The Lord of Five Rivers: Maharaja Ranjit Singh” was shelved, and “Border” director J.P. Dutta lined up a Ranjit Singh biopic after his 2018 war film “Paltan.” Ranjit Singh’s son Duleep Singh, the last Sikh maharaja, was the subject of Kavi Raz’s 2017 feature “The Black Prince,” starring Satinder Sartaaj.
The music is entirely human-made. Every song was written, composed and performed by people, and dubbing and narration were recorded with actors in the studio. Playback singer Sukhwinder Singh sang two versions of the title track, composed by up-and-coming singer Hargun Kaur, after the team also considered Rahat Fateh Ali Khan, Wazir Patar and Daler Mehndi. Recorded in Mumbai, the track pairs modern production with traditional instruments of 19th-century Punjab. Internet sensation Inayat Kaur Bajaj makes her film debut, Punjab-based Azaad and Gurprasad Singh composed and performed several tracks, and the lyricists include Harmanjit Singh, known for “Rani Tatt,” as well as Ferro Fluid, Azaad and director Kaur.
On copyright, Singh relays Pall’s view that there are two separate questions. What data the AI models were trained on is one for the companies that own them. How Intelliflicks uses those models is the studio’s responsibility, and it avoids drawing on any style or IP that belongs to a person or company.
Singh pushes back on concerns that AI filmmaking will put actors, animators and other crew out of work, pointing to a century of the industry adopting new tools, VFX among them.
“Having made this film we see a need for human creativity and talent, from storytelling to cinematography to music. Sometimes the specific roles change but human involvement will be needed to make compelling cinema with AI,” he says.
Glitches may remain, Singh concedes, but he likens them to the flaws of the earliest motion pictures.
“I definitely see ‘Maharaja in Denims’ as an experiment, and a successful one at that, because technically, it will be the first full-length generative AI film rooted in realism, and it will be there for everyone to see,” he says.
Intelliflicks is already exploring an adaptation of Singh’s next novel, “The Opium Toffee,” a love story set in Punjab during the 1980s, and has a spy script and a war film in development. The company is also open to partnering with outside filmmakers on hybrid productions.
