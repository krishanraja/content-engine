Coca-Cola trades AI Christmas ads for live-action Enjoy Joy films

New Enjoy Joy films put holiday rituals on screen, with a US caravan, family recipes and a move away from AI-made ads.

Zoe Park

20 Nov 2026

For decades, Coca-Cola has made Christmas recognizable with a red truck, Santa imagery and the promise of people coming together. The images are familiar enough that a new Coke holiday commercial arrives with expectations attached, even before anyone presses play.

That history makes the brand's latest decision unusually interesting. After two holiday seasons dominated by arguments over AI-generated advertising, Coca-Cola is putting family gatherings, handmade food traditions and live-action storytelling at the center of its new U.S. films. The creative is called "Enjoy Joy," and its approach offers a fresh test of what holiday nostalgia should look like on screen.

A Christmas ad made for family rituals

A game of football, a turkey being deep-fried and a meal shared with relatives are ordinary moments that can feel enormous in the middle of the holidays. They are the kinds of scenes people recognize from their own routines, not a fantasy universe built around a product.

Those experiences appear in Coca-Cola's new "Enjoy Joy" commercials, which began their 2026 U.S. run on November 19. The brand has introduced two versions, including creative tailored for Hispanic audiences, with 30-, 15- and six-second cuts for television, digital video, streaming and social media. The new holiday films sit inside the wider "Refresh Your Holidays" campaign platform that Coca-Cola introduced in 2025.

The ads do not ask viewers to learn a new mascot, follow a plot twist or decode a clever cultural reference. Instead, they return to familiar holiday scenes and suggest that the brand belongs somewhere within them. That is a different creative bet from trying to make a Christmas advertisement feel technically unprecedented.

There is a useful distinction between nostalgia and repetition here. Coca-Cola can repeat its holiday iconography and still change the story it tells around it. Where the trucks and Santa graphics preserve recognition, the new film's human-scale moments create room for viewers to find a memory of their own.

Why Coca-Cola is making films without generative AI

The biggest difference between "Enjoy Joy" and Coca-Cola's recent holiday advertising is not the red color palette. It is the production choice behind the characters on screen.

The company's holiday films in 2024 and 2025 used generative AI and became the subject of criticism over visual quality and emotional authenticity. For the new U.S. commercials, Coca-Cola has moved away from that method. The 2026 films were directed by Paul Dektor and produced by Mayan Productions, with work developed through WPP Open X and led by Ogilvy, as detailed in the November 19 campaign reporting.

That change is newsworthy precisely because Coca-Cola had invested in AI-led holiday creative for more than one year. It illustrates how a brand can continue experimenting with technology while reconsidering which production style best fits a particular emotional brief. The company has not publicly shown that a move to filmed creative will outperform its earlier holiday campaigns, and the decision should not be treated as evidence that it has stopped using AI everywhere.

Coca-Cola Trademark vice president Stacy Jackson described the campaign as finding new ways to be part of the season while staying connected to traditions people already love. Her comment frames the challenge as continuity rather than technological one-upmanship: a campaign still has to feel unmistakably like Coke.

The cultural tension is easy to understand. A holiday advertisement often trades in memory, family rituals and tiny gestures that seem authentic because they are imperfect. Even a technically polished image can struggle when the audience is invited to think about the tool used to make it instead of the scene itself. That is an editorial reading of this creative shift, not a measured reaction to the new films.

From grandma recipes to a nationwide holiday caravan

The film is only one piece of the 2026 holiday push. Coca-Cola is also giving smaller, familiar traditions a home on social platforms through "Grandma's Holiday Table," a series about relatives who preserve family recipes, stories and seasonal rituals.

The premise is more specific than another generic message about togetherness. It identifies the people who remember how a dish should taste, who taught someone else to cook it and who keep a tradition alive because they keep repeating it. For an audience watching short videos, that gives the brand a subject grounded in the lives of the people it wants to reach.

Creator-led material is planned across Meta, TikTok, Pinterest and YouTube, while an Amazon media partnership combines creator activity with commerce. These elements give the holiday message different forms, from a filmed story to social content that can sit alongside recipes, family photos and shopping.

The physical side is just as important. Coca-Cola's Holiday Caravan began touring in Nashville on November 5 and is scheduled to visit communities nationwide, including a stop in New York's Times Square on November 24. Families are offered photo opportunities with Santa and the company's polar bear character, along with festive activities and giveaways.

More than 270 planned stops in over 28 U.S. cities. The Holiday Caravan gives the seasonal message an in-person component, according to Coca-Cola's campaign details.

The brand is also working with the USO on opportunities to send messages of gratitude to military personnel and families. On store shelves, Santa-themed limited-edition packaging will appear across Coca-Cola, Coca-Cola Zero Sugar and Diet Coke, while Holiday Creamy Vanilla and its zero-sugar variant return for a limited period.

Taken together, the pieces are not identical advertisements pasted across different screens. They are different versions of participation: watching a film, remembering a recipe, visiting a caravan, picking up a festive pack or sharing a message. Each one puts an established holiday association into a different part of daily life.

The holiday marketing race is getting more handmade

Coca-Cola is not alone in making the construction of a holiday ad part of its creative identity. In a season full of fast-moving digital material, visible craft has become one way to make a campaign feel distinct.

Levi's has revisited stop-motion claymation for its "Let's Get Fuzzy" holiday work, while beauty brand e.l.f. has used stop-motion animation for "Made to Stand Out." Both examples appear in recent reporting on 2026 holiday advertising. Neither is a direct replica of Coca-Cola's approach, but all three draw attention to the way an image was made, not simply how quickly it could be produced.

There is no single conclusion that handmade-looking advertising is always more effective. A technically ambitious campaign can still be moving, and an old-fashioned shoot can still be forgettable. What matters is whether the creative method helps deliver the audience experience the brand promises.

For a denim label, claymation can refer back to the brand's own advertising history. For a beauty company, hand-built characters can connect to an idea about individuality. For Coca-Cola, familiar people and places keep the emphasis on Christmas rituals that already exist outside an advertising campaign.

The broader pattern matters because holiday work has to compete against memories as well as other brands. Consumers have seen countless snowy streets, family dinners and wrapped presents. The strongest distinction may come from a believable detail within those familiar scenes, rather than a promise that the technology behind them is new.

What marketers should know about the festive reset

"Enjoy Joy" is a reminder that cultural memory is an asset brands have to maintain. The new films and their accompanying activities show how a seasonal idea can reach people without asking them to abandon the traditions that made the brand recognizable in the first place.

Keep the familiar codes, change the human moment. Coca-Cola still uses its Santa packaging and holiday imagery, but the films give center stage to families, shared meals and everyday rituals. Recognition and fresh storytelling do not have to be opposites.

Make social content about something people already do. The "Grandma's Holiday Table" concept begins with recipes and family stories. That is a more concrete invitation than a vague instruction to celebrate the season with a brand.

Give a campaign a place in the real world. A caravan photo or community visit is a different kind of encounter from an online ad. The planned tour connects the festive imagery with something people can see and experience locally.

Match production choices to the emotional task. The new films choose a live-action language after earlier AI-based efforts proved divisive. That does not settle the argument about generative AI, but it does show a large advertiser making a different decision for a highly recognizable seasonal property.

The question for brand teams is less about whether a campaign looks traditional or futuristic. It is whether the creative earns its place in a moment people already care about. The strongest holiday storytelling often begins with a simple observation about how people spend time together.

Coca-Cola has chosen to make that observation visible through a football game, a family table and a familiar red truck. Whether the change brings better results remains to be seen. What is clear from the launch is that the brand is putting its long-established place in Christmas culture back at the center of the story.
