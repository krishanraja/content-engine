The detail that decides whether Genie 4 is a toy or a line item is not the 720p picture. It is that Goog​le bills the model by the second of streamed output and keeps billing until your code tells it to stop. Genie 4 reached the Gem​ini API on November 19, 2026, one day after it went live inside Project Genie for Goog​le AI Ultra subscribers, and on the same day Project Genie invitations started reaching Goog​le AI Pro members who had joined the waitlist. That pricing model — roughly $0.10 per second, no free tier, no caching, no batch discounts — is the part worth reading carefully before anyone puts a world model behind a product surface. The comparison everyone will make is against Gem​ini Omni 1.2 Flash, Goog​le's offline video model at about $0.07 per second of 720p; the two look close on a rate card and behave nothing alike in a build.
Three Dates, and Only One of Them Is the Launch
Genie 4 has been announced three times in three weeks, which is why the coverage of it keeps talking past itself. Goog​le DeepMind published the research announcement on October 27, 2026, with access limited to trusted testers and a public waitlist. On November 18 it began rolling the model out inside Project Genie to eligible Goog​le AI Ultra subscribers, alongside upgrades to Genie 3 and a community gallery. On November 19 it opened the door developers actually care about: models/genie-4 on the Gem​ini API, through the Interactions API and Goog​le AI Studio.
The fourth event is the one in the signal: on the evening of November 19, TestingCatalog posted a screenshot of an invitation telling a Goog​le AI Pro member they were off the Project Genie waitlist and now had access "as part of your Goog​le AI Pro subscription." That is a user report, not a Goog​le announcement, and at the time of writing the Project Genie page still reads "available at launch for eligible Goog​le AI Ultra subscribers. Goog​le AI Pro members can join the waitlist." But waitlist invitations going out in volume is how a staged rollout widens, and it is the difference between a $99.99 subscription and a $19.99 one for the consumer half of this product.
What none of those four events changed: the model. Same weights, same session cap, same limitations Goog​le published in October and again in the November model card.
The Real Price of a World, Per Second
Genie 4 is priced as video tokens rather than as a request. Goog​le's rate card lists $1.50 per million input tokens for text and reference images, $9.00 per million output text tokens, and $17.50 per million output video tokens with the 48 kHz stereo audio bundled in. Because the model emits 240 video tokens per frame at 24 frames per second — 5,760 tokens a second — that works out to roughly $0.10 per second of streamed world, or about $6.05 a minute. These are Goog​le's published numbers, not an independent measurement; nobody outside Goog​le has published a metered bill to check them against.
The arithmetic that matters is what a session can cost. Goog​le caps a session at 120 seconds, and says visual coherence and object permanence are strongest during the first 45 to 90 seconds. So the useful part of a session is $4.50 to $9.00, and the ceiling on one uninterrupted session is about $12.00. That is cheap for a pre-visualization pass and expensive for anything that runs while a user is idle.
The billing mechanics are where a prototype turns into an incident. Goog​le's own documentation warns that exiting a local block or closing a browser tab detaches the transport without stopping server-side generation. The only things that stop the meter are complete() or cancel(); detachment does not, and Genie 4 has no session resumption, so a dropped socket cannot be resumed and still costs you until the session ends. Goog​le's prompt guide notes that AI Studio itself pauses a stream when the tab is hidden for more than 10 seconds, precisely so idle tabs do not burn paid video tokens. Your client has to do that job yourself.
What Calling It Actually Looks Like
Genie 4 is not a generateContent model and it is not on the Live API. It runs on the Interactions API, which Goog​le has described as generally available since June 2026 and as the place new models and capabilities launch. The endpoint accepts models/genie-4 or the bare genie-4, requires api_version v1beta, and requires a paid-tier API key — the free tier is listed as not available.
The connection model is the unusual part, and it shapes what you can build:
• Transport — WebRTC for browsers (video element plus an SCTP data channel for JSON control events) or WebSocket for Python and Node, which delivers base64 JPEG frames and 48 kHz 16-bit stereo PCM you decode yourself.
• Session tokens — the server creates an interaction, mints a single-session auth token, and only that token goes to the client. The API key never reaches the browser, which is the one piece of this design that is straightforwardly good.
• Events — a world_setup event seeds the world from text, a 16:9 reference image under 48 KB on WebRTC, or both; user_action carries native key_down/key_up for WASD and the four arrow keys; mid-session input events carry text that changes weather, objects or events while the stream runs.
• Startup cost — first frame arrives roughly 5 to 9 seconds after the world prompt, per Goog​le's integration guide. That is dead air you pay for only insofar as tokens are emitted, but it is dead air a user stares at.
• Steering latency — about 0.2 to 0.5 seconds of visual response at low network round-trip time. Goog​le serves the model from the United States, Western Europe and Japan, and says higher latency outside those regions delays steering and can reduce frame rate. A product aimed at users in, say, Brazil or India is not running this at the frame rate the demos show.
The Limits That Decide Whether It Ships
Rate limits are stated in concurrent sessions, not requests, which is the right unit for a streaming model and an awkward one for capacity planning. On API tier 1 you get two concurrent active sessions and 50 requests per day; tier 2 gives five and 250; tier 3 gives ten and 1,000. In-session control messages do not consume separate quota. Two simultaneous worlds is a demo budget, not a launch budget.
Capability gaps are just as consequential, because they are the ones an architect discovers late:
• No function calling, no structured outputs, no code execution and no context caching on Genie 4. The reasoning has to happen in a different model and be pushed in as text.
• No Batch API, no Flex inference and no Priority inference. You cannot trade latency for cost, and you cannot buy your way to the front of a queue.
• Safety filters are session-fatal, not request-fatal. Goog​le's prompt guide says real human faces, public figures and recognizable brand IP can block a world_setup or close an active session mid-run — which, given the billing model, means a blocked session is a session you were paying for.
• Roughly a minute of usable coherence. Two minutes is the cap, 45 to 90 seconds is the honest working range, and Goog​le lists camera latency, intricate physical interactions, audio precision and long-term scene permanence as open problems.
Genie 4 or Gem​ini Omni 1.2 Flash?
Both are video models with native audio on the same API key, and they answer different questions. Gem​ini Omni 1.2 Flash generates and edits fixed clips of up to 15 seconds, is generally available on the paid tier, charges $12.00 per million video tokens — about $0.07 per second at 720p, $0.023 at 360p, $0.104 at 1080p with upsampling, $0.209 at 4K — and can be batched, edited and upsampled offline. Genie 4 generates a world you steer in real time and cannot be scheduled, batched or cached at all.
• Interactive vs. fixed — Genie 4 responds to keys and mid-session prompts; Omni renders a clip you then edit.
• Cost per second — about $0.10 for Genie 4 vs. about $0.07 for Omni at 720p, before Omni's cheaper 360p option and Genie's unavoidable session overhead.
• Ceiling — 120 seconds of live stream vs. 15 seconds of clip, but the clip can go to 4K and the stream tops out at 720p.
• Transport — a persistent WebRTC or WebSocket session vs. an ordinary request.
• Ops burden — Genie 4 needs session lifecycle management and a billing kill switch; Omni needs neither.
If the output is a shot, use Omni. If the output is someone walking around inside it, pay the extra three cents a second and accept the operational weight.
What the Pro Waitlist Actually Changes
For a developer, nothing measurable. The Gem​ini API path to Genie 4 is gated by billing tier, not by subscription, and a Goog​le AI Pro membership does not come with an API key that unlocks it. What the invitations in the signal change is the consumer side: Project Genie is drifting from a $99.99 Goog​le AI Ultra feature toward something the $19.99 Goog​le AI Pro tier can reach, which is roughly how Goog​le has widened access to every Gem​ini-adjacent product it has shipped. If invitations are going out now, the reasonable planning assumption is broader Pro access in weeks, not quarters — but assume it, do not quote it, because Goog​le has not published a date and the waitlist page still reads as a waitlist.
If you already pay for AI Ultra, the API is a separate meter and a separate key. Nothing about the subscription discount carries over.
Where a Router Fits in a Streaming Stack
Genie 4 is not on OrcaRouter and there is no honest way to pretend a stateful, per-second video stream is a chat completion. The reasoning half of a Genie pipeline is a different matter: the prompt construction, the event planning, the safety pre-checks and the post-session analysis are ordinary model calls, and Gemini 4 Argon at $2.00 / $10.00 per million tokens and Gemini 3.8 Flash at $0.75 / $3.75 per million are both live on our catalogue through one OpenAI-compatible key. That is one contract and one bill for the text side while the video side stays on Goog​le's meter, and the 0% markup means a Goog​le price change on those models lands on our side the same day rather than whenever a reseller reprices.
Automatic failover matters more here than in a normal chat workload, because a Genie session cannot be retried cheaply: if the planning call that produces a world prompt fails, you have burned the setup latency and, in the worst case, part of a paid session. Routing that call across providers is the cheap insurance.
Who Should Spend the Money This Week
Build the prototype if the thing you are testing is whether an interactive world changes a user's behaviour — a spatial design review, an agent-training environment, a pre-visualization pass where a director wants to walk the set. At $12 for a two-minute session you can run fifty sessions for the price of a day of a contractor's time, and the 720p ceiling is not the constraint on that decision.
Wait if the thing you are testing is cost per output minute for finished video. Genie 4 is more expensive per second than Gem​ini Omni 1.2 Flash, capped at 720p, and offers no batch or priority path; the world model's advantage is that a human is inside it, and that advantage evaporates the moment your pipeline is unattended. Wait too if your users are outside the three serving regions, because the frame rate you demo in California is not the frame rate a user in São Paulo sees.
The open question worth watching is whether Goog​le publishes an idle or paused-session rate. Right now the meter runs on streamed output until completion is called, which makes every Genie 4 integration a small exercise in billing discipline. A pause primitive, or a cheaper low-motion mode, would change the economics of this model more than any quality improvement in the next release.
