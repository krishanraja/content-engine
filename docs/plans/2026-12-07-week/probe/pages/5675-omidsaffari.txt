Genie 4 API: How One Live World Session Works, and What It Costs
Genie 4 opened on the Gemini API on Nov 19, 2026. How one live world session runs, in Google's own code, what a minute costs and where it stops.

The rule that falls out of it: a browser app or game takes WebRTC, Google's recommended browser transport. The browser decodes sound and picture natively onto a
<video autoplay playsinline>
element, and your control events travel on a separate data channel. A Python or Node.js backend, a frame recorder or your own pipeline takes WebSocket. There, every frame arrives as one base64 JPEG in a
step.delta
event, the audio as raw 16-bit 48 kHz stereo PCM, and only one
receive()
loop is allowed per connection. Google's sample renderer numbers frames as they arrive and drops any that finishes decoding after a newer one, so a slow decode never paints over the present.
Genie 4 serves from data centers in the United States, Western Europe and Japan. Google expects round trips under 150 ms in North America, Western and Central Europe and East Asia; further away, keyboard steering lags and the frame rate can drop. Google's advice: put backend relays near a serving region, and test browser WebRTC latency from your target region before you ship reflex-heavy controls.

Text is slower than a key for a reason. Genie 4 plans changes to the world between generation steps, and its planner reads incoming text only at step boundaries, every 4 frames at 24 fps. So a text change takes about 1-2 seconds to show. Two more rules from the guide. To talk to a character in front of you, wrap the words in quotation marks, as in “What vintage would you recommend?”. And describe changes inside the current view: a request for a camera cut, such as “cut to aerial view”, breaks the world's spatial continuity.

What makes a world hold together
Google's
prompt and controls guide
opens with a warning: prompts written like movie trailers behave unpredictably in Genie 4. Room-wide triggers fire at frame 1, music keywords turn into physical sound effects, and camera cuts break the space apart. Its five tested patterns come down to five habits.
Hang rules on objects, not places
A
Rule:

Three phrasing rules come with it. Tie the trigger to a concrete object or character (“Pulling the red lever”), never to a place (“Entering the bridge”), because the model cannot measure the edge of a zone. Put the trigger “far away from me”, or Genie 4 may fire it during setup, before the player presses W. And tell a character to wait, with “who waits until a rule is triggered”. Google calls rules probabilistic steering, not a game script: when a button must work every time, bind it to a key.
Put your game's verbs on keys
Native keys move and look; everything else is a key your client binds to a text prompt, sent 10 times at 150 ms with a 2-second cooldown. Google's canal village binds three: E for heavy snow, Q for golden fireworks, R for “Send a flock of white doves flying past”. Google calls walk-first worlds like this the most reliable, because walking gives a 1-2 second change time to land.
Its desert-buggy cockpit variant works when the round trip to a serving region is under 100 ms. In the buggy, S slows or reverses the view gradually instead of braking, panning while holding W bends its path toward where the camera looks, and above 150 ms of latency tight canyon turns clip through the rock walls.
Seed with a 16:9 picture
A 16:9
reference_image
fixes the first frame's composition, palette and art style before the user takes the camera. Google prefers stylized art, architectural renders or empty landscapes. Street photos with identifiable bystanders, public figures or copyrighted logos often trip the safety filters and end the stream. A portrait or square picture forces padding or invented side walls. And the picture is not a memory: walk forward into it and it holds; turn 180 degrees and look back 10 seconds later, and you see a fresh interpretation, not the starting pixels.
Ask for the sound of contact, and show what moves you
Genie 4's planner rewrites prompts before it renders picture and sound, and the guide's advice is to write with it. It strips or rewrites soundtrack requests and narrow drones such as “engine hum”, “deep rumble” or “jet roar”, so ask for the sound of surfaces meeting: footsteps on wet cobblestone, gravel crunching, water lapping against wood. Give forward motion a visible cause in the first frame, or the model invents one mid-session, such as a boat hull, a kayak paddle or a vehicle hood. Even with no key held, small things keep moving: steam, ripples, leaves, lantern light.

What you can build with it this week
Google names three uses in
its API post
: prototyping interactive experiences, visualizing designs for spatial concepts, and training robotics and AI agents. Each fits the documented limits in its own way.
A playable concept.
For a pitch, a game idea or a brand-free campaign moment, a walk-first world with three key-bound events, like Google's canal village, is the shape the guide trusts most. Plan a forward path of 30-60 seconds; it fits in one session with room to spare.
A one-object moment.
The espresso vignette is the template: one object, one change on a key, the camera held on it, 25-45 seconds. Two warnings for product teams. Readable text such as signs, gauges and clocks deforms over time, and recognizable brand IP can block a world at startup or end it mid-stream. It can show what a thing does; it cannot be trusted with what its label says.
A walk into a design.
Seed with an architectural render, one of the picture types Google prefers, and walk forward into it. Don't promise a return trip: after 8 seconds or more out of view, landmarks shift. Where a layout or a measurement must hold, Genie 4 is the sketch, not the record.
Frames for an agent or robotics experiment.
WebSocket hands you every frame as a JPEG and the audio as PCM, and Google lists it for frame capture and custom pipelines. With no persistent 3D scene and counts that drift, a generated world is a source of test ideas, not test evidence;
our Physical AI guide
covers where validated simulation takes over.
Worth a prototype this week when every line holds:
The experience fits inside 120 seconds, and its best part inside the first 45-90.
It moves forward or stays on one object, with no return trip.
Your users are near the United States, Western Europe or Japan.
It needs no exact text or counts, no real people and no brands.
It needs no voice or camera input, no tool calls and no saved state between sessions.
If one fails, read the limits below before you build.

Where it stops
Google's limitations, in its words
Short-horizon object permanence:
“Genie 4 simulates frames over a rolling temporal window rather than maintaining a persistent 3D scene graph. Looking away from an object (8+ seconds) or turning 360 degrees can cause landmarks, doors, or buttons to shift or reset, and visual stability may degrade past 60-90 seconds (hard cap 120 seconds).”
Two-speed responsiveness:
“Native WASD and arrow-key actions take effect within ~0.2-0.5s, whereas mid-session text input prompts pass through world planning and take ~1-2s to appear on screen.”
Fixed 16:9 output and regional serving footprint:
“Output streams at 1280x720 (720p, 16:9, 24 fps) from data centers in the United States, Western Europe, and Japan. Portrait aspect ratios (9:16) and configurable frame rates are not supported, and connections outside North America, Western and Central Europe, and East Asia experience higher steering latency and potentially lower frame rates.”
No live microphone/webcam input or tool calling:
“Genie 4 accepts text, a single 16:9 startup image, and keyboard/camera user_action events. It does not accept live user audio or video streams, and does not support function calling, Google Search grounding, structured outputs, or session resumption across completed interactions.”
Safety and recitation filters:
“Prompts or reference images containing identifiable real people, public figures, or copyrighted brand IP can be blocked at startup or terminate an active stream mid-session.”
Readable text and exact counts:
“Signs, gauges, and clocks deform over time, and exact object counts drift across camera pans.”
Google's post of November 19 says Gemini reasoning keeps the world state consistent and coherent throughout. The list above is how far “throughout” reaches: a rolling window of seconds, not a world that remembers.

Common questions
When did the Genie 4 API open?
On November 19, 2026. Google's changelog lists Genie 4 as generally available that day on the Interactions API (
v1beta
), and Google AI Studio opened its playground the same day.
Is the Genie 4 API free?
No. It needs a paid-tier Gemini API key; the free tier does not support it, and the AI Studio playground needs a paid-tier key too.
How much does a minute of Genie 4 cost?
About $6.05 of video output, at $17.50 per 1M video tokens and 5,760 tokens a second. A full 120-second session is about $12.10.
How long can a Genie 4 session last?
120 seconds at most. Google says coherence and object permanence are strongest in the first 45-90 seconds.
Can a Genie 4 world run in portrait on a phone?
No. Output is fixed at 1280x720, 16:9; portrait 9:16 and other frame rates are not supported.
Can I save a world and come back to it?
No. Genie 4 has no session resumption across completed interactions, and within a session it keeps a rolling window, not a 3D scene.
Key Takeaways
Genie 4 is generally available on the Gemini API since November 19, 2026: models/genie-4, on the Interactions API, paid tier only.
One session: create it and mint a token on your server, connect over WebRTC or WebSocket, send one world_setup, steer with keys and text, end with complete().
About $0.10 a second and $6.05 a minute; a full 120-second session is about $12.10.
Build short and forward: one path or one object, 25-90 seconds, no return trip, nothing that must stay exact.
A closed tab does not stop the bill. complete() or cancel() does.
