 Etsy clarifies AI terms after seller backlash

 One veteran illustrator dismisses revisions as “sugar coating.”

After backlash from sellers frustrated by Etsy’s new AI policies, which appeared to allow the company to train AI on user artwork, Etsy has now clarified its stance.

In a note published on Monday, CEO Kruti Patel Goyal discussed recent tweaks to the company’s terms of service and admitted that her team “should’ve given you more context on what the updates meant for your creative content.”

“Our recent terms update was meant to reflect the evolving technologies we’re using to build better tools and experiences for sellers and the Etsy community,” Goyal wrote this week. “It was not meant to enable us to train AI models for the purpose of creating copycats of your items. We do not and will not do that.”

Aaron Meshon, a longtime professional illustrator and Etsy seller, told Ars that even though the company had dialed back its AI language somewhat, the changes were mere “sugar coating.”

He said he still plans on shutting down his 15-year-old Etsy store on December 1.

“I would say I’m losing 20 percent of my yearly income,” he said. “It’s a few mortgage payments.”

He noted that Etsy’s announcement also seems to be rather tone-deaf, given that many retailers make a substantial portion of their annual revenue in the run-up to the end-of-year holiday season.

“This is the worst two-to-three weeks out of the year to do this, bar none,” he said.

AI training

The fracas began earlier this month, when the company first proposed new contractual language. The updated terms read:

This also includes the right for us and our third party partners and vendors to use Your Content to develop, train, test and improve artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies, including to improve the Services and/or help keep them safe, subject to any applicable opt-out rights described in our Privacy Policy.

As a result, a slew of vendors pulled up stakes, with some saying they had “burned their Etsy shop to the ground” or that they “just killed our Etsy store after 13 years.”

Etsy’s change is reminiscent of a recent dust-up involving Meta, where the tech giant had wanted to “require Meta Business account users to allow extensive AI training on external websites linked on Facebook.” Meta then indefinitely paused that move.

Last month, Etsy also told sellers that it would “set clearer restrictions on AI use,” despite the fact that AI-generated items remain for sale all over the site.

“Today, many active buyers say they can’t tell which items involve AI-generated content,” James Ossman, the company’s vice president of customer operations, wrote on October 22. “It’s become a top-three barrier to shopping more often on Etsy.”

For years, Etsy has been notoriously poor at enforcing its own rules, not only regarding AI but also the official prohibitions on selling sex toys, pornography, and even purported witchcraft spells.
