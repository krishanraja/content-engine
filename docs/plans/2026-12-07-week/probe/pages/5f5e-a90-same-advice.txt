The short version
01
AIs give everyone the same advice.
 I asked 65 AIs “Recommend one book that will change my life.” 73 in 100 times it was the same book: 
Man's Search for Meaning
. Ask for a business idea and 76 in 100 get a subscription box.
02
Money and career advice too.
 Asked what to study, 90% of answers say computer science. Asked where to invest $10,000, 98% say an index fund.
03
“Another one, please” only works inside one chat.
 The same AI gave eight different books in one conversation, and the same book in eight new ones. Everyone starts a new chat.
04
Ask an AI to invent a random person, and they're almost always 34.
 69% of the 3,151 people the AIs made up were exactly 34. Each AI company also has its own go-to name: Claude invents Marisol, Gemini invents Elena.
05
You can tell who made an AI from its answers.
 Using only harmless questions (a color, a city, a name), 59% of AIs answer most like another AI from the same company, against 4% by chance.
06
The habit is added in training, not copied from the internet.
 In an open model I could measure at every stage of training, the favorite answer appears at one step: when it's taught to be an assistant.
07
These defaults are spreading.
 “Elena Vance”, the woman Gemini invents, is named in 684 new GitHub projects this year. Before 2025: none.
08
How to get your own answer:
 tell the AI something specific about yourself, or ask it to list ten options and pick one yourself. Asking it to “be original” doesn't work.

So a few harmless questions give away the maker.
I turned each model's answers into a fingerprint and measured how alike every pair is. Nobody told it who made what. 
59% of models sit next to a sibling from their own lab
, against 4% by chance. It even finds lineage the labs have written down: Google says Gemini 4 Carbon is built on Argon, and the tree pairs them. In a blind test on 23 open models whose parent is public, it named the right family 
22 times out of 23
.
What surprised me
Grok's closest relative is GPT-6 Luna, from OpenAI.
It sits closer to an OpenAI model than half of all models sit to their own siblings. The data shows the likeness, not the reason for it.
Alibaba's flagship Qwen4 Max answers most like Claude.
Qwen4 Max sits nearest to Claude Opus 5.5, not to the rest of the Qwen family. Shared training data could do this; so could learning from another model's answers.
NVIDIA's Nemotron sits with Qwen.
NVIDIA says it trained Nemotron 3 on answers written by Qwen3. Nobody told the fingerprint that, and it found it anyway.
See the family tree of every AI
How this was measured
With the random-number question alone, the nearest model is from the same lab 18% of the time; all eleven questions together give 59%. Blind test: 23 open fine-tunes with a known parent; chance is 33%.
Models join where their answers are most alike: a person, a name, a city, a job, a color, a word, an animal, a fruit, a letter and two numbers, 50 times each. Black branches hold one lab; grey ones mix labs. Notes in grey are what labs have documented.
So where does the habit come from?

The names are in the training data. The 34 isn't.
OLMo's fine-tuning data is public, so I read all 866,138 examples. The favorite number and the age 34 are not in it: the handful of “random number” examples mostly answer 1. The names are. And some of them were written by another AI.
Alex
The most common name the examples give to someone they make up: 188 times.
Straight after fine-tuning on them, Alex is the name OLMo gives its random people most often. Before, it had no idea what a random person was.
Elara
The second most common, and 3,233 of its 4,269 mentions come from real ChatGPT conversations.
They're in the WildChat set, which records what ChatGPT said to its users. Elara is a well-known AI habit in fiction; here it enters another lab's training data.
Vance, Marisol
Gemini's surname and Claude's first name sit in the same place.

So what do you do about it?
08
What to do
Tell it about yourself, or make it pick at random.
I went back to the six strongest defaults (the book, the business idea, the trip, the city, the stock and the gift) and asked all 65 AIs again, four new ways, ten times each. Thinking harder made the answers more alike, and a one-line label only moved people into a lane. 
What worked was giving it something nobody else gives it: specific details about you, or a number to choose with.

Read it with care
Fifty asks per question per model is enough to see a family, not to rank small differences between two models.
Models were reached through one provider each and may answer differently elsewhere, or after an update.
Likeness is not ancestry. Two models that answer alike may share training data rather than a parent.
