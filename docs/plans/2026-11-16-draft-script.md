Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta will spend nearly three quarters of a trillion dollars this year building for AI. And in the last three months, they spent more on building than their businesses brought in.

These four are called hyperscalers. Everyone in a board meeting says that word now, but hardly anyone could tell you what one is. So I want to break it down, plainly. What is a hyperscaler, how does it make money, and where does it get the money to build all this?

It matters to you, because the AI tools your team pays for run inside their buildings. I'll take it in three parts. First, what a hyperscaler is and how it earns. Second, where the money comes from now. And third, why this month's US elections make that money harder to use.

Part one. A hyperscaler is a landlord for computers. Think of a landlord who builds a huge block of flats, except this block is full of computers. We call those buildings data centres.

Then the landlord rents that computing out to businesses like yours. You never see the building. You just pay for the AI tool, the email or the online storage. Somewhere, a data centre does the work.

Amazon, Microsoft and Google are the biggest landlords, and they rent their computers out to anyone. Meta, which owns Facebook and Instagram, mostly builds for itself, so it's its own tenant. And then there are newer, smaller landlords like Oracle, borrowing hard to catch up.

So how does a landlord for computers make money? It's simple. Build first, and collect the rent later. The building costs a fortune now, and the rent comes in slowly, often on contracts that run for years.

That's a fine business, as long as two things hold. You can pay for the building, and the tenants turn up when it opens.

Part two is where the money comes from. For years the answer was easy. These landlords had other businesses that threw off huge piles of spare cash. Google has its ads, Amazon has its online shop, and Microsoft has its office software. So a new data centre was paid for out of profits, and they hardly needed to borrow at all.

Now look at the last three months. Between them, the four spent more on building than their businesses brought in. Amazon, Google and Meta each came up short, and only Microsoft still had cash left over.

So where does the rest come from? Picture a receipt for the money behind one data centre, with a line for everyone who chipped in. I'll read it out.

Line one is the landlord's own profits. That's the old way, and it still pays the biggest share.

Line two is borrowing. The landlord sells bonds, which are loans from investors that it pays back with interest.

Line three is selling new shares. That brings in cash, but it hands a slice of the business to new owners.

Line four is partners. Another firm pays for the building, and the landlord rents it back. So the bill moves off the landlord's books, but the rent doesn't go away.

Line five is the chip maker Nvidia, which sells the chips that go inside these buildings. It's putting its own money into AI firms like OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT. Those firms then spend it renting more computing. So the chip maker is helping pay for its own customers.

And line six is the tenants themselves. Some big AI firms pay up front, before the building is even finished.

Now take just one line, the borrowing. By one count, new borrowing now pays for about one in three dollars of their building. Two years ago, it was about one in ten. So the build has quietly moved from spare cash to other people's money.

Part three is the votes. All of this is a bet that everything happens on time. The building goes up, it gets plugged into enough power, and the tenants move in and start paying.

But data centres use huge amounts of power. And lots of people now blame them, at least in part, for higher power bills. So in this month's midterm elections, lots of US towns and counties also voted on new data centres. One election tracker counted 41 of those local votes. In all but one, voters chose to limit new data centres.

Within days, OpenAI repeated its promise to pay the full cost of powering the data centres it runs. That tells you how loud the backlash got.

Now think like a landlord who borrowed to build. First, you have to win over the town, and that takes time. Then you're expected to pay for your own power, so it doesn't land on everyone else's bills. That's a new cost. And every month you wait is another month of interest on the loan. The rent comes late, but the loan payments come on time.

So who feels that first? Look at Oracle, the landlord with the most lines on the receipt. Right now it's selling new shares and borrowing more, both at once. The rating firms put it one step above junk, which is the grade where borrowing gets a lot dearer. Its biggest customers pay up front, and that helps. But Oracle itself doesn't expect to have spare cash again until the second half of 2028. So for Oracle, a late building really hurts, because it's borrowed money waiting on rent that hasn't started.

Here's what I'd take into your next board meeting. Nearly three quarters of a trillion dollars is going into AI buildings this year. About one in three of those dollars is now borrowed. And in all but one of those local votes, people chose to limit new data centres. Then ask one question. Which landlord does our AI run on, and does it pay from profits or borrow to build?

So what do I think happens? I think the money side of AI still changes, but it won't change as fast as promised. And the US economy is tied up with it, so the same goes there. The hyperscalers just won't get as free a hand as they had before.

That has a few knock-on effects. Each new building costs more and opens later. It has to win the town and pay for its own power. Lenders already charge the weakest landlords more, so the ones that borrow to build feel it first. If the buildings open later, there's less spare computing to go round. So AI may not get cheaper as fast as the sellers say. And I think a lot of US growth now rides on this build. So a slower build means slower growth too.

If I ran a business, I'd ask each AI supplier which landlord it runs on. If that landlord borrows to build, I wouldn't plan on my AI bill coming down any time soon.
