Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta will spend nearly three quarters of a trillion dollars this year building for AI. And in the last three months, they spent more on building than all the spare cash they made between them. So who's paying for all this?

It matters to you, because the AI tools your team pays for run inside their data centres. These four are called hyperscalers, and you'll hear that word in every board meeting now. So I want to break it down plainly. What is a hyperscaler, how does it make money, and who pays to build it?

I'll take it in three parts. First, what a hyperscaler is and how it earns. Second, where the money comes from now. And third, why this month's US elections make building slower and dearer.

Part one. A hyperscaler is like a landlord, but what it rents out is computing power. Think of a landlord who builds a huge block of flats, except this block is full of computers. Those buildings are the data centres you hear about.

The tenants are businesses, and usually you don't rent from the landlord yourself. You pay a software firm for the AI tool, the email or the online storage. That firm rents the computing from a landlord. So part of what you pay is that rent.

Amazon, Microsoft and Google are the biggest landlords, and they rent their computing out to anyone. Meta, which owns Facebook and Instagram, mostly builds for itself, so it's landlord and tenant at once. Oracle isn't one of the big four. You might know it as an old business software firm. Now it's racing to become a landlord too, and borrowing hard to catch up.

So how does a landlord for computing make money? Build first, and collect the rent later. The building costs a fortune now, and the rent comes in slowly, often on contracts that run for years. That works fine, as long as the building gets paid for and the tenants turn up when it opens.

Part two is where the money comes from. For years the answer was easy. These landlords had other businesses that made huge piles of spare cash. Google and Meta have their ads, Amazon has its online shop, and Microsoft has its office software. So a new data centre was paid for out of profits, and they hardly needed to borrow at all.

Now look at the last three months. Amazon, Google and Meta each spent more on building than the spare cash their businesses made. Only Microsoft still had cash left over.

So where does the rest come from? Picture the bill for one data centre, and next to it, a list of who chipped in to pay it. There are six lines, and I'll read them out.

Line one is the landlord's own profits. That's the old way, and it still pays the biggest share.

Line two is borrowing. The landlord sells bonds, which are loans from investors that it pays back with interest.

Line three is selling new shares. That brings in cash, but it hands a slice of the business to new owners.

Line four is partners. A partner pays for the building, and the landlord rents it from them. So the landlord skips the big bill, but signs up for years of rent instead.

Line five is the chip maker Nvidia, which sells the chips that go inside these buildings. It's putting its own money into AI firms like OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT. Those firms then spend that money renting computing from the landlords. So the chip maker is helping pay the rent that pays for the building.

And line six is the tenants themselves. Some big AI firms pay up front, before the building is even finished.

Now take just one of those lines, the borrowing. By one count, about one in every three dollars they spend on building now comes from new borrowing. Two years ago, it was about one in ten. So more and more of this build runs on other people's money.

Part three is the votes. All of this is a bet that everything happens on time. The building goes up, it gets plugged into enough power, and the tenants move in and start paying.

But data centres use huge amounts of power. And many people now blame them, at least in part, for higher power bills. So in this month's US midterm elections, many towns and counties also voted on new data centres. One election tracker counted 41 of those local votes. In all but one, voters chose to limit new data centres.

Within days, OpenAI repeated its promise to pay the full power bill for the data centres it builds.

Now think like a landlord who borrowed to build. First, you have to win the town's approval, and that takes time. Then you're being pushed to pay for your own power, so it doesn't land on everyone else's bills. And every month you wait is another month of interest on the loan. The rent comes late, but the loan payments come on time. In the end, those extra costs end up in the rent. And the rent is part of what you pay for AI.

So who feels it first? Look at Oracle, the landlord leaning hardest on other people's money. This month it sold new shares and borrowed more, both at once. The firms that rate loans put Oracle one step above junk. That's the grade where borrowing starts to cost a lot more. Its biggest customers pay up front, and that helps. But at two of its own sites, people have packed town meetings to oppose them. And Oracle doesn't expect to have spare cash again until its 2028 financial year. So every month an Oracle building sits late is another month of interest, waiting on the rest of the rent.

So what do I think happens? I think the big changes AI promises still come, just slower than the AI firms say. And so much of US growth rides on this build that a slower build slows that down too. The hyperscalers just won't get to build as freely as before.

That has a few knock-on effects. Each new building costs more and opens later. Lenders already charge weaker landlords like Oracle more, so the ones that borrow to build feel it first. And if the buildings open later, there's less spare computing to go round. So AI may not get cheaper as fast as people hope.

So if you take three numbers into your next board meeting, make it these. Nearly three quarters of a trillion dollars is going into AI buildings this year. About one in three dollars they spend on building is now borrowed. And in all but one of those local votes, people chose to limit new data centres.

If I were buying AI for a team this year, I'd ask each supplier whose data centres it runs on. And I'd budget for that bill to stay flat, not fall.
