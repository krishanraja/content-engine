Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta will spend nearly three quarters of a trillion dollars this year building for AI. And last quarter, between them, they spent more on building than all the spare cash their businesses made.

These four are called hyperscalers. Everyone uses that word now, but few people could tell you what one is. So what is a hyperscaler, and where does it get all that money? It matters to you, because the AI tools your team pays for run in their data centres. I'll take it in three parts. First, what a hyperscaler is. Second, where its money comes from. And third, why this month's US elections made that money harder to use.

Part one. A hyperscaler is a landlord for computers. It builds giant buildings full of computers, called data centres. Then it rents that computing out to businesses like yours, often on contracts that run for years. Amazon, Microsoft and Google rent theirs out. Meta mostly builds for itself, so it's its own tenant. And Oracle is a newer, smaller landlord, borrowing hard to catch up.

So the business is simple. Build first, and collect the rent later. That works fine, as long as you can pay for the building.

Part two is where the money comes from. For years, the answer was easy. The landlords paid out of their other businesses, like ads, online shopping and office software. Those made so much spare cash that a data centre was pocket money. Back in 2024, they hardly needed to borrow at all.

Now look at the bill. Last quarter, Amazon, Google and Meta each spent more on building than their spare cash. Only Microsoft still had cash left over.

So where's the rest coming from? Picture a receipt for the money behind one data centre, with a line for everyone who chipped in. Line one is the landlord's own profits. Line two is borrowing, by selling bonds to investors. Line three is selling new shares. Line four is partners, who pay for the building and rent it back to the landlord. Line five is the chip maker Nvidia, putting its own money into AI firms so they can rent more computing. And line six is those AI firms themselves, paying up front.

Add it all up, and by one estimate four in every ten dollars now come from somewhere other than profits. A couple of years ago, that was hardly anything.

And no landlord has more lines on that receipt than Oracle. It's selling new shares and borrowing more at the same time. The rating firms put it one step above junk, the grade where borrowing gets a lot dearer. Its biggest customers pay up front, which helps. But Oracle doesn't expect to have spare cash again until the second half of 2028.

Part three is the votes. All of this is a bet that the buildings go up on time, get plugged in, and fill with renters. But data centres use huge amounts of power, and people have started blaming them for higher power bills. So in this month's midterm elections, many US towns also voted on whether to allow new ones. One election tracker counted 41 of those votes, and all but one went against the data centres. Within days, OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, promised to pay the full cost of powering its own data centres.

So think like a landlord who borrowed to build. First you have to win over the town. More and more, you're also expected to pay for your own power, instead of it landing on everyone's bills. And every month you wait is a month of interest, before any rent comes in. The rent arrives late, but the loan payments arrive on time.

So here's what I'd take into your next board meeting. Take three numbers with you. Nearly three quarters of a trillion dollars is going into AI buildings this year. About four in ten of those dollars come from somewhere other than profits. And all but one of those local votes went against new data centres.

So what do I think happens? I think the money side of AI still changes, just not as fast as promised. Each new building will cost more and open later. Lenders already charge the weakest landlords more, so the borrowers feel it first. If buildings open later, there's less spare computing to go round, and the extra costs get passed on. So the AI tools you pay for may not get cheaper as fast as the sellers say. And because so much of US growth now rides on this build, a slower build means slower growth too.

If I ran a business, I'd ask each AI supplier which landlord its tools run on. If that landlord borrows to build, I wouldn't budget for my AI bill coming down any time soon.
