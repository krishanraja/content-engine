You've probably danced to Movin' To The Sun this autumn, maybe at a wedding or under someone's holiday video. It reached No. 2 in the UK and spent 19 weeks on the Official Charts. Its first version came out of an AI music app, made by someone who had never written a song.

We went through the credits, the producers' own accounts and the Grammys' ruling to work out whether it's an AI song or a human one. It's both, at every step, and that's worth knowing well beyond music.

## THE SONGS YOU KNOW

Two songs carry this story. Movin' To The Sun is now credited to HUGEL, Imael Angel and Ultra Naté. Blikk, a Hungarian paper, reports it led Beatport's global dance chart for nearly 90 days (a record, by its count) and says Imael Angel's songs have passed 113 million plays on Spotify. We haven't seen either number confirmed anywhere else, so treat them as reported.

Bad Times is the other one. Its charting version peaked at No. 67 in the UK, and it went to No. 1 on Shazam in Greece.

How people found them matters more than the numbers. Most listeners met these songs as background audio on short videos, under clips of cats or a father-daughter dance, and then went looking for them. In the Reddit threads we read, about a third of the mentions are people asking some version of "what's this song?"

So AI is already in the mainstream. It got there inside songs people chose to play again.

## WHO WROTE IT

Imael Angel is the stage name of Bernadett Gango, a Hungarian-born Reiki master who lives in England. She's also written children's books. Music was never her field.

Her method is simple. She writes the lyrics and the idea, then puts them into Suno or Udio, apps that turn words and a chosen style into a finished song in minutes. Everything comes out of the app, right down to the singing voice. So the first versions of her songs are sung by an AI voice that belongs to nobody. This summer she set up a company for the music, IMAEL ANGEL RECORDS LTD, registered at Companies House on 16 July 2026 (quite a side project for a Reiki master).

Until very recently, someone with no band and no contacts had almost no way into the charts. A demo needed a producer and a singer, and money to pay them, before a stranger ever heard it. These apps took that first wall away. Gango's songs reached millions of people before a single professional had touched them.

That's the bit people tend to skip. What AI did here was let someone in who'd never have been in the room.

## THE PEOPLE WHO FINISHED IT

Getting a song onto the radio is a different job, and people did it.

HUGEL is a DJ and producer. He heard Movin' To The Sun and rebuilt it. He told the dance site We Rave You in September that he programmed the parts by hand and rebuilt the instruments, then brought in the house singer Ultra Naté, who is 58, to sing it fresh. By his account, the only AI left in the finished track is a layer of Angel's original AI vocal sitting under Naté's. His team has called the record "about 95% human", a figure DJ Mag France points out nobody has checked independently.

Bad Times went the same way. Its credits on Qobuz show the charting version re-sung by a human singer, Cali Bee, reproduced by Robin Brandes, and released under exclusive licence to Universal Music. MistaJam, the broadcaster, described it as "completely reproduced and re-vocaled by humans".

Follow either song and you get the same steps. Someone has an idea and an app makes the demo. Millions of short videos carry it, and viewers hunt it down on Shazam. A producer rebuilds it, a singer records it, and a major label puts it on the radio. Take any one step away and there's no hit.

## BOTH AT ONCE

On 17 October, The Australian reported how the Recording Academy, which runs the Grammys, had ruled on Movin' To The Sun. It's barred from the songwriting categories, because the released version still uses material from Angel's Suno original. It's eligible for the performance categories, because a human performs it.

Read that again. One recording counts as AI for one prize and as human for another, at the same time. People whose whole job is judging music looked at this song and gave two answers, and both of them hold.

Listeners had settled it already. Under Ultra Naté's own video of the song, comments that mention AI make up 5.6% of the total and draw 0.8% of the likes. Most of the top comments are about memory: one listener wants to "cry and dance like I was 18 again". Under Bad Times, someone wrote "Who cares about AI I love this compo". Over on Reddit, a fan in r/EDM reckoned the HUGEL version is "not 0% AI", which is about as precise as anyone has got.

Nominations come out on 16 November, so we'll know soon whether a song that started in an app can still win a prize for the people who sang and rebuilt it.

## BACK ON THE DANCE FLOOR

Asking whether this is AI music or human music stops working once you look closely. These songs are both at every layer: a person's idea, an AI voice, a human rebuild, a human singer and a label's contract. Even the Grammys couldn't fit this one into a single box.

That pattern reaches well beyond music. The advert your team signs off and the report on your desk are heading the same way, with AI doing some steps and people doing others. Asking whether something was made by AI tells you less every month. Asking which step AI did, and who is named for each step, tells you what to check and who answers for it. Music just happens to be the first industry working that out in public, with the awards and the labels writing their rules as they go.

But the bigger change is who gets in. Gango had ideas and, until now, no way to be heard. A first draft now costs a monthly subscription, and the rest still needs people with real craft. Expect more hits, adverts and products that start with someone the old route would have kept out. We think another UK top-ten single will be shown to have started as a Suno or Udio demo by 30 June 2027, and we'd put that at 75%. Two songs took this route in a single autumn, and the steps are now public for anyone to copy.

The song you can't stop dancing to is already both, and that's how music gets made now.
