# A Reiki master wrote a UK number two, and it's both AI and human

## The song you already know

If you've opened TikTok or Instagram this year, you've heard Movin' To The Sun. It plays under cats in dresses and dads dancing badly in parked cars. On the Official Charts it reached number two in the UK and stayed on the singles chart for months, credited to HUGEL, Imael Angel and Ultra Naté.

Most people dancing to it never wondered where it came from. Fair enough. The ones who did kept typing the same thing into the comments, some version of "is this AI or real?"

We went looking for the answer, and we think the question has stopped working. This song is both, at every layer. Once we'd traced how it was put together, we started seeing the same pattern well beyond music.

## Who wrote it

Imael Angel is the stage name of Bernadett Gango, a Hungarian-born Reiki master living in England. Bands and studios were never part of her life.

She had lyrics and an AI music app. Suno works like this: you type your lyrics into one box, describe the style you want in another, and get back a finished song with a singer on it. (If you don't like the second verse, you don't rewrite it. You generate again.) Her early catalogue was made with Suno and Udio, so the first voices on her songs were generated by AI.

That's the part people struggle with. We think it's the real story, too: without studio time or years of learning the software, someone with her background had almost no route to a chart hit. In our view, AI tools moved that gate, and a finished demo from an app got her through it.

## How a demo became a hit

A demo on its own goes nowhere. Strangers did the next bit. Lots of them.

What we saw, scrolling the clips and threads ourselves, was people hearing it behind someone else's video and going hunting. In the comments and the Reddit threads we read, a lot of people were simply trying to find out what the song was (after hearing it under a cat video, say, or a father-daughter dance).

As we read it, short videos came first and the charts followed, after strangers had already decided they liked it.

That order matters more than it used to, because music has never been more crowded. Deezer, the streaming service, said in July 2026 that AI-made tracks had passed 50% of the new music uploaded to it every day. Its April report put the share of listening those tracks actually get at between 1% and 3%. Making a song isn't the hard part any more. Getting a stranger to go looking for it is. This one got found.

## The people who finished it

HUGEL, a DJ and producer, didn't just re-release her AI song. He sampled pieces of her version and rearranged the track from there, with a new drop. His own list of what went in: drums from stock and Splice sample packs, a Serum bass preset layered with a Moog line, an extra piano and a string layer for tension.

Even that "human" version isn't as human as it sounds. Drums from sample packs are recordings anyone can buy and reuse, and a bass preset is a sound someone else designed. We'd call that normal for dance music, and it was normal long before AI turned up.

Next HUGEL brought in Ultra Naté, the American house singer best known for her 90s hit "Free". She sang the new version. Properly. Under her voice, in a few places, he kept a thin layer of the original AI singer. We love this detail: the hit you've been dancing to has a robot humming along under a dance legend.

In HUGEL's own words, "The AI was just used to do the layer of the vocal." By his account, then, the record isn't fully AI, and it isn't fully human either.

## Both at once

The either-or question falls apart in public at the Grammys.

They give one prize for writing a song and another for performing it. In September, reports said the Recording Academy had removed the track from Grammy consideration over its AI involvement, and HUGEL challenged that, pointing to his project files. Then on 16 October, The Australian reported a more specific ruling: the song was ineligible in songwriting categories because it incorporated material from Imael Angel's AI-generated original, while HUGEL and Ultra Naté could still be eligible in performance categories.

Sit with that for a second (we did). The same recording is out for the writing and still in for the singing. The organisation whose whole job is judging music couldn't file this song under one word, because no single word fits it.

Count the layers and you see why. Her words and an AI app's first version and voice sit at the bottom. On top of those come a producer's rebuild, mostly from samples and presets, and a legend's vocal, with a trace of the AI voice still underneath.

## Back on the dance floor

We'd stop asking whether it's AI music or human music. The song you can't stop dancing to is already both, and that's how music gets made now.

That shift is worth carrying out of this story, because the pattern isn't confined to music. The advert you scrolled past this morning and the deck your team sent last night are increasingly made the same way. A person has the idea, a machine does a first draft, and people rebuild and finish it, with some of the machine's work still in there. "Did AI make this?" tells you very little about any of them. It's a yes-or-no question about something that isn't yes or no any more.

Who did which bit tells you far more. Much more. Whose idea it was, what the tool made and who put their name to it are the answers that say how much to trust a thing and who to call when it goes wrong.

This story also shows who gets in. As we see it, a Reiki master with no studio got a UK number two because AI got her as far as a finished demo and people carried it the rest of the way. We think that door is opening in every field, yours included, and the people coming through it won't look like the ones who came before.

Next time a song gets stuck in your head, or work lands on your desk that's better than you expected, ask who did which bit. Almost every time, the answer will be both. Every layer.

## OUR PREDICTION

HUGEL's challenge won't change the split. By 10 February 2027, Movin' To The Sun will still be out of the Grammys' songwriting categories, even if it gets nominated for performance. How sure we are: 85%.
