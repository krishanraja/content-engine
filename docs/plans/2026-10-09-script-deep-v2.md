I've been watching what's coming with AI, before everyone notices. I'm Krish.

I use AI apps every day. Claude, ChatGPT, all of them. And I've noticed something odd about how they're sold. Every launch is about a name. A new number, a livestream, a chart, a week of people arguing online. So I wanted to break it down properly. Does the name you pick actually change the answer you get?

Let me start by saying this. Some of the new AIs really are better. The people building them aren't lying to you. But I think the name is the least important thing on your screen.

The setting that changes your answer most has a boring label. It starts low, unless you change it. And almost nobody touches it.

So let's start with the names.

Open Claude Code, which is Anthropic's coding app. Its help page lists more than a dozen AIs you can pick by name. Open ChatGPT and you'll find GPT-6. Under the hood that's powered by something called GPT-6 Sol. And there's GPT-6 Astra behind the Pro option. That's a lot of names.

Each name is built for a launch day. It gets a headline. It gets a chart showing it beat the last one. And it gets a week of people online picking sides.

Here's the funny part. Even the people choosing the names think it's got out of hand. In February 2025, Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, said they hate the picker too. Those were nearly his words. Then in April 2025 he promised to fix the naming by that summer. And he said people could keep making fun of them until then. He said they deserved it.

So the name gets the headline. But a headline isn't an answer. The question that matters is how much the name changes what you actually get back. And for most of what people ask, I don't think it changes much at all.

Here's why. Most people don't use AI the way the launches imagine.

OpenAI and economists from Harvard studied one and a half million ChatGPT chats. Nearly eight in ten were people asking for practical help, looking something up, or getting help writing. About half of all messages were simply people asking something. Only about four in a hundred were about computer code.

And by July 2025, people were sending more than two and a half billion messages a day.

That's not a lab. That's a search box. People type a question, the way they'd type it into Google, and they read what comes back.

And people asking like that don't open menus. Here's the number that surprised me most.

In August 2025, OpenAI launched GPT-5, and the app started choosing for people when to think harder. Afterwards, Altman shared what happened. Take free users. Before, under one in a hundred used a thinking AI each day. Afterwards, seven in a hundred did. Among paying users, it went from seven to twenty-four.

Think about that. The option had been sitting in the menu all along. Hardly anyone had pressed it.

So for an everyday question, which AI answers barely matters. What would have mattered was the setting nobody touched.

So what is that setting? Let me explain with one person.

Picture a woman who runs a small business. Call her Priya. She pastes a supplier contract into her AI app, and asks what she should worry about. In four seconds, it gives her a tidy answer. It sounds sure. She signs.

Six months later, a clause bites her. A careful reader would have caught it.

Now, nothing was wrong with the name at the top of her screen. What was missing is simpler. The AI never stopped to think.

ChatGPT and Claude now both let you choose how hard the AI thinks. At the low end, it answers straight away. Turn it up, and it works through the problem first. It checks itself before it replies.

Here's the picture I keep in my head. The name is the badge on a car. The thinking setting is the gear you're in. We spend all our time arguing about badges. And most of us drive everywhere in first gear.

So how big is the gap? OpenAI tested its AIs on a tough set of medical questions. The older GPT-4o scored zero. The new GPT-5, answering without thinking, scored about twenty-five out of a hundred. The very same GPT-5, thinking first, scored about forty-six.

Same name on the box. Nearly double the score, just from stopping to think.

Anthropic says something similar about Claude. The more it's allowed to think, the more maths questions it gets right. Each extra bit of thinking helps a little less than the last. But it keeps helping.

And we've seen what happens when the switch breaks. Soon after GPT-5 launched, the part that decided when to think stopped working for a while. Altman said GPT-5 seemed way dumber. Same name. Same app. Worse answers.

Now, to be fair to the names, they're not nothing. When OpenAI took away an older AI, GPT-4o, lots of people missed its personality. They pushed back hard, and OpenAI brought it back. But when OpenAI later retired it for good, it said something telling. Only one user in a thousand still chose it each day. People missed it. Very few actually went back to it.

So who decides how hard your AI thinks? I went looking, and my answer is this. Mostly the app does, through the setting it starts you on.

In 2025, OpenAI's answer was a router. That's a hidden part of ChatGPT that reads your question and decides which AI answers it. OpenAI says the router learns from real signals. And when you hit your usage limit, a smaller AI takes over.

Then, in September 2026, OpenAI changed things for its paying users. It stopped switching up to thinking on its own. Now the picker is literally a slider for thinking. Instant, Medium, High, Extra High.

On the Plus and Pro plans, OpenAI's help page says Instant won't step up on its own. Even if you ask it to think harder. So the choice is back with you. And Instant, the quickest level, is where you start.

Claude works in a similar way. Each AI has an effort level marked as the one it starts on. And Claude's help page is honest about the trade. Higher effort means a more thorough answer, but you'll hit your usage limit faster.

That's the real reason the starting setting matters. Thinking uses more of the AI's work. That's why it eats your limit faster. My read is that it costs OpenAI and Anthropic more to run, too. So the quick setting is the one you get, unless you go looking.

And remember the number from earlier. Among free users, daily use of thinking had started from under one in a hundred. People don't go looking.

I don't think that's a plot. It's just what starting settings do. Most of us never change them.

So what should you actually do?

For quick, everyday stuff, leave it alone. A recipe, a summary of an email, a quick fact. The fast setting is fine, and the name really doesn't matter.

For anything you'll act on, change the gear. A contract. A medical question. Money. A decision you can't take back.

On ChatGPT, pick Medium or High. On Claude, turn the effort up. On ChatGPT's free plan, use the Think option. It costs you a few more seconds, and you'll hit your limit sooner. That's the trade. And for the questions that matter, it's worth it.

If you build products on AI, here's my advice. Don't choose an AI by its name or its launch chart. Take your own hardest questions. Run them through each setting. Check the answers yourself. And pay for thinking only where it changes the result.

There's also a better way the apps could do this. Under every answer, they could show you whether the AI actually stopped to think. A small label, like the gear on a car's dashboard. Then you'd know when to ask again, with the gear up.

So here's my bet. By the end of 2027, the main picker in ChatGPT for paying users won't show any AI names at all. Just how hard it should think. I'd be wrong if the next big launch needs a name on the button to sell it.

We've spent two years learning their names. The thing worth learning was where they hid the dial.
