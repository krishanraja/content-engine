# Narrative lock: Wed 18 Nov under.the.hood - Everyone thinks they can vibe code their way out of the software bill

piece_id: db9f9f6a-1092-49be-8024-f335e060305a
Publishes: 2026-11-18

## Surface story
Starbucks spends about $400 million a year on software, and Bloomberg reports it is building AI tools to replace a Microsoft inventory system and an IBM maintenance tool. Retool, which sells tools for building, says 35% of the teams it surveyed have already swapped at least one software subscription for something they built, mostly workflow automations, admin tools and dashboards. McKinsey says 32% of firms skipped buying at least one product or feature because they could build it with AI. And every company now has a Joe from engineering who is sure he can build the CRM over a weekend.

## Deeper story
AI made writing software cheap. It did nothing to the cost of owning it, and owning is most of the bill. Work Starbucks' own move through with sample numbers and grant the build is far cheaper than the licence: the first year still costs about the same as just paying, because of everything the subscription was quietly paying for, and the saving only starts in year two, if nothing goes wrong. Starbucks may well be right to do it: it is big enough to pay a team to look after its own tools. Most businesses getting overexcited by their confident vibe-coding engineer are not. Put build and buy on trial and both sides have a real case for and a real case against. The verdict: the line between build and buy moved, and it moved to what you are willing, and able, to look after. Industry commentary, with one light line from Krish: he knows for a fact that maintaining your own tools takes a lot of energy.

## Arc
- Hook: Starbucks spends about $400 million a year on software, and it's building AI tools that could replace some of it.
- The excitement: a third of the teams Retool asked have already swapped a subscription for something they built. Theo says AI made the best engineers 100 times better than the average. A reply sums up the mood: companies think their internal engineer can rebuild HubSpot, "Joe from engineering with the confidence of an alligator hunter".
- So let's put it on trial, using Starbucks as the case. Sample numbers, said as sample numbers.
- Why Starbucks is doing it: it is cutting $2 billion in costs, it is reviewing "every contract and service" in technology, and its CTO says "There's clear opportunities to reduce the spend in software."
- The case for build: say the maintenance tool costs $8 million a year. With AI, six engineers build a replacement in nine months for under $1 million. On paper that is a steal.
- The case against build: the receipt. Looking after it: a team of eight, security checks, hosting and backups, keeping it talking to the other systems, about $3 million a year. Switching over: six months of paying for both, $4 million, and training store managers. Year one comes to about $8.3 million, about the same as just paying the $8 million. Over five years, writing it is less than a twentieth of what it costs. When nobody pays for the looking after, it shows: an AI agent deleted a live company database during a code freeze; a flaw in an AI app builder let strangers into private company apps, HR ones included. Krish's one line: he knows for a fact that looking after your own tools takes a lot of energy.
- The case for buy: the vendor carries the looking after, the audits and the blame. Even the famous "we shut down Salesforce" story moved its HR system to another subscription.
- The case against buy: the bill keeps changing shape. Vendors are adding pay-per-use credits on top of seats, and in HubSpot's own trials customers used two to three times more credits. You rent the roadmap, and you wait on their support.
- The verdict: from year two, Starbucks saves about $5 million a year on our sample numbers, if nothing goes wrong, and it is big enough to pay the team that makes sure. Or it shows IBM what it built and asks for a quarter off: $2 million a year, from day one, with nothing to look after. Writing got cheap, looking after didn't. The line between build and buy now sits at what you are willing, and able, to look after.
- The three questions to ask before you build the next part of your stack (The use, below), each shown on customer service, productivity and marketing tools.
- His close.

## The fun
A trial, told tongue in cheek, with Starbucks in the dock. Joe from engineering is the star witness, confident to the end. Build and buy each take the stand twice, once for and once against, and each case brings one exhibit. The exhibit for the case against build is an itemised receipt with the sample numbers on it: the build line reads "Writing it: under $1 million", then the receipt keeps printing (the team of eight, security checks, hosting and backups, keeping it connected, six months of paying for both, training) until the total lands next to IBM's $8 million and nearly matches it. Every amount on screen is marked as a sample number. The verdict is three questions anyone can use. Every line is said the plain way you would say it to a friend; no clever phrasing (Krish, 9 Nov: "It's really important we aren't trying to cryptically confuse everyone.").

## The use
Three questions to ask yourself when someone asks whether to build or buy the next part of your tech stack (Krish: "three questions to ask themselves when they're asked whether they should build versus buy the next part of their technology stack (focus on CX and productivity tech and martech as examples)"):
- Who looks after it when it breaks at 2am? If nobody has their name on it, buy. Customer service example: a bot that answers customers overnight needs someone awake when it goes wrong.
- Does it hold customer data, or does it have to pass an audit? If yes, buy. Marketing example: the email platform that holds your customers' consent records. A dashboard built on data you already own is fair game to build.
- Does it make you different, or does everyone do it the same way? Build what is yours, buy what is the same everywhere. Productivity example: an approval flow shaped around how your team works is yours to build; logins, email and calendars are the same everywhere, so buy them.
New against recent pieces: the last three locks gave "ask your AI supplier where its new computing is being built", the Amazon AI-usage actions and no action; none is a build-or-buy test.

## His close
His own line, on camera, in his words, written once the piece is built. The test he set: "the closing line should leave them clearer on how to think about the options than they were 5 minutes before". Working line until then: Buy what holds your customers and passes your audits. Build the small things on top that make your team faster. And before you build anything, name who looks after it.

## Outline
Spine: Now that AI can write software, should you build the next tool or keep buying it?
Answer: Writing got cheap and looking after didn't, so build only what you are willing and able to look after, and three questions tell you which is which.
1. THE $400 MILLION BILL | proves: big companies really are trying to build their way out of software spend, and Joe from engineering is sure he can do it
2. WHY STARBUCKS IS BUILDING | proves: the reasons are real (a $2 billion cost cut, every contract under review) and the build itself is far cheaper than the licence
3. THE RECEIPT | proves: on sample numbers the looking after and the switch make year one cost about the same as buying, and home-built apps break when nobody looks after them
4. BUY ON TRIAL | proves: buying pays someone else to look after it, and the bill keeps changing shape
5. THE VERDICT | proves: building pays only from year two and only for those who can pay the team, while the threat of building wins a discount from day one
6. THREE QUESTIONS | proves: anyone can sort their next tool with who looks after it, the data and audit test, and what makes them different
