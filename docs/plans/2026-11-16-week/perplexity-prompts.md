## 1. Monday (follow.the.money): where does the money to build AI actually come from?

Your words: "a broader piece about where, in general, money comes from that tech companies get to build AI? The output of this article should be devastatingly simple and insightful even though it's pulling in a lot of complexity of insight."

```
I am researching one question for a business audience: where does the money that technology companies spend building AI actually come from, and who carries the risk if the demand arrives late?

Cover the period January 2025 to today. For the largest builders (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Oracle, OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, CoreWeave and any other company spending more than $10 billion a year on AI data centres, chips or power), find how each one funds that spending. Sort every source of money into one of these buckets, and add a bucket if the evidence needs one:

1. Cash from the existing business (operating cash flow from ads, cloud, software).
2. New debt: bond issues, bank loans, term loans. Give amounts, dates, interest rates and credit ratings where public.
3. New shares: equity raises, share sales, venture and private rounds, sovereign wealth funds.
4. Off-balance-sheet structures: special purpose vehicles, joint ventures, private credit deals, sale-and-leaseback of data centres.
5. Supplier money: chipmakers or cloud providers investing in customers who then buy from them (circular deals). Name each deal, its size and which way the money flows.
6. Customer money: prepayments, take-or-pay contracts, multi-year commitments, customers buying their own hardware.
7. Government money: subsidies, tax credits, public loans, state-backed power deals.

For each company give: total AI-related capital spending for the latest year or quarter it reports, the split across the buckets above as far as public filings allow, and the single most important number. Then answer:

- What share of all AI building money now comes from debt and outside investors, rather than from the companies' own cash, and how has that changed since 2024?
- Which deals are circular (money that leaves a supplier and comes back as a purchase)? Estimate how much of reported AI revenue they account for.
- Who is left holding the bill if usage grows more slowly than planned: bondholders, shareholders, customers who prepaid, chipmakers, governments, or electricity ratepayers?
- What dates matter most: when debts fall due, when promised profits are supposed to arrive, when contracts can be cancelled?
- What do the strongest sceptics (credit analysts, short sellers, ratings agencies) and the strongest believers each say? Quote them with dates.

Rules for the evidence:
- Prefer primary sources: company filings (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, S-1, bond prospectuses), earnings call transcripts, ratings agency notes, central bank and IMF reports. Use press only to point to a primary source, and say so when no primary source exists.
- Give every number with its date, its unit, and a link to the page that shows it. Quote the exact sentence.
- Mark each claim CONFIRMED (seen on a primary page), REPORTED (press only) or ESTIMATE (an analyst's or your own calculation, showing the working).
- Do not round or combine numbers from different dates without saying so.

Finish with: one table of every company by funding bucket; a list of the ten most surprising facts, each with its source; and a five-step plain-English chain that explains where the money comes from to someone who has never read a balance sheet.
```

## 2. Wednesday (under.the.hood): can you really vibe code your way out of the software bill?

Your pick (wed-2), tongue in cheek, both sides: the case for building with AI against the case for buying, and what the subscription was quietly paying for.

```
I am researching one question for business leaders: now that AI can write software, are companies actually replacing the software they pay for with tools they build themselves, and does it save money once you count everything?

Cover January 2025 to today. Find:

1. Real companies that have replaced a paid software product (CRM, HR, finance, project management, internal tools, analytics) with something built in-house using AI coding tools. For each: who, what they replaced, when, what they said it saved, and whether there is any follow-up showing it held up, was reversed, or caused problems. Include the cases that are widely quoted (for example any claims by Klarna, Starbucks or others) and check each one against what the company itself said.
2. Surveys and data on how many companies are doing this and for which kinds of software. Say who ran each survey, whether they sell building tools (and so have an interest), the sample size and the date.
3. What owning software really costs after it is written: maintenance, security patching, hosting, compliance and audit, upgrades, on-call support, and the people who understand it. Find studies on what share of a system's lifetime cost comes after the first version, and anything specific to AI-written code (bugs, security flaws, code nobody on staff understands).
4. Incidents: data leaks, outages or security problems traced to AI-written or "vibe coded" internal apps. Dated and sourced.
5. How software vendors are responding: price changes, moves from per-seat to usage pricing, falling seat counts, vendors adding their own AI building tools, and what their earnings calls say about customers building instead of buying.
6. What is myth and what is real: separate claims backed by numbers from claims that are only stated.

Rules for the evidence:
- Prefer primary sources: company statements, filings, earnings call transcripts, the survey's own report, security researchers' own write-ups. Use press only to point to a primary source, and say so when none exists.
- Give every number with its date and a link to the page that shows it, and quote the exact sentence.
- Mark each claim CONFIRMED (seen on a primary page), REPORTED (press only) or ESTIMATE (show the working).

Finish with: a table of every real replacement case with its outcome; a short list of what the software subscription was quietly paying for that a home-built tool must now cover; the five most surprising facts with sources; and a plain-English verdict on where the line between build and buy now sits for a company of 200 to 2,000 people.
```
