# Who Pays for the AI Build-Out: Funding Sources and Risk Holders, January 2025 – November 2026

Most of the money for AI building still comes from the existing businesses of Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta. That share is falling fast. By my estimate, about 35–45% of the AI capital spending planned for 2026 by the largest builders is paid for with new debt, new shares, off-balance-sheet vehicles and venture money. In 2024 the figure was under 10%. If demand arrives late, the bill falls first on Oracle and CoreWeave creditors and shareholders, then on Nvidia as investor and guarantor. Electricity ratepayers on the PJM grid are already paying.

## TL;DR

- **Funding has shifted from own cash to outside money.** In Q3 2026, three of the four largest hyperscalers spent more than their operating cash flow. Alphabet's Q3 free cash flow was −$9.065B, Meta's −$2.12B and Amazon's trailing twelve-month figure −$42.4B. Amazon borrowed $135.8B gross in nine months, Alphabet set out an $80B equity raise, and Meta launched about $35B of new stock. Microsoft is the only one of the four still funding itself; its fiscal Q1 2027 free cash flow was +$18.6B.
- **Circular money is a small share of today's revenue and a large share of the backlog.** About 5% of the AI revenue booked across the chain in 2026 is cash that a supplier first invested in a customer (ESTIMATE). The share is far higher in contracted backlog at Oracle and CoreWeave, because their biggest customers, OpenAI and the other labs, are funded largely by Amazon, Nvidia, Google, Microsoft and SoftBank. The IMF's October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report counted 14 such deals since 2023: about $300B of equity and $650B of debt, set against $1.4 trillion of future revenue commitments. The IMF's Jason Wu told CNBC on 13 October 2026: "What makes this a little bit unusual compared to previous deals is the future revenue commitments, hence the circularity."
- **Ranked by exposure, the bill sits with:** (1) Oracle and CoreWeave bondholders and shareholders; (2) Nvidia, which holds $99B of equity investments, $25B of further equity commitments and $36B of capacity purchase commitments, and has signed lease guarantees; (3) private-credit and insurance lenders in SPVs such as Meta's Hyperion; (4) shareholders of Meta, Alphabet and Amazon, through dilution and negative free cash flow; (5) PJM electricity customers, whose capacity charges include $29.4B attributed to data centers across the last four auctions, 46.2% of all capacity charges (Monitoring Analytics market monitor report, 28 July 2026). The dates that matter most are CoreWeave's results on 10 November 2026, the December 2026 PJM auction, the 2027 debt and commitment wall, and Oracle's fiscal 2029, when S&P expects its free cash flow to turn positive.

**Evidence labels used throughout:** CONFIRMED = seen on a primary page (SEC filing, company release, transcript, IMF publication). REPORTED = press or secondary analysis only. ESTIMATE = my calculation or an analyst's, with the working shown.

## Key Findings

1. **The four hyperscalers' 2026 capex guidance adds up to about $720–740B** (REPORTED, compiled after Q3 2026 calls). The company figures are Amazon ~$220B, Alphabet $195–205B, Microsoft ~$170B and Meta $135–145B. The comparable 2025 total was about $410B (REPORTED).\[1\] Meta's range of "$135-145 billion" is CONFIRMED in its 21 October 2026 release.\[2\]\[3\]
2. **In Q3 2026, capex at the four combined exceeded their operating cash flow** (ESTIMATE, from company figures):
   - Capex: Microsoft $45.2B, Alphabet $51.4B, Amazon $75.9B (REPORTED), Meta $34.91B; total $207.4B.
   - Operating cash flow: Microsoft $58.0B, Alphabet $42.3B, Amazon $42.4B, Meta $32.79B; total $175.5B.
   - The gap is $31.9B, so operating cash covered about 85% of capex. That is before dividends and buybacks.
   - Microsoft is the only one of the four with a surplus. The fiscal calendars line up because Microsoft's fiscal Q1 2027 is the quarter ending 30 September 2026.
3. **The share of building money from debt and outside investors rose from under 10% in 2024 to roughly 35–45% in 2026** (ESTIMATE; working under "Share of money from outside").
4. **Investment-grade bond markets now carry the build-out.**
   - The IMF's October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report states, per CNBC on 13 October 2026: "Hyperscalers' share of global investment-grade bond issuance has risen to 10% from less than 1% since 2024."
   - Bank of America estimated $270B of AI-related bonds issued in 2026 by early July, $194B of them from hyperscalers. AI-related issuance in all of 2025 was $136B (REPORTED, Fortune, 8 July 2026).\[4\]
5. **The two weakest credits are Oracle and CoreWeave, and both rest on OpenAI.**
   - Oracle is rated BBB- by S&P after a downgrade on 9 July 2026 (REPORTED).\[5\]\[6\]\[7\] Its fiscal 2026 free cash flow was −$23.7B (CONFIRMED).\[8\]
   - CoreWeave's unsecured 2031 debt traded "as low as 79.5 cents on the dollar, according to Trace pricing data," and its 2030 and 2032 notes had "yields exceeding 15%" (REPORTED, Bloomberg, 4 November 2026). CNBC reported on 6 November 2026 that the yield on the 9.625% 2032 bond "has climbed above 16%."
6. **Nvidia has become a lender of last resort to its own customers.**
   - Its 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 July 2026 shows "equity investments of $99 billion and equity investment commitments of $25 billion" (CONFIRMED).\[9\]
   - It also shows cloud capacity commitments that "totaled $36 billion" (CONFIRMED), plus new guarantees tied to OpenAI's leases (CONFIRMED in nature; the size is REPORTED at up to $105B).\[9\]\[10\]
7. **Ratepayers are the only group already paying regardless of how AI demand turns out.** PJM's market monitor attributes $9.33B (2025/26), $7.27B (2026/27), $6.5B (2027/28) and $6.3B (2028/29, out of $16.4B) of capacity charges to data-center load. Monitoring Analytics' report of 28 July 2026 states: "The total capacity market charges that have resulted from data center load over the last four capacity market auctions equal $29.4B, or 46.2 percent of total capacity market charges."

## Who Holds the Bill if Usage Grows Slowly

1. **Oracle and CoreWeave bondholders and shareholders hold the most.** Each has negative free cash flow, large lease obligations that have not started, and concentrated customers. CoreWeave pays 9–9.75% on unsecured notes, and the market already prices its unsecured 2031 debt as low as 79.5 cents on the dollar (Bloomberg, 4 November 2026, citing Trace pricing data). Oracle sits one notch above junk.\[62\] S&P has said its rating depends on cash "at years three, four, five of their contracts" (REPORTED).\[122\]
2. **Nvidia is next.** It holds $99B of equity investments and $25B of further equity commitments (CONFIRMED), $36B of capacity purchase commitments (CONFIRMED), and guarantees that may reach $105B for OpenAI's Ohio leases (REPORTED).\[9\]\[10\] If the labs miss their targets, Nvidia takes write-downs and pays out on guarantees in the same downturn in which it loses sales.
3. **Private-credit and insurance lenders are third.** They include PIMCO's roughly $18B in Hyperion and roughly $10B in Oracle's Michigan campus,\[53\]\[64\] and Apollo in xAI and SpaceX. Hyperion's bondholders are well protected by Meta's residual value guarantee, which moves the risk back to Meta's shareholders. The IMF warns that direct-lending funds "have committed to provide funding on demand" (CONFIRMED, IMF blog), which can drain liquidity in stress.\[123\]
4. **Shareholders of Meta, Alphabet and Amazon are fourth.** They are being diluted (Meta: $35B now and $60B planned; Alphabet: $80B), and buybacks have stopped at Meta (REPORTED).\[55\] Their bondholders are safe, given AA and A ratings and large operating cash flows.
5. **Prepaying customers come fifth.** OpenAI and others have put $75B into prepaid or self-supplied GPUs at Oracle (CONFIRMED).\[8\] If Oracle stumbles, those customers are unsecured counterparties holding hardware in someone else's buildings.
6. **Electricity ratepayers are already paying, and they are the group least able to get out.** PJM capacity charges include $29.4B attributed to data-center load across four auctions, 46.2% of all capacity charges (Monitoring Analytics market monitor report, 28 July 2026), and capacity is bought three years ahead against forecast load. If data centers arrive late, households will have paid for capacity reserved for them anyway.
7. **Governments carry the least direct exposure and the largest indirect one.** The IMF's downside scenario has a steep drop in AI investment "cutting global GDP by 1.1% by 2028, including a 1.8% drop in US output" (REPORTED, Reuters, 13 October 2026).\[124\]

## Recommendations

- **Bond investors:** treat Oracle and CoreWeave as the bellwether credits. A CoreWeave miss on 10 November 2026, or a weak price in the 19 November Stargate loan auction, should be read as an early default signal for the neocloud layer. Hyperscaler AA/A paper remains safe and is now paying extra yield (Amazon's July concession).
- **Equity investors:** expect continued dilution at Meta and Alphabet through 2027. Value Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft partly on their stakes in Anthropic and OpenAI, and recognise that those gains rise and fall with the same customers.
- **Corporate buyers of AI compute:** avoid prepaying for GPUs at providers rated BBB- or lower. Negotiate step-down rights that match your consumption-based revenue.
- **Policymakers and utility regulators:** require large-load tariffs that put the cost of capacity built for data centers on the data centers, before the December 2026 PJM auction locks in another year.

## Five-Step Plain-English Chain

1. **The big tech companies earn huge amounts from ads, cloud rentals and software.** For years that cash paid for every new data center.
2. **The AI build grew faster than that cash.** In mid-2026, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta started spending more on buildings and chips than their businesses bring in, so they began borrowing from bond investors and selling new shares.
3. **Smaller builders such as Oracle and CoreWeave borrow much more, at higher interest.** They also use partner companies and special vehicles whose debt does not appear on their own books.
4. **Many of the customers renting this capacity, mainly OpenAI and other AI labs, lose money.** They pay their bills with cash raised from investors, and many of those investors are the same chip and cloud companies they buy from. Part of the money goes around in a circle.
5. **If AI usage grows more slowly than promised, the chain breaks at its weakest borrowers first.** Their lenders and shareholders lose money, Nvidia pays out on its guarantees, and the big companies write down their stakes. Households on stretched power grids keep paying higher electricity bills for power plants reserved for data centers.
