```
I am researching build versus buy for business software, for two outputs: a funny short video and a serious, enterprise-grade article that explains the money so simply a child could follow it. I need true, sourced stories and exact numbers. Cover any year for the stories (older classics are welcome if they are well documented) and January 2024 to today for prices and AI.

PART 1. Engineers going wild: true stories of teams that built what they could have bought
Find 10 to 15 documented cases where engineers built their own version of something they could have bought (chat, CRM, ticketing, wiki, payroll, billing, analytics, deployment tools, an in-house database or programming language, a "we'll rewrite it over the weekend" project) and what happened next. I especially want the funny or painful ones: the rewrite that took years, the tool only one person understood who then left, the home-built system the company later paid to escape, the team that went back to the product it replaced. Examples to check, not assume: Uber's in-house chat app and its move off it; Segment moving from microservices back to one system; any company that built its own CRM or HR system and returned to a vendor; the Netscape rewrite; "not invented here" stories from large tech firms. For each: who, what they built, what they could have bought instead, how long and how many people it took, what it cost if public, the outcome, and a quotable line from someone involved.

PART 2. Commercial leaders whose eyes were bigger than their stomachs
Find 8 to 12 documented cases where an executive (CEO, CFO, COO, sales or marketing leader, not an engineer) announced or ordered a big in-house build, an "AI will replace our software" plan, or a cancel-the-vendors push, and how it turned out. Include: public statements by CEOs that they would replace SaaS tools with AI or in-house builds (2024 to today), and any follow-up showing it held, shrank or was reversed; big in-house projects that ran far over budget or were scrapped (ERP, CRM, payroll, government systems), with the overspend in dollars and the delay in months; executives quoted saying they underestimated upkeep, migration or hiring. Give the quote, the date and the link.

PART 3. Software that charges through the roof
Build a list of the steepest documented price rises for business software from January 2023 to today. For each: vendor, product, old price, new price, percentage change, date, what changed (per-seat rise, forced bundle, licence model change, per-employee counting, AI add-on, credits or usage fees, minimum commitments), and what customers or analysts said, with the source. Check at least: Broadcom's VMware licensing changes; Oracle Java's move to per-employee pricing and its licence audits; Microsoft 365 and Copilot price changes and bundles; Salesforce list price rises and Agentforce pricing; Slack, Atlassian (Jira, Confluence, the end of Server), Adobe, DocuSign, Zoom, HubSpot, Zendesk, Intercom's per-resolution AI pricing, ServiceNow and Workday; Twitter/X API pricing; Unity's runtime fee. Also find: the average yearly SaaS price rise (from Vertice, Tropic, Spendflo, Zylo, Gartner, or similar: say who runs each and that they sell to buyers), the share of SaaS licences companies pay for and do not use, and typical renewal increases.

PART 4. The AI labs' own software bill (the piece's title)
What business software do OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta's AI teams, Mistral and xAI publicly pay for or run on (CRM, HR, finance, support, collaboration)? Use only public evidence: vendor customer case studies and press releases, executive statements, conference talks, and job postings that name tools. Did any of them say they replaced a vendor with their own AI-built tool, or chose not to? Give each with its link and date, and say clearly where evidence is missing.

PART 5. The money, line by line, with real benchmarks
I will explain build versus buy as a household budget. Give me sourced, dated benchmarks for each line, so I can show the real costs side by side:
- Fully loaded cost of a software engineer (salary plus benefits, tax, laptop, office) in the US and UK, per year and per hour.
- The share of a system's lifetime cost spent after the first version (maintenance, fixes, upgrades). Name the study and its sample.
- The cost of security and compliance for an in-house tool: SOC 2 audit, penetration test, single sign-on, data backups. Typical prices.
- Switching and migration costs: data migration, retraining staff, running two systems at once. Typical weeks and dollars.
- Hiring: how much longer it takes to train a new hire on a home-built tool versus a well-known product, if anyone has measured it.
- How much companies typically win off a renewal by showing they could build or switch (negotiation leverage), from procurement data.
- AI coding tool costs: what companies pay per engineer per month for Claude Code, Cursor, GitHub Copilot and similar, and any measured change in how fast tools get built.
Then work three examples end to end, showing every line and its source, for a 200-person company deciding whether to (a) build its own CRM instead of paying for 50 Salesforce or HubSpot seats, (b) build its own customer support help desk instead of Zendesk or Intercom, (c) build its own email marketing tool instead of Klaviyo or Mailchimp. Show year 1 and year 3, and mark which inputs are your assumptions.

PART 6. Pictures for an enterprise article
Point me to data series that would make clear charts: SaaS price rises over time, SaaS spend per employee over time, share of companies building versus buying by category, maintenance as a share of lifetime cost. Give the source and the numbers for each.

Rules for the evidence:
- Prefer primary sources: company filings, earnings call transcripts, official pricing pages and their archived copies (Wayback Machine links for old prices), vendor press releases, court records, government audit reports, named people's own posts. Use press only to point to a primary source, and say so when none exists.
- Every number with its date, unit and a link to the page that shows it. Quote the exact sentence for every quote.
- Mark each claim CONFIRMED (seen on a primary page), REPORTED (press only) or ESTIMATE (an analyst's or your own calculation, with the working).
- Say who each survey or benchmark comes from and whether they sell something related (procurement software, building tools, consulting).
- Do not invent or smooth over anything. If a famous story is mostly legend, say so.

Finish with: a table of every story in Parts 1 and 2 (who, what, outcome, cost, source); a table of every price rise in Part 3, sorted by percentage; the three worked examples from Part 5 as tables; and the ten funniest true details you found, each with its source.
```
