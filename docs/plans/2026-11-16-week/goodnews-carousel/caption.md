This week AI helped people speak, sign and get around.

Her own voice back. Anne-Marie Poirier in Montreal lost her speech to ALS. The Vocality app speaks her sentences in a copy of her own voice, built from recordings of her talking. Over 100 people use it daily. (The Canadian Press, 11 Nov 2026)

Talking both ways. Thatsign turns a parent's speech into British Sign Language and a deaf child's signs back into words. Still a prototype, it knows 13 signs, and a deaf teacher gave it 9 out of 10. Over 9 in 10 deaf children have hearing parents. (Techpoint Africa, 12 Nov 2026)

A guide that steers. Glide, from Glidance, steers blind and low-vision walkers around obstacles and tells you what is around you when you ask. It is reaching its first customers. (Glidance, 10 Nov 2026)

Glasses that read. Google's new AI glasses describe what is in front of blind and low-vision wearers and read small print aloud. On sale 10 December. Google says they are no mobility aid. (Google, 12 Nov 2026)

A 17-year-old's fix. Yash Bhatnagar built Visionably so blind people can read their own bank statements. It works on about 95% of the PDFs it is tested on. (The Better India, 16 Nov 2026)

Why now: AI that hears, speaks and sees is ready to buy, so small teams build on it. Vocality's voice copy runs on ElevenLabs.

The bigger picture: the World Health Organization says over 2.5 billion people need help such as glasses, hearing aids or wheelchairs, and in many countries most go without.

Try this week: turn on VoiceOver (iPhone) or TalkBack (Android) and listen to one of your own PDFs. Whatever it skips, a blind customer misses.
