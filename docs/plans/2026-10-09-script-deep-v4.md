I've been watching what's coming with AI, before everyone notices. I'm Krish.

I've been using AI apps every day for years now. And I've noticed something odd about how they're sold. Every launch is about a name. A new number, a livestream, a chart. Claude Opus 4.8. Then Opus 5. Then Opus 5.5. At this rate, when's Opus 5.56 coming out? So I wanted to break it down. Does the name you pick actually change the answer you get?

To be fair, some of the new AIs really are better. The people building them aren't lying to you. But I think the name is the least important thing on your screen.

There's a setting that changes your answer much more. It decides how hard the AI thinks before it answers. The app usually starts it low, and almost nobody changes it. There are a few reasons OpenAI and Anthropic would rather you look at the name. Let's unpack them.

And I think the AI itself will end up deciding how hard it thinks. That's good news and bad news.

First, the names. Anthropic makes Claude. It launched Opus 4.8 in May, Opus 5 in July, and Opus 5.5 in September. OpenAI makes ChatGPT. It went from GPT 5.1 last November to GPT 6.1 by the end of September.

Why so many? OpenAI and Anthropic don't say it's to get in the news. So this next bit is my own opinion. Look at what a launch gets them.

In March 2025, OpenAI added picture making to ChatGPT. Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, then said a million new people joined in one hour. That same day, OpenAI said investors were putting in forty billion dollars, hoping OpenAI grows. OpenAI had been lining up that money for months. But announcing it on such a big day made OpenAI look even better.

So what does matter? Picture a woman called Sally. She runs a small shop. She's about to sign a deal with a supplier, who sells her stock. She pastes the deal into her AI app and asks what she should worry about. In four seconds, it gives her a tidy answer. It sounds sure. She signs.

Six months later, she finds one small rule hidden in the deal. It costs her a lot of money. A careful reader would have spotted it.

Nothing was wrong with the name on her screen. The AI just never stopped to think.

ChatGPT and Claude both let you choose how hard the AI thinks.

I looked up what thinking means. The AI writes out its working first. At most, you see a short summary of it. It breaks the question into steps. It tries more than one way. Then it checks its own answer. If the app lets it search the web, a higher setting usually means more searching, too.

Think of a car. The name is the badge on the front. The thinking setting is the gears. Right now, these apps are like a manual car, where you change gear yourself. In a low gear, it barely thinks. In a high gear, it thinks hardest. We argue about badges. And most of us drive everywhere in first gear, the lowest one.

How big is the difference? A group called Artificial Analysis tests these AIs. They ran the very same Claude, Opus 5.5, in different gears. In a low gear, it scored 42 on their test. In its top gear, 58. The older Opus 5, trying its hardest, only got 51. So changing gear beat getting a new badge.

But more thinking isn't always better. Each extra bit helps a little less than the last. Researchers asked thinking AIs a really simple question. What is two plus three? They used about twenty times more words than normal AIs. One of them worked out the answer thirteen times.

So the gear matters most on hard jobs, like Sally's deal. On easy ones, extra thinking is wasted money. Whose money?

Every word of that hidden working runs on huge computers, and running them costs money. If Opus 5.5 thinks as hard as it can, it costs about eleven times as much as thinking a little.

Who pays for that thinking depends on how you pay.

Some people pay for every word the AI writes. Usually that's businesses putting AI inside their own apps. For them, more thinking means a bigger bill. So when businesses turn up the thinking, OpenAI and Anthropic earn more.

But most of us pay the same amount every month, however much we use. Then every extra thought costs OpenAI or Anthropic, not us. In January 2025, Sam Altman said OpenAI was losing money on its most expensive plan. It cost two hundred dollars a month. People used it so much that running it cost more than they paid.

I wanted to know how much money OpenAI makes on a hard-thinking answer, compared with a quick one. Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic tells us.

All we get is an average across OpenAI's paying users. From every dollar they pay, about seventy cents is left after running the AI. So on average, answering us makes OpenAI money.

But that's an average. On a monthly plan, a hard-thinking answer costs OpenAI far more than a quick one, while you pay the same. That, I think, is why the app starts you in a low gear. Nobody has said it out loud.

So who decides how hard your AI thinks? The AI already decides more than you'd guess. Even inside one gear, it chooses how much to think. OpenAI's own instructions say its AIs think less on simple jobs, and harder on tricky ones.

The next step is the AI choosing the gear for you, and Google has started. Since the ninth of October, the free Gemini app starts on a setting called Auto. It decides for you how much power each question gets, like an automatic.

OpenAI has gone the other way, and taken ChatGPT's automatic gear picker away, twice. Last December, free users lost it. A magazine called WIRED said the picker sent lots more free users to the slower, thinking answers. Those cost OpenAI more. Then in September, paying users lost it too. OpenAI didn't say why. Now, if you pay, you pick the gear.

I don't think that lasts, and here's my guess why. Think what each part of the car is worth to OpenAI or Anthropic. The badge brings attention. Launches come with headlines, new users and a good moment to ask investors for money. So they make sure you watch the badge. Then you don't notice the gear. Even Anthropic's launch chart for Opus 5.5 shows it in top gear. That's its best, not the gear most apps start you in. The gears decide the bill. On a monthly plan, every high gear costs them. They'd like their own hand on the gearstick, so they decide how much thinking each question gets. Most of us would rather not change gear at all. So I think the automatic wins.

Is that good? Some of both. The good first. People don't change settings. Back in 2025, when ChatGPT's gear picker first arrived, far more people ended up with careful, thinking answers. Before, fewer than one free user in a hundred used thinking each day. After, seven in a hundred did.

Anthropic learned this too. This March, it moved its coding app down to the middle gear. Users said it felt less smart, but most kept it in the middle gear. Anthropic later called that a mistake, and put it back up. So whoever picks the starting gear decides how hard most people's AI thinks. An AI that picks for each question would at least think hard when it matters.

Now the bad. You can't see it. This January, OpenAI made ChatGPT think for less time, because people like faster answers. By mistake, it also cut the longest thinking setting. It took weeks to fix. Nobody looking at their screen would have known.

And whatever does the deciding has two jobs. Give you a good answer, and keep OpenAI's costs down. Those don't always agree.

When the AI picks your gear, is it thinking about you, or about the bill? And can you see what it picked?

So what should you do?

For quick, everyday stuff, a low gear is fine.

For anything you'll act on, change the gear yourself. A deal you're signing. A health question. Money. A choice you can't take back. On ChatGPT, pick Medium or High. On Claude and Gemini, the gear is called effort, so turn it up. It takes a few more seconds. For the questions that matter, it's worth it.

Formula 1 found a better way decades ago. Drivers change gear with a paddle behind the steering wheel. A computer does the fiddly part of each change. Software that changes gear by itself was tried, but it's been banned since 2004. The driver still picks every gear. Let the AI pick our gear, if it must. But give us paddles, so we can take over. And show us which gear it picked, under every answer.

We've just spent two years learning to drive a manual. I think the car is going automatic next. For most people, that's easier. But Formula 1 still keeps the paddles in the driver's hands. And some people who love driving still choose a manual. For anything that matters, I'll be one of them.

Here's my bet. By the end of June 2027, ChatGPT will let the AI pick the gear on its paid Plus plan. Nobody will have to switch anything on. I'd be wrong if, on that date, Plus users still have to pick the gear themselves. I'm 85% sure.
