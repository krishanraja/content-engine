# Reader test 2: "Will your AI auditor find your missing money, or the skeletons in your closet?"

1. Enjoyable: 9/10. It opens on a real $195,000 bill, keeps a light touch ("Never pick a paperwork fight with someone who has a chatbot and a free evening.") and turns well at "To your customers, you're the hospital."
2. Big picture: 9/10. "The auditor who never gets bored" and "Who reads it first" say plainly what this means for the reader's business and for their job as the manager who decides who reads the findings.
3. Investigative: 8/10. Every story is sourced and qualified ("one side of a dispute", "no misconduct was found"), but the business claim the piece rests on has no evidence: "Each one's minor. Across two years of paperwork they add up." gives no figure or real company case.
4. Incredibly easy to follow: 8/10. Most terms are explained where they first appear (Medicare, earnout, Court of Chancery, OpenSSL, ImageTwin). Three gaps: the numbers that look alike don't add up on a single read ($195,000 less "around $100,000" is not $33,000, so a reader wonders where the other $62,000 went); "Fortis Advisors v. Krafton" never says who Fortis is; "each ending on a dated call" is unexplained in the opening line.
5. Easy to learn from: 9/10. The method is clear and repeatable (ask for the detail, let the AI read all of it, have a person check each flag), and the three steps are concrete.
6. Visual cues: 7/10. There are four figures for seven sections, each placed where its question comes up and each readable at a glance. But two say things the article does not (see the fixes), and the Krafton excerpt needs words the article never explains.

Fixes:
- Investigative, "Each one's minor. Across two years of paperwork they add up.": add one sourced figure or named company case for what a full invoice reread recovers.
- Easy to follow, "which Tom's Hardware says accounted for around $100,000": add one clause saying the rest of the cut came from the wrong codes and the negotiation, so $195,000 to $33,000 adds up (only if the source supports it).
- Easy to follow, "in a case called Fortis Advisors v. Krafton": add "(Fortis acted for the studio's former owners)" or drop the case name.
- Easy to follow, "each ending on a dated call": say "each ending on a prediction with a date on it".
- Figure hindsight.png: "Found by AI-written tests, September 2024" gives a month the article never mentions (the article says Google wrote in November 2024). Either change it to "Reported November 2024" or put September 2024 in the article with its source.
- Figure hindsight.png: the breast-cancer bar starts partway along an axis labelled "About two decades ago" to "Today", which suggests a start date the article never gives. Make the start undated, or name the scan years in the article.
- Figure skeletons.png: the Krafton excerpt opens on a cut-off "258 days." and uses "the Buyer's CEO" and "the Target's operations", none of which the article explains. Crop to start at "the court quoted at length" and label it "Buyer = Krafton; Target = the studio".
- Figure skeletons.png: "Two old records that someone outside read" doesn't fit Krafton, where the court read the chief executive's chats in a lawsuit. Use "Two records that came back to be read, and came back to bite."
- Figure bill-found.png: the Tom's Hardware headline in the screenshot has an em dash and the words "other violations", which the article doesn't use. Krish decides whether a source screenshot is exempt from the no-em-dash rule. If it isn't, crop the screenshot to the headline's first clause.
- Figure bill-found.png: "BILLED TWICE: ABOUT $100,000" makes the claim sound firmer than the article does, since the article says around $100,000 that Medicare "would have rejected". Use "BILLED FOR THE WHOLE AND EACH PART: ABOUT $100,000".
