# Will your AI auditor find your missing money, or the skeletons in your closet?

*AI is at its best on paperwork that's already finished. Point it at your invoices and contracts and it finds money you're owed. It also finds the things you hoped nobody would ever reread.*

A family was sent a hospital bill for $195,000 for four hours of intensive care. They asked for the bill line by line, gave it to Claude (Anthropic's AI chatbot, a rival to ChatGPT), and say it came down to $33,000. If you run a business, your last two years of supplier invoices, expense claims and customer contracts are the same kind of paperwork, and the same tools will read them for you (and for anyone else who gets hold of them).

## The $195,000 bill

The story comes from a user of Threads, Meta's social app, who goes by nthmonkey, and Tom's Hardware reported it on 29 October 2025. Their brother-in-law died after a heart attack, two months after his health insurance had lapsed, so the family owed the whole bill: $195,000 for his final four hours of intensive care.

They didn't argue with the total. First they made the hospital break the bill down. One line just read "Cardiology" at $70,000, and it took some back and forth to get the standard charging codes behind lines like that. Then they gave the lot to Claude.

It found the hospital had billed for a main procedure and then again for every part of it, which Tom's Hardware says accounted for around $100,000 that Medicare (the US government's health insurance for older people, whose rules set what a hospital may charge it) would have rejected. It found inpatient codes used where emergency codes applied. It helped write the letters too. The bill came down to $33,000.

That's the family's account. Tom's Hardware says it hasn't checked the story independently and the hospital hasn't commented, so we read it as one side of a dispute. We still think it's worth your time, because the method is so ordinary: ask for the detail, then let something tireless read it.

And it isn't a one-off. In December 2024 Upworthy reported on a tenant, Chris Maloney, whose landlord kept over $600 of his deposit. He pasted his lease into ChatGPT, which pointed him to a 2019 law where he lives (he didn't name it in his post) that caps a deposit at one month's rent. He took the landlord to court, the landlord countersued for $5,000, and the judge threw the counterclaim out. Never pick a paperwork fight with someone who has a chatbot and a free evening.

## Why hindsight

These same tools still get plenty wrong elsewhere. They're good at this because an old bill can't argue back. It says what it says, and the rules it should have followed are written down: the price list, the contract, the billing codes, the law on deposits. Checking one against the other is slow and dull, which is exactly the kind of work these tools do well.

Ask an AI about next year and it's guessing, like the rest of us. Ask it about last year and it's reading. That makes it a poor fortune teller and a very good auditor.

The same pattern shows up far from household bills. In November 2024 Google's security team wrote on its blog that AI-written tests (programs that throw odd inputs at software to see what breaks) had found a flaw in OpenSSL, software that protects much of the internet, which had "likely been present for two decades". And a UCLA Health study published in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute in May 2025 found AI could flag some breast cancers that were visible on routine scans but missed at the time. In each case the record already existed. The AI just read it again, all of it, without getting tired.

## The auditor who never gets bored

Now point that at a business. Your rules are in your supplier contracts and your price lists, and your history is in your invoices and expense claims. Most firms check a sample, because nobody has time to reread everything. An AI can reread everything.

What it tends to turn up is small and dull: the invoice paid twice, the rate above the one in the contract, the credit nobody claimed, the subscription still running for a team that left last spring. Each one's minor. Across two years of paperwork they add up.

It cuts both ways, and the second way is the one we think most businesses haven't clocked yet. To your customers, you're the hospital. They can paste your invoices into a chatbot and check them against the price you agreed, just as that family did. Some will.

## The skeletons

An auditor doesn't choose what it finds. It opens every drawer.

In January 2024 the biologist and blogger Sholto David went through papers from the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, using ImageTwin (AI-powered software that compares a paper's images against about 50 million others) alongside his own eyes, and flagged duplicated and edited images in studies going back years. NBC News reported that Dana-Farber asked for six papers to be retracted, and 31 were being corrected. Its research integrity officer said "the presence of image discrepancies in a paper is not evidence of an author's intent to deceive", and no misconduct was found. Still, someone outside the building read the old record before anyone inside did.

The newest skeleton might be your own AI chats. In March 2026 the Delaware Court of Chancery (the US court where most big company disputes are heard) ruled against Krafton, the games company that bought the studio behind Subnautica, in a case called Fortis Advisors v. Krafton, over a $250 million earnout (extra payments owed to the studio's founders if it hit its targets after the sale). According to the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance, Krafton's chief executive had asked an AI chatbot how to take control of the studio, and the court "quoted at length" from its answers. He also admitted deleting some of the chat logs. Krafton lost.

Whatever you type into an AI today is part of tomorrow's record. Type it as if a judge might read it.

## Who reads it first

So set the auditor loose, with two rules in place before you do.

First, treat every flag as a question for a person to answer. The family's case worked because someone checked each line Claude raised and took it to the hospital. The UCLA researchers said the same about their scans: AI isn't perfect and shouldn't be used on its own. A flag that nobody checks is just a rumour.

Second, decide who reads the findings before you start digging. If the audit turns up a duplicate payment, finance should see it. If it turns up something worse, you'll want the right person to see it first (before it's forwarded round the whole company).

## Your audit

Start small, with one money pile. Supplier invoices from the last two years are the obvious place: the contracts behind them say what you should have paid, and the invoices say what you did. Give both to the AI your company already pays for, ask it to list anything that doesn't match, and have the person who owns each supplier check each flag.

## 3 things you can do tomorrow

1. Pull last year's invoices from your three biggest suppliers, with the contracts behind them, and ask your company's AI to list every charge that doesn't match the agreed price.
2. Name the one person who reads the findings first, before anyone runs the audit.
3. Read your own AI chats from the last month as if a judge were reading them, and change what you type from now on.
