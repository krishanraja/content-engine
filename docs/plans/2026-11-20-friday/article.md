# Will your AI auditor find your missing money, or the skeletons in your closet?

*AI is at its best on paperwork that's already finished. Point it at your invoices and contracts and it finds money you're owed. It also finds the things you hoped nobody would ever reread.*

A family was sent a hospital bill for $195,000 for four hours of intensive care. They asked for the bill line by line, gave it to Claude (Anthropic's AI chatbot, a rival to ChatGPT), and say it came down to $33,000. Your business's last two years of invoices, expense claims and contracts are the same kind of paperwork. The same tools will read them for you, and for anyone who gets hold of them.

## The $195,000 bill

The story comes from nthmonkey, a user of Threads (Meta's social app), and the tech news site Tom's Hardware reported it in October 2025. Their brother-in-law died after a heart attack. His health insurance had lapsed two months before, so the family owed the whole bill: $195,000 for his final four hours of intensive care.

First they asked the hospital to break the bill down. One line just read "Cardiology" at $70,000, and it took some back and forth to get the standard charging codes behind lines like that. Then they gave the lot to Claude.

It found the hospital had billed for a main procedure and then again for every part of it. Tom's Hardware says that came to around $100,000 that Medicare would have rejected. (Medicare is the US government's health insurance for older people, and its rules set what a hospital may charge it.) It found inpatient codes used where emergency codes applied, which changes what a hospital can be paid. It helped write the letters too. Over a series of exchanges, the hospital dropped some disputed lines and recoded others. The bill came down to $33,000.

That's the family's account. Tom's Hardware says it hasn't verified the story independently, so we read it as one side of a dispute. It's still worth your time, because the method is so ordinary: ask for the detail, then let something tireless read it.

And it isn't a one-off. In December 2024 the news site Upworthy reported on a tenant, Chris Maloney, whose landlord kept over $600 of his deposit. He pasted his lease into ChatGPT, which pointed him to a 2019 law where he lives that caps a deposit at one month's rent. He sued, the landlord countersued for $5,000, and the judge threw the counterclaim out. Never pick a paperwork fight with someone who has a chatbot and a free evening.

## Why AI is good at the past

AI tools are good at this because an old bill can't argue back. The rules it should have followed are written down: the price list, the contract, the billing codes, the law on deposits. Checking one against the other is slow and dull, which is exactly the kind of work these tools do well.

Ask an AI about next year and it's guessing, like the rest of us. Ask it about last year and it's reading. That makes it a poor fortune teller and a very good auditor.

In November 2024 Google's security team wrote on its blog about a flaw in OpenSSL, software that protects much of the internet. AI-written tests (programs that throw odd inputs at software to see what breaks) had found it, and it had "likely been present for two decades". And in May 2025 UCLA Health, the University of California's Los Angeles hospitals, published a study in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute. It found AI could flag some breast cancers. They were visible on routine scans but missed at the time. In each case the record already existed. The AI read it again, all of it, without getting tired.

## The auditor who never gets bored

Now point that at a business. Your rules are in your supplier contracts and your price lists, and your history is in your invoices and expense claims. Most firms check a sample, because nobody has time to reread everything. An AI can.

What it turns up is small and dull. The invoice paid twice, the rate above the one in the contract, the credit nobody claimed, the subscription still running for a team that left last spring. Each one's minor. Across two years of paperwork they add up. In 2022 Washington State's auditor wrote that, by industry experts' estimates, up to 2% of the payments organisations make are duplicates. Nobody we know of has published what an AI reread recovered, so that part is our expectation.

It cuts both ways, and the second way is the one we think most businesses haven't clocked yet. To your customers, you're the hospital. They can paste your invoices into a chatbot and check them against the price you agreed, just as that family did. Some will.

## The skeletons

An auditor doesn't choose what it finds. It opens every drawer.

In January 2024 the biologist and blogger Sholto David went through papers from the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute. He used his own eyes and ImageTwin, AI software that checks a paper's images against about 50 million others. He flagged copied and edited images in studies going back years. NBC News reported that month that Dana-Farber asked for six papers to be retracted, and 31 were being corrected. Its research integrity officer said "the presence of image discrepancies in a paper is not evidence of an author's intent to deceive". That conclusion "can only be drawn after a careful, fact-based examination", the officer added. Still, a blogger outside the institute reread those old papers before anyone inside did.

The newest skeleton might be your own AI chats. Krafton is the games company that bought the studio behind Subnautica. It owed the studio's founders up to $250 million more if the studio hit its targets after the sale (an earnout). In March 2026 the Delaware Court of Chancery, the US court where most big company disputes are heard, ruled against Krafton over that money. The case was Fortis Advisors v. Krafton, and Fortis acted for the studio's former owners. Krafton's chief executive had asked an AI chatbot how to take control of the studio. The court "quoted at length" from its answers, according to the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance. He admitted deleting some of the logs. Krafton lost.

Whatever you type into an AI today is part of tomorrow's record.

## Who reads it first

So set the auditor loose, with two rules in place before you do.

First, treat every flag as a question for a person to answer. The family's case worked because they took each line Claude raised to the hospital and disputed it. The UCLA researchers said the same about their scans: AI isn't perfect and shouldn't be used on its own. A flag that nobody checks is just a rumour.

Second, decide who reads the findings before you start digging. If the audit turns up a duplicate payment, finance should see it. If it turns up something worse, you'll want the right person to see it first (before it's forwarded round the whole company).

## Your audit

Start with supplier invoices from the last two years: the contracts behind them say what you should have paid, and the invoices say what you did. Give both to the AI your company already pays for, ask it to list anything that doesn't match, and have the person who owns each supplier check each flag.

## 3 things you can do tomorrow

1. Pull the last two years' invoices from your three biggest suppliers, with the contracts behind them, and ask your company's AI to list every charge that doesn't match the agreed price.
2. Name the one person who reads the findings first, before anyone runs the audit.
3. Read your own AI chats from the last month as if a judge were reading them, and change what you type from now on.
