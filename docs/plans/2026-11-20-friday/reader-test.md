# Reader test: "Will your AI auditor find your missing money, or the skeletons in your closet?"

Read once by a fresh reader, as a busy non-technical business leader, with the three figures in place.

1. Enjoyable: 8/10. Strong opening story, a good laugh line ("Never pick a paperwork fight with someone who has a chatbot and a free evening"), and the turn to "To your customers, you're the hospital" lands. Held back by one clumsy sentence at the top of "Why hindsight".
2. Big picture: 9/10. "The auditor who never gets bored" says plainly what this means for a business (small, dull losses that add up across two years; your customers can audit you too), and "Who reads it first" speaks to the reader's job as a manager.
3. Investigative: 8/10. Sources, dates and caveats are named, and the hospital story is honestly labelled one side of a dispute. Held back by a vague claim and a weak link: the deposit law is "a 2019 law where he lives", and the Dana-Farber case was found with "image-checking software", which the article never says was AI, yet it sits as an AI-auditor example.
4. Incredibly easy to follow: 7/10. Several terms appear unexplained where they first come up: "Claude" (line 5), "Threads", "Medicare", "earnout", "Delaware Court of Chancery", "AI-written tests". The lookalike numbers ($195,000, $100,000, $70,000, $33,000) are kept apart well.
5. Easy to learn from: 9/10. One concrete starting pile (supplier invoices against contracts), two plain rules (a person checks every flag; decide who reads first), and three steps you could do tomorrow.
6. Visual cues: 6/10. Three figures for seven sections, where the bar is at least four. "The auditor who never gets bored", "Who reads it first" and "Your audit" have none. Each figure sits where its question comes up and reads at a glance, but two carry words the article does not say, and one subtitle does not fit one of its two cases.

Fixes:

- Enjoyable, "A chatbot is good at this, even though the same tools still get plenty wrong elsewhere, because an old bill can't argue back.": split it. "These tools still get plenty wrong. But they're good at this, because an old bill can't argue back."
- Investigative, "which pointed him to a 2019 law where he lives that caps a deposit at one month's rent": name the place and the law, or say Upworthy didn't.
- Investigative, "using image-checking software alongside his own eyes": say whether that software was AI. If it wasn't, say the example is about rereading the record rather than about AI, or move it out of the AI argument.
- Easy to follow, "gave it to Claude" (line 5): "gave it to Claude, an AI chatbot like ChatGPT".
- Easy to follow, "a Threads user": "a user on Threads, Meta's social app".
- Easy to follow, "around $100,000 that Medicare would have rejected": add "(Medicare is the US government's health insurance for older people, and its rules say what a hospital may bill)".
- Easy to follow, "a $250 million earnout owed to the studio's founders": "a $250 million earnout (a bonus owed to the founders if the studio hit its targets after the sale)".
- Easy to follow, "the Delaware Court of Chancery": add "the US court where most big company disputes are heard".
- Easy to follow, "AI-written tests had found a flaw": "AI-written test programs, which throw odd inputs at software to see what breaks, had found a flaw".
- Visual cues, missing figures: add one figure in "The auditor who never gets bored" (the four small finds it names: paid twice, rate above contract, credit never claimed, subscription for a team that left, and "To your customers, you're the hospital"), and ideally one in "Your audit" (contract says / invoice says / flag / a named person checks it).
- Visual cues, figure hindsight.png, footer "UCLA HEALTH, JOURNAL OF THE NATIONAL CANCER INSTITUTE, MAY 2025": the article never names the journal. Name it in the text or drop it from the figure.
- Visual cues, figure hindsight.png, "Breast cancers visible on routine scans but missed at the time.": the article says "some breast cancers". Add "Some" so the figure claims no more than the text.
- Visual cues, figure hindsight.png, "The invoice, the contract, the code are all on file.": "the code" reads as software code next to OpenSSL; the article means billing codes. Say "the billing code", or match the article's list (price list, contract, billing codes, the law).
- Visual cues, figure hindsight.png, footer "GOOGLE SECURITY BLOG": the article says "Google's security team said". Minor; match one to the other.
- Visual cues, figure skeletons.png, footer "20 APRIL 2026, ON FORTIS ADVISORS V. KRAFTON": the article gives neither the date of the Harvard piece nor the case name. Add them to the text or drop them from the figure.
- Visual cues, figure skeletons.png, subtitle "Two old records someone else read before the people inside did.": this fits Dana-Farber, but not Krafton, whose chief executive wrote the chats himself and knew what was in them. Try "Two old records that came back to be read by someone outside."
- Visual cues, figure skeletons.png, "Six retractions requested, 31 corrections.": the article says 31 were asked to be corrected. Write "31 corrections requested" so it doesn't read as 31 made.

Numbers or claims in a figure that the article does not say: the journal name (hindsight.png); "20 April 2026" and "Fortis Advisors v. Krafton" (skeletons.png); "Breast cancers" without "some" (hindsight.png, a stronger claim than the text). Every dollar figure, date and count in bill-found.png matches the article.
