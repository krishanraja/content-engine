This month, voters in Piqua, a small town in Ohio, sacked their mayor. His crime? Saying yes to a Meta data centre. Which is wild, because a data centre is just a big shed full of computers. It doesn't even glow. But say you're the one at work who signs off the AI bill. Towns like Piqua could decide when your tools get new features, and what they cost. So let's find out. Where does AI get built when towns say no?

So I've been digging into Piqua all week, and it's the whole AI story in one small town. Meta, which owns Facebook and Instagram, wanted a data centre there, on the business park by the main road. And last year the mayor and the town's council said yes.

Then the neighbours started doing the maths. A big data centre uses as much electricity as a small city. To feed one, the power company often has to build new power lines and power plants. And the cost of those can end up on everyone's electricity bill. So people in Piqua started to worry they'd be the ones paying for Meta's shed.

And they're not alone. Gallup, the polling firm, found something odd. More Americans object to a data centre next door than to a nuclear power plant. Which is quite something for a building that doesn't glow.

So on the third of November, Piqua went to vote with two questions on the ballot. One, should we ban big data centres? Two to one, yes. Two, should we sack the mayor and the two councillors who said yes to Meta? About seven in ten, yes. Meta says it will work with local officials on the future of its plans. Which, roughly translated, means no one saw that coming.

And Piqua wasn't a one-off. Ballotpedia, which tracks American elections, counted 41 local votes on data centres that day. In all but one, the data centre lost.

Now here's my favourite detail. Two days later, OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, made a promise. It would pay the full cost of the new power its data centres need. That way, the neighbours' bills don't go up. Lovely promise. It's also the exact same promise it made in January. So clearly, a promise alone isn't winning towns like Piqua round.

So what does a builder do when the Piqua of this world says no? It comes back asking the town for less. And I've found three ways it's doing that, each one smaller than the last.

First, it stops asking for the town's power. Some builders now put a gas power plant right beside the data centre. Oracle, which rents out computer power for AI, has signed a deal for a whole fleet of them. It's like a neighbour who promises not to touch your electricity, and then builds a power station in their garden. And funnily enough, neighbours don't love a gas plant either.

Second, it stops asking for the town at all. Instead of one giant shed, builders put little ones out in the countryside, next to solar and wind farms. Those farms often make more power than the power lines can carry away. So a little shed next door soaks it up. The catch is that a little shed can only run the AI tools that already exist. Building the next, smarter AI is a giant job, with thousands of computers working together in one place. So that still needs a Piqua to say yes.

And the third way round, the builders didn't even plan. Your phone. Phones and laptops can now run small AI tools that once needed a data centre. Apple's newer iPhones already do some of their AI on the phone itself. And no one in Piqua gets a vote on your phone. The catch is that a pocket-sized AI does less. It's great for a quick email, and less great for a long, tricky report.

So, where does AI get built when towns say no? Honestly, I think that's the wrong question now. Your everyday AI is spreading out, finding its own power and moving into your pocket. The real question is where the next, smarter AI gets built. Because that still needs a giant shed, and a town that says yes. And if fewer towns say yes, I reckon the new stuff arrives later, and costs more.

Here's what I'm going to do. Next time we renew an AI tool, I'll ask the seller where its new data centres are being built. And if the answer sounds a lot like Piqua, I'll budget for a price rise.
