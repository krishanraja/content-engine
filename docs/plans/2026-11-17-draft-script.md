More Americans object to an AI data centre next door than to a nuclear power plant. And if your business pays for AI, that fight could change where your tools run and what they cost. So if towns say no, where does AI get built now?

I've been digging into this all week, and the surprise is that the answer makes AI smaller. So there are three parts. Why towns say no. The three ways AI is getting round it. And what that means for the AI you pay for.

Part one. A data centre is a giant warehouse full of computers, where the AI on your phone really does its thinking. The big ones use as much electricity as a small city. A town often needs new power lines and power plants for one. And that bill can end up shared by everyone on the network. So people worry they'll pay for it on their own bill.

Seven in ten Americans told the polling firm Gallup they'd object to one nearby. Only about half object to a nuclear plant. So the data centre is now less welcome than a nuclear plant. Which is quite an achievement for a building that mostly just hums.

Then on the third of November, America voted in its midterm elections. Ballotpedia, which tracks American elections, counted 41 towns with a data centre question on the ballot. These were local votes on things like bans, pauses and limits. And in all but one, voters backed the limits.

Two days later, OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, made a big promise. It said it would pay the full cost of the new power its data centres need. That way, its neighbours' bills don't go up. It's a good promise. It's also the same promise it made in January. And saying it twice is usually what you do when you're not sure anyone heard you.

So that's part one. Part two is the bit I love. Picture the data centre as the neighbour no one wants. It keeps getting turned away, so it keeps coming back asking the town for less. There are three ways it's doing that, and each one comes with a catch.

Way one, it stops asking for the town's power. Some builders now plan their own gas power plants right next door. Oracle, which rents out computer power for AI, has signed a deal for a whole fleet of them. It's like a neighbour who promises not to touch your electricity, then builds a power station in their garden. And that's the catch. Neighbours don't love a gas plant either, so it's just a new fight.

Way two, it asks for less land. Instead of one giant warehouse, builders put lots of little ones next to solar and wind farms. Those farms often make more power than they can sell, so the little site soaks it up. The catch is that a small site can run the AI tools you use. But teaching a brand new AI from scratch still takes a giant one.

Way three, it asks for nothing at all, because the AI moves into your pocket. Phones and laptops can now run small AI tools that once needed a data centre. Apple's newer iPhones already do some of their AI on the phone itself. And no one holds a town vote about your phone. The catch is that a pocket-sized AI does less. It's great for a quick email, and less great for a long, tricky report.

Part three is what that means for you. Put the three together and you get a trend. More AI gets smaller, spreads out, and finds its own power. And some of it ends up right in your pocket. The giant warehouses won't disappear. But I reckon the AI that still needs one is where the fights will be. And the delays, and the price rises.

And that changes what you're buying. When you pay for an AI tool, you're also paying for where it runs. Say that warehouse is stuck in a fight with its town. Then new features can come later, and prices can go up. If the tool runs on your own laptop, that fight never touches it.

So if you remember one thing, make it this. Voters now get a big say in where AI gets built. And that's one big push towards AI that's smaller and closer to you.

If I ran a team, I'd do one thing this week. I'd ask whoever sells us our AI tools where they're building the data centres those tools will run on. If it's a town that's fighting them, I'd plan for higher prices.
