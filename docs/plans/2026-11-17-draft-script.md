This month, a small town in Ohio called Piqua sacked its mayor for saying yes to a Meta data centre. Which is wild, because a data centre is just a big shed full of computers. And Gallup, the polling firm, found something odd. People now object to a data centre next door more than to a nuclear power plant. If you sign off the AI bill at work, this is your problem too. Let's find out. Where does AI get built when towns say no?

So I've been digging into Piqua all week, and honestly, it's the whole AI story in one small town. Meta, the social media giant, wanted a data centre on the business park by the main road. And last year the mayor, Jim Vetter, and his council said yes.

Then the neighbours started doing the maths. A big data centre uses as much electricity as a small city. To feed one, the electricity company often has to build new lines and new plants. And the cost of those can end up on everyone's bill. So people in Piqua started to worry they'd be paying for Meta's shed.

Then on the third of November, Piqua got its ballot paper. Question one, ban big data centres from Piqua? Yes, by two to one. Question two, sack the mayor and two of the councillors who backed Meta? Yes, by about seven in ten. And Piqua wasn't a one-off. Ballotpedia, which tracks American elections, counted 41 local votes on data centres that day. In all but one, the data centre lost.

Now here's my favourite detail. Meta's shed was already being built. So Piqua now has a data centre going up in a town that has just banned data centres. Meta says it will work with local officials on what happens next. Which is a very polite way of saying, we'll get back to you.

And the backlash has rattled the biggest names too. Two days after the vote, OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, made a promise. It would pay the full cost of the new power its data centres need. That way, the neighbours' bills don't go up. Lovely promise. It's also the exact same one it made back in January. So a promise on its own clearly isn't calming anyone down.

So what do Meta and its rivals do when a town says no? They're already trying three ways round it, and each one has a catch.

The first is to make your own electricity. Oracle, the software giant, has signed up for a whole fleet of gas plants to power its data centres. That way it never touches the town's supply. It's like the neighbour who promises not to use your electricity. Then they build a gas power station in the back garden. And, shockingly, neighbours don't love a gas plant either.

The second is to move out to the countryside, with lots of little sheds next to solar and wind farms. Those farms often make more power than the cables can carry away. So the little sheds use it up right there, with far fewer neighbours to annoy. Think of it as AI moving to a cottage. But a little shed can only run the AI tools that already exist. Building the next, smarter AI needs thousands of computers working together in one giant shed. So you're right back to needing a Piqua to say yes.

And the third way round, Apple planned for its own reasons. It's the phone in your pocket. On a newer iPhone, ask it to sum up a long email. The answer can come back without ever leaving the phone. No shed, no power lines, and no one in Piqua gets a vote. The catch? A pocket-sized AI does less. It's great for that email, and less great for writing your quarterly report.

So here's the whole story in one breath. A town said no. A mayor got sacked. A shed kept going up. And the builders went looking for ways round. So where does AI get built when towns say no? Honestly, I think that's the wrong question now. The everyday stuff, like email summaries and meeting notes, can live in a little shed or in your pocket. The real question is how many towns will say yes to the giant sheds that build the next, smarter AI. Because if fewer towns say yes, I reckon the new features in your team's tools arrive later, and cost more. As for Meta's shed, it's still going up in a town that just banned it. I'd love to be at Piqua's next council meeting.

Here's what I'm going to do. Next time we renew an AI tool, I'll ask our supplier where its new computing is being built. And if it's waiting on a town that's just said no, I'll budget for a price rise.
