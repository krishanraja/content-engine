This month, a little town in Ohio called Piqua just got rid of its mayor for approving a Meta data centre to be built there. That's a big building full of computers that runs all your AI. They're very unpopular with the left wing who are concerned about the environmental impacts - and very popular with the right wing who see big tech as their economic growth machine at all costs. And Gallup (big business that does surveys), found people now object to one next door more than they do to a nuclear power plant. So if you pay for AI at work, could the midterm results in America push up your bill? And where does AI get built when towns say no to the big data centers? Let's find out.

So I've been digging into Piqua all week, and honestly, it's the whole AI story in one small town. Meta, the social media giant, wanted a data centre on the business park by the main road. And last year the mayor, Jim Vetter, and his council said yes.

Then the neighbours started doing the maths. A big data centre uses as much electricity as a small city. To feed one, the electricity company often has to build new lines and new plants. And the cost of those can end up on everyone's bill. So picture opening your electricity bill and it's gone up double. And then you find out you're chipping in for the power lines to Facebook's new data centre. That's what people in Piqua were worried about.

So on the third of November, Piqua voted on two questions. Should we ban big data centres? Yes, by two to one of its residents. And should we sack the mayor and two of the councillors who backed Meta? Yes, from about 70% of them. Talk about a bad day at the office. And Piqua wasn't a one-off. Ballotpedia, which tracks elections in the US, counted 41 local votes on data centres that day. In all but one, the data centre lost.

Now here's my favourite detail. Meta's data centre was already being built when the town voted. So Piqua now has a data centre going up in a town that has just banned data centres. Meta says it will work with local officials on what happens next. Which is a very polite way of saying, we'll get back to you.

And the backlash has rattled the biggest names too. Two days after the vote, OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, made a promise. It would pay the full cost of the new power its data centres need. That way, the neighbours' bills don't go up. Lovely promise. It's also the exact same one it made back in January, and the towns kept voting no anyway.

So what is everyone building AI doing about it? Oracle, the software giant, decided to skip the town's electricity altogether. It has signed up for a whole fleet of gas plants to power its own data centres. It's like the neighbour who promises not to use your electricity. And then they build a gas power station in the back garden. And, shockingly, neighbours don't love a gas plant either.

Others are heading for the countryside. Soluna, a small energy firm, builds small data centres right next to wind and solar farms. Those farms often make more power than the cables can carry away. So Soluna's data centres use it up on the spot, with far fewer neighbours to annoy. But a small data centre can only run the AI tools that already exist. Building the next, smarter AI needs thousands of computers working together in one giant data centre. So you're right back to needing a little town like Piqua to say yes.

And then Apple is going the other way entirely. It's putting more AI on your phone. On a newer iPhone, it can sum up a long email without sending it anywhere. No data centre, no power lines, and no one in Piqua gets a vote. The catch? A pocket-sized AI does less. It's great for that email, and less great for writing your quarterly report.

So here's the whole story in one breath. A town said no. A mayor got sacked. A data centre kept going up. And the AI companies went looking for ways round. And honestly, I think the bigger question is who pays for the power. When a town says no, the AI companies have to find their own power, or pay for it. Think Oracle's gas plants, or OpenAI's promise. And I reckon that cost gets passed on to you, at your next AI tool renewal. As for Meta's data centre, it's still going up in a town that just banned it. I'd love to be at Piqua's next council meeting.

Next time we renew an AI tool, I'll ask our supplier two things. Where are its new data centres being built, and who pays for their power? Because if it's a town that's just said no, that power bill is heading for my desk.
