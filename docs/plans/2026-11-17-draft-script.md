More Americans object to an AI data centre next door than to a nuclear power plant. And if your business pays for AI, that grudge could slow down your tools. It could push up their price, too. So if towns say no, where does AI get built now?

I've been digging into this all week, and the answer surprised me. AI is learning to need less from the towns that don't want it. So first, I'll show you why towns say no. Then the three tricks AI is using to get round them. And then what that means for you.

A data centre is a giant warehouse full of computers. It's where tools like ChatGPT really do their thinking. The big ones use as much electricity as a small city. To feed one, the power company often has to build new power lines and power plants. And the cost of those can end up on everyone's electricity bill. So people worry they'll be the ones paying for it.

Seven in ten Americans told the polling firm Gallup they'd object to one nearby. Only about half object to a nuclear plant. So a data centre is less welcome than a nuclear plant. Which is quite an achievement for a building that mostly just hums. Mind you, it's a very expensive hum.

Then on the third of November, America voted in its midterm elections. Ballotpedia, which tracks American elections, counted 41 local votes on data centres. They were about things like bans, pauses and limits. And in all but one, voters backed the limits.

Two days later, OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, made a big promise. It said it would pay the full cost of the new power its data centres need. That way, the neighbours' bills don't go up. It's a good promise. It's also the same promise it made in January. So clearly, one promise wasn't enough to win the towns round.

So that's why towns say no. Now the three tricks, and this is the bit I love. Picture the data centre as the neighbour no one wants. It keeps getting turned away. So each time, it comes back needing less from the town. And each trick comes with a catch.

Trick one, it brings its own power. Some builders now plan gas power plants right beside the data centre, so they never touch the town's power lines. Oracle, which rents out computer power for AI, has signed a deal for lots of them. It's like a neighbour who promises not to touch your electricity, then builds a power station in their garden. That's the catch. Neighbours don't love a gas plant either.

Trick two, it splits up and moves out of town. Instead of one giant warehouse, builders put little ones next to solar and wind farms. Those farms often make more power than the power lines can carry away. So the little site uses it up on the spot. The catch is that a little site can only run the AI tools that already exist. Building the next, smarter AI still takes a giant warehouse. So the newest features still depend on the places that get fought over.

Trick three is the sneakiest. It needs nothing from the town at all, because the AI moves into your pocket. Phones and laptops can now run small AI tools that once needed a data centre. Apple's newer iPhones already do some of their AI on the phone itself. And no one holds a town vote about your phone. The catch is that a pocket-sized AI does less. It's great for a quick email, and less great for a long, tricky report.

Now, what that means for you. Put the three tricks together and you get a trend. AI is spreading out, finding its own power, and some of it is shrinking right into your pocket. The giant warehouses won't disappear. But I reckon they're where the fights will be, and fights mean delays.

And that changes what you're buying. When you pay for an AI tool, you're also paying for the warehouse it runs in. If new warehouses get held up, there's less room for everyone. And when there's less room, I'd expect prices to go up and new features to come later. The bits that run on your own laptop dodge that fight completely.

So if you remember one thing, make it this. Towns now get a real say in where AI gets built. And that's pushing AI to spread out, find its own power and move closer to you.

If I ran a team, I'd do one thing this week. I'd ask whoever sells us our AI tools where they're building the data centres those tools will run on. If it's a town that's fighting them, I'd budget for a price rise.
