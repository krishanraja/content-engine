This month, a small town in Ohio called Piqua sacked its mayor for saying yes to a Meta data centre. Which is wild, because a data centre is just a big shed full of computers. Yet more Americans object to one next door than to a nuclear power plant. Now say you're the one at work who signs off the AI bill. Fewer sheds could mean slower, pricier tools. So let's find out. Where does AI get built when towns say no?

So I've been digging into Piqua all week, and honestly, it's the whole AI story in one small town. Meta, which owns Facebook, wanted a data centre on the business park by the main road. And last year the mayor, Jim Vetter, and his council said yes.

Then the neighbours started doing the maths. A big data centre uses as much electricity as a small city. To feed one, the power company often has to build new power lines and power plants. And the cost of those can end up on everyone's electricity bill. So people in Piqua started to worry they'd be paying for Meta's shed.

So on the third of November, picture the ballot paper. Question one: ban big data centres from Piqua? Yes, by two to one. Question two: sack the mayor and the two councillors who backed Meta? Yes, by about seven in ten. And Piqua wasn't a one-off. Ballotpedia, which tracks American elections, counted 41 local votes on data centres that day. In all but one, the data centre lost.

Now here's my favourite detail. Meta's shed was already being built. So Piqua now has a data centre going up in a town that has just banned data centres. Meta says it will work with local officials on what happens next. Which is a very polite way of saying, we'll get back to you.

Even OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, has noticed. Two days after the vote, it promised to pay the full cost of the new power its data centres need. That way, the neighbours' bills don't go up. Lovely promise. It's also the exact same one it made in January. And the towns still voted no.

So picture Meta, packing up the plans in Piqua. Where does it go next? It's really got three options.

Option one, bring your own power station. It's like a neighbour who swears they won't touch your electricity. Then they build a power station in their garden. Oracle, the software giant, has signed up for a whole fleet of these gas plants. And, shockingly, neighbours don't love a gas plant either.

Option two, move to the countryside. Lots of little sheds next to solar and wind farms. Those farms often make more power than the cables can carry away. Think of it as AI moving to a cottage, with lovely views. But a little shed can only run the AI tools that already exist. Building the next, smarter AI needs thousands of computers working together in one giant shed. So you're right back to needing a Piqua to say yes.

Option three isn't even the builders' idea. It's the phone in your pocket. On a newer iPhone, ask it to sum up a long email. The answer comes back without ever leaving the phone. No shed, no power lines, and no one in Piqua gets a vote. The catch? A pocket-sized AI does less. Great for that email, and less great for your quarterly report.

So, where does AI get built when towns say no? Honestly, I think that's the wrong question now. Your everyday AI can live in little sheds and in your pocket. The real question is where the next, smarter AI gets built. Fewer towns saying yes means fewer giant sheds to build it in. And when something's in short supply, I reckon it arrives later, and costs more. Just ask the shed in Piqua.

Here's what I'm going to do. Next time we renew an AI tool, I'll ask the seller where its new computing is being built. And if it's waiting on a town that keeps saying no, I'll budget for a price rise.
