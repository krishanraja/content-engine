This month, a small town in Ohio called Piqua sacked its mayor for saying yes to a Meta data centre. Which is wild, because it's just a big shed full of computers. Gallup, the polling firm, even found people object to one next door more than to a nuclear power plant. So if you're the one at work who approves the AI subscriptions, and winces at every price rise, listen up. So let's find out. Where does AI get built when towns say no?

So I've been digging into Piqua all week, and honestly, it's the whole AI story in one small town. Meta, the social media giant, wanted a data centre on the business park by the main road. And last year the mayor, Jim Vetter, and his council said yes.

Then the neighbours started doing the maths. A big data centre uses as much electricity as a small city. To feed one, the electricity company often has to build new lines and new plants. And the cost of those can end up on everyone's bill. So picture opening your electricity bill, and finding you're chipping in for a tech giant's shed. That's what people in Piqua were worried about.

So on the third of November, Piqua voted on two questions. Should we ban big data centres? Yes, by two to one. And should we sack the mayor and two of the councillors who backed Meta? Yes, by about seven in ten. Talk about a bad day at the office. And Piqua wasn't a one-off. Ballotpedia, which tracks American elections, counted 41 local votes on data centres that day. In all but one, the data centre lost.

Now here's my favourite detail. Meta's shed was already being built when the town voted. So Piqua now has a data centre going up in a town that has just banned data centres. Meta says it will work with local officials on what happens next. Which is a very polite way of saying, we'll get back to you.

And the backlash has rattled the biggest names too. Two days after the vote, OpenAI, which makes ChatGPT, made a promise. It would pay the full cost of the new power its data centres need. That way, the neighbours' bills don't go up. Lovely promise. It's also the exact same one it made back in January, and the towns kept voting no anyway.

So what is everyone building AI doing about it? Oracle, the software giant, decided to skip the town's electricity altogether. It has signed up for a whole fleet of gas plants to power its own data centres. It's like the neighbour who promises not to use your electricity. And then they build a gas power station in the back garden. And, shockingly, neighbours don't love a gas plant either.

Other builders are heading for the countryside instead, with lots of little sheds next to solar and wind farms. Those farms often make more power than the cables can carry away. So the little sheds use it up right there, with far fewer neighbours to annoy. But a little shed can only run the AI tools that already exist. Building the next, smarter AI needs thousands of computers working together in one giant shed. So you're right back to needing a Piqua to say yes.

And then Apple is going the other way entirely. Instead of finding a town, it's putting more AI on your phone. On a newer iPhone, ask it to sum up a long email. The answer can come back without leaving the phone. No shed, no power lines, and no one in Piqua gets a vote. The catch? A pocket-sized AI does less. It's great for that email, and less great for writing your quarterly report.

So here's the whole story in one breath. A town said no. A mayor got sacked. A shed kept going up. And the builders went looking for ways round. But honestly, I don't think the big question is where AI gets built. It's who pays for the power. Piqua decided it wasn't going to be them. OpenAI says it'll be OpenAI. And I reckon when the builders end up paying more for power, they pass it on. Guess to who? You, at your next renewal. As for Meta's shed, it's still going up in a town that just banned it. I'd love to be at Piqua's next council meeting.

Here's what I'm going to do. Next time we renew an AI tool, I'll ask our supplier where its new data centres are being built. And who pays for their power. And if the answer is a town that's just said no, I'll budget for a price rise.
