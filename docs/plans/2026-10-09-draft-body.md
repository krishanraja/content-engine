I've been noticing something about the AI apps I use every day. Open Claude and there's a menu: Opus, Sonnet, Haiku, Fable. Open ChatGPT and it's GPT-6, with a Thinking slider from Instant to Extra High. Behind it, GPT-6 Sol does the answering, and on the dearer plans GPT-6 Pro runs on GPT-6 Astra.

There are a lot of them now. I think of these models as the engines underneath the app. And here's my bet: soon you won't see any of them. The app will pick for you, and you'll only get a choice if you really go looking. That sounds like a small change to a menu. I don't think it is. So I wanted to break down what it does to money, to getting the job done, and to who holds the power.

## HOW WE GOT A MENU THIS LONG

In November 2022 OpenAI launched ChatGPT, built on a model from its GPT-3.5 series. You typed, it answered, and nobody asked you to choose anything.

In March 2024 Anthropic, the company behind Claude, split its brains into three, like cup sizes in a coffee shop. In February 2025 the brains got a dial. Claude 3.7 Sonnet could answer fast or stop and think for longer, from the same brain. OpenAI's top model, GPT-6 Astra, now has effort levels from low to max. One brain plus a dial looks like five brains on the menu.

Count the combinations and you get dozens of "different AIs" from two companies. Claude Code's own help page lists more than a dozen models you can pick by name.

Why so many? My read is that each name is doing four jobs at once. It's a version number, so engineers know what changed. It's a price tag, because small models are cheap to run and big ones are dear. It's an advert, because every new name gets a launch day. And it's a queue ticket: the computers behind the big models are scarce, so easy questions go to the small ones. None of those jobs is yours. They're the companies' reasons.

## THE MENU IS ALREADY GOING

The obvious fix for a confusing menu is to take it away. OpenAI tried. On 7 August 2025 it launched GPT-5 as one system with a built-in router that picked the brain for you, and it replaced the older brains people had chosen for themselves.

It broke on day one. Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, said the "autoswitcher was out of commission for a chunk of the day" and that GPT-5 "seemed way dumber". People missed their old brains so much that, the next day, OpenAI said Plus subscribers could keep using GPT-4o.

People complain about too much choice, then riot when it's taken away. But I don't think the riot wins long term. Most people want one box and no homework. The menu goes, and the choosing moves behind the counter. So what happens once it's there?

## 1. MONEY: WHOEVER PICKS, PRICES

When you pick the model, you know roughly what you're getting. When the app picks, the app decides how much engine your money buys that day.

The plans already work like this, quietly. A report from a research firm called SemiAnalysis, dated 5 October, compared both companies' plans. On the everyday brains, they found Claude's plans gave about five times the value of ChatGPT's. On a Claude plan, the newest top brain, Fable 5.1, "can only be used for 50% of your limit". Same plan, very different amounts of brain, depending on which name gets used.

Now take the name away. My bet is that on a busy day, or in a tight quarter, you get the cheaper engine, and you'd never know. Once the pick belongs to the company, it becomes something they can package: a "fast" tier, a "deep" tier, a premium that guarantees the big model. The thing being picked for you is the brain itself.

## 2. GETTING THE JOB DONE: SAME QUESTION, DIFFERENT ANSWER

This is the one I think people will feel first. Ask the same question on two different days and a router can send it to two different engines. When the answer's worse, you can't tell why. Was it your question? Or did the router decide your question looked easy?

GPT-5's bad first day is the clean example. Same app, same name on the screen, and it "seemed way dumber" because one hidden part wasn't working. Most of the time nobody tells you. And if an answer suddenly feels dumber, a different brain may have answered. Ask the app which one it was, while you still can.

## 3. IF YOU BUILD A BUSINESS ON AI

If your product runs on someone else's AI, a router sitting between you and the engine means your costs and your quality can move without you touching anything. Last month's result might not come back next month.

So stop asking "which AI is best?" and ask "what does a good answer cost me, all in?" A brain that costs five times more per word can be cheaper if it gets the job right first time. Pin the exact version your product runs on, because "latest" can change under you overnight. And keep a record of which brain did what, so when quality slips you can tell whether it was you or them.

## 4. POWER: THE MODEL DISAPPEARS INTO THE APP

When you can't see the model, you stop being loyal to it. You're loyal to the app. The engines turn into parts, like the chip inside your phone: it matters enormously, and almost nobody chooses it.

That moves power to whoever owns the box you type into. Prices are already falling fast. Stanford's AI Index found that the cost of an AI as good as GPT-3.5, the model family behind the original ChatGPT, fell from $20.00 to $0.07 per million tokens between November 2022 and October 2024. On OpenRouter, in the week to 8 October, most of the traffic went to cheap, fast models, and Anthropic's Opus 5.5 ranked ninth. When the engines get cheap and look alike, the money goes to whoever controls the choosing.

The choice won't vanish completely. It'll get buried in settings, for the few people who go looking.

## WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT

If you just use AI, use the default for everyday stuff. When it really matters, a contract, a medical question, a big decision, find the setting, pick the biggest model and turn the thinking up.

If you build on AI, pick a model for each job yourself, and rerun the same test every month. If the results move and you didn't change anything, now you know who did.

## MY PREDICTION

My bet is that by the end of 2027, the big AI apps won't show you a list of models unless you ask. You'll get one box, and choosing will be for people who really go looking.

I'll be wrong if a big app wins by doing the opposite: printing which model answered, and what it cost, under every reply. I'd love to be wrong about that one.
