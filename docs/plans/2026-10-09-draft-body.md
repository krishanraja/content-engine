Open Claude today and it asks you to pick a brain. Opus, Sonnet, Haiku, Fable. Then a version number. Then how hard you want it to think. Open ChatGPT and it's Astra, Sol and Luna, with a thinking dial that runs from low to max. It's like walking into a coffee shop and being asked to choose the bean, the roast date and how much effort the barista should put in. You just wanted a coffee.

So here is the question: why are there suddenly so many AI models, and what does it actually mean for you?

## FIRST, WHAT YOU ARE LOOKING AT

Quick jargon check. A "model" is the brain behind the app. "ChatGPT" and "Claude" are the apps, the shop front. Behind the shop front sit lots of brains, and the name on your screen is a stack of labels squashed into one:

1. **The app**: Claude, Claude Code, ChatGPT, Codex.
2. **The size**: small, medium or large. Haiku, Sonnet, Opus. Luna, Sol, Astra.
3. **The version**: 4.5, 5, 5.5. Newer number, newer brain.
4. **The effort**: how long it thinks before it answers. Low to max.
5. **The speed option**: fast, standard or slow-and-cheap.
6. **Your plan**: which of all that you are allowed to see.

Count the combinations and you get dozens of "different AIs" from two companies. Claude Code alone lists 14 versions you can pick by name.

## WHAT IT USED TO BE

In November 2022 there was one chatbot and one brain. You typed, it answered, nobody asked you to choose anything.

In March 2024 Anthropic, the company behind Claude, split its brains into three sizes, like cups in a coffee shop. Haiku was small and fast. Sonnet was the middle. Opus was the big one for the hardest jobs.

Then something odd happened. Three months later the new middle size, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, beat the old big one on many tests and ran twice as fast. In October 2025 the new small size, Haiku 4.5, coded about as well as the middle size from five months before, at a third of the price.

So the sizes are a conveyor belt more than a ladder. This year's medium beats last year's large. "Opus" means "the biggest one this season", and the seasons are getting shorter.

## WHAT IT IS NOW

In February 2025 the brains got a dial. Claude 3.7 Sonnet could answer fast or stop and think for longer, from the same brain. OpenAI's models now do the same, with effort levels from low to max. One brain plus a dial looks like five brains on the menu.

The result is a menu that even the experts misread. This week, on OpenRouter, a website that lets app builders use hundreds of AI models through one account, a score that blends several tests ranks Claude's middle size, Sonnet 5.5, above Fable 5.1, the one Anthropic aims at the hardest open-ended jobs. Same company. The bigger, pricier name scored lower. Tests are not everything, but if the menu can't tell you which brain is better, who can?

## WHY THERE ARE SO MANY

Here is the root of it. **The name on the menu is doing four jobs at once**, and each job wants a different name.

1. **It's a version number**, like iPhone 15 and iPhone 16. It tells engineers which brain changed.
2. **It's a price tag**, like economy and business class. Small brains are cheap to run. Big brains are dear. Different sizes let the company charge you by the seat.
3. **It's an advert.** Every new name is a launch day, a headline and a reason to look again. Anthropic alone has launched new Claude versions every few months since March 2024.
4. **It's a queue ticket.** The computers that run these brains are scarce. Sending easy questions to small brains frees the big ones for hard work.

One name can't do four jobs well. So the menu grows.

And then AI agents arrived. A chat answer is a few hundred words. An AI agent, an AI that does a task for you step by step, can read a whole project, try things, check its work and keep going for hours. Running the biggest brain for every one of those steps is like hiring a surgeon to take your temperature. So the companies sell a set of brains, a cheap one for the dull steps and a dear one for the hard calls. The long menu is the box of parts.

## FOLLOW THE MONEY

Here's the twist that kills the obvious conspiracy theory. Prices are falling, fast. AI is priced by the token, a small chunk of a word. Stanford's AI Index found that the cost of an AI as good as the original ChatGPT brain fell from about $20 to 7 cents per million tokens between November 2022 and October 2024. Anthropic's big brain, Opus, cost $75 per million tokens of writing in May 2025. Today's Opus 5.5 costs $20. And the newest small brain, Haiku 5.5, costs 50 cents.

So where does the money come from? Volume. Bills go up even as prices go down, the way your phone data got cheaper per gigabyte while your phone bill stayed the same, because you started streaming everything. OpenAI says the thinking that businesses buy to build with grew 320 times per company in a single year.

And the two big companies earn very differently. ChatGPT had more than 900 million weekly users by March, by OpenAI's own count. Anthropic has far fewer people and sells mostly to businesses running long, token-hungry jobs like writing code. Yet Reuters reported that Anthropic was bringing in money at a pace of more than $65 billion a year by the end of July, and that OpenAI told investors its September pace was almost $50 billion. They count differently: Anthropic counts money before paying its partners, OpenAI after paying Microsoft's share. So don't crown a winner. The lesson still stands: a few heavy users can pay as much as hundreds of millions of light ones.

The menu even shapes what your monthly plan buys. This week a research firm called SemiAnalysis tested both companies' plans by watching the usage meter. On the everyday brains, they found Claude's plans gave about five times the value of ChatGPT's. On a Claude plan, the newest top brain, Fable 5.1, "can only be used for 50% of your limit". Same plan, very different amounts of brain, depending on which name you pick.

## WHO PICKS FOR YOU

The obvious fix for a confusing menu is to take it away. OpenAI tried. On 7 August 2025 it made GPT-5 a "single auto-switching system" that picked the brain for you, and removed the older brains people had chosen for themselves. It broke on day one. Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, said the "autoswitcher was out of commission for a chunk of the day" and that GPT-5 "seemed way dumber". People missed their old brains so much that OpenAI brought them back within days.

That's the lesson in one story. People complain about too much choice, then riot when it's taken away. What they want is a sensible default, a clear way to ask for more, and an honest answer to one question: which brain just answered me?

## IS IT A CON?

Our stance: the menu is a real tool with a hidden meter. The strong conspiracy fails, because prices are dropping and builders are flocking to the cheap brains. On OpenRouter most of the traffic goes to cheap, fast models, and Anthropic's top brain ranks ninth.

The catch is quieter. Most apps don't show you which brain answered, how hard it thought, or how much of your allowance it ate. The company knows all three. You know none of them. Picture a taxi with no meter and a driver who picks the route. The route might be fine. You just can't check. And my read is that the windows are about to get darker.

## WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

If you just use AI: you don't need to learn the menu. Learn two moves. Use the default for everyday stuff. When it really matters, a contract, a medical question, a big decision, switch to the biggest brain and turn the thinking up. And if an answer suddenly feels dumber, a different brain may have answered. Ask the app which one it was.

## WHAT IT MEANS IF YOU BUILD A BUSINESS ON AI

Stop asking "which AI is best?" and ask "what does a good answer cost me, all in?" A brain that costs five times more per word can be cheaper if it gets the job right first time. Use small brains for the dull, high-volume jobs and big ones for the moments that matter. Pin the exact version your product runs on, because "latest" can change under you overnight. And keep a record of which brain did what, so when quality slips you can tell whether it was you or them.

## HOW THE OLD WAY IS CHANGING

Software used to be one product at one price. You bought Word, you got Word. AI is turning into a utility, sold by the size and the effort, like electricity at peak and off-peak or post sent first or second class. The money is moving the same way: towards whoever runs the most work through the brains, not whoever has the most fans. And the creative work is changing too. People who make things with AI now use several brains at once, one to plan, one to do, one to check, and the skill is picking the right one for each job.

## THREE WAYS THIS COULD GO

**The Autopilot.** The menu disappears. You choose "quick" or "deep", and the app quietly picks the brain. Good for people who just want answers. Bad for anyone who wants to know what they paid for. First sign: the model picker vanishes from a big app's main screen, for paying users too.

**The Meter.** The brains blur into each other, and you pay for thinking time instead, like first-class post. "Effort" becomes the price tag. Good for people who like knowing the cost. Bad for anyone surprised by a big bill. First sign: an app charges or counts your allowance by "thinking minutes" instead of messages.

**The Glass Box.** After one too many quiet swaps, a big company competes on honesty. Every answer comes with a label: which brain, how hard it thought, what it cost. Good for everyone who wants to check. Bad for companies whose margins rely on you not looking. First sign: a big app prints the model and cost under every answer by default.

The three paths split for good. Our bet: the Autopilot, with the Meter running quietly underneath, and the Glass Box the least likely. Most people want one button and no homework, so the menu goes. And whoever picks the brain controls what every answer costs them to make. Giving that up for honesty's sake would be a big ask of any company. Expect the choice you see to shrink, and the meter to stay hidden.

## OUR PREDICTION

By 30 September 2027, at least two of OpenAI, Anthropic and Google will have their software pick the brain automatically, by default, for businesses that build apps on their AI.

How sure we are: 85%.
