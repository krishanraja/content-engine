Two years ago, picking an AI brain was like picking an ice cream flavour. Chocolate or vanilla. You chose. Now it's an ice cream shop with forty flavours and three sizes. There's a "thinking" scoop that costs extra. And there's a guy behind the counter who sometimes just picks for you without asking. Last time we wrote about him, he picked your flavour. This time he picks the size of the scoop, and he changes it when you are not looking.

Here is the question this piece answers: when you pay for AI, who decides how much brain you actually get?

## THE RECEIPT

On 5 October 2026, a research firm called SemiAnalysis published a test of every big AI subscription plan. Their method was simple and slow. Run the same questions on each plan. Watch the usage meter move. Work out how many tokens a month's plan really buys, where a token is one small block of the words an AI reads or writes, and every one is priced. Then price those tokens at the rate each company charges app builders, which the firms call the API price. The result is what they call the plan's API-equivalent value: what your month would cost if you paid by the block.

At the mid tier, the brains both companies market as "the intended daily driver for most users", their finding was blunt. Comparing Claude Opus 5.5 with GPT-6.1 Sol, "Anthropic is an overwhelmingly better deal, offering ~5x the API-equivalent value across the board". Same price, about five times the AI.

The $200 plans tell the same story with a twist. On SemiAnalysis's numbers, "your $200 ANT plan would still have 50% left after consuming $2,485 worth of Fable 5.1, whereas the equivalent OAI plan would be fully exhausted after $2,897 of Astra". So the top brains come out close. The gap is in the middle, where most people live.

## THE TWIST: IT IS A GIFT, AND GIFTS END

Why would a company sell you $2,000 of something for $200? Because it is a gift to win you over, the way early Uber rides were cheaper than the driver cost. SemiAnalysis puts it plainly: subscriptions are "highly subsidized". The plans bring in a tenth of the money and eat nearly half the machines that answer questions, which the industry calls inference compute. In their words: "Despite being just 10% of overall revenue, subscriptions can take up over 40% of inference compute".

The maths at the extreme is wild. A user who maxes out Opus 5.5 on the $200 plan runs, on their estimate, at a gross margin of minus 369%. The same user on the newest brain, Fable 5.1, runs at about 1%. Which leads to the line that explains the whole business: "Anthropic would likely already have software like margins on their subscription plans if everyone only used Fable".

So the $20 is a price tag. What you get for it is someone else's choice, and it can change.

## THE CATCH, BEFORE A CRITIC SAYS IT

Three things cut the other way. First, the five times is a mid-tier result; at the top, the report says the limits are "quite similar across the board". Second, SemiAnalysis's own June report pointed the other way at $200, so the number moves. Third, more dollars of AI is not always more work done. Another firm, Artificial Analysis, measures the cost of one finished task on a set test, which the industry calls a benchmark, and gets very different answers. SemiAnalysis says it does "not believe the benchmark tasks in the AA Intelligence Index are at all representative of real work". Two careful firms, two measures, two answers. Hold the number loosely.

And one more thing you should know: this publication is made with Claude. We checked every quote against the source's own words, and the receipt picture uses only the numbers in the open. Take the rest with a pinch of salt.

## THE GUY BEHIND THE COUNTER CHANGES THE SCOOP

Here is what the price tag hides. Both companies have changed what their brains do without telling you, more than once this year.

In August 2025, OpenAI's switcher broke on its first day. In Sam Altman's words, "GPT-5 seemed way dumber". By 12 August people had lobbied the old brain back onto the menu. On 4 March 2026, Anthropic quietly lowered how hard Claude Code thinks by default, from high to medium, to save time. Its own write-up on 23 April called it "the wrong tradeoff" and reset everyone's limits. In June, Anthropic's new Fable 5 was built to quietly give worse answers to people it thought were building rival AI. About 48 hours of backlash later, the company said invisible safeguards were "the wrong tradeoff". It now shows you when it moves you to the older Opus 4.8.

Then, in the week before SemiAnalysis published, OpenAI cut its $200 plan. The report's words: OpenAI "picked the nuclear option of just immediately cutting to Fable-level limits across the board", "halving the API-equivalent value for the $200 plan and introducing a new $500 tier". If you bought the old plan, you keep the old limits "until October 29th". After that, the same money buys half as much.

The test even caught a company doing it quietly. One of three identical accounts SemiAnalysis tested had about a fifth less allowance than the other two. The company said it was an "extremely tiny" A/B test on limits. SemiAnalysis's conclusion: "It proves providers can silently change subscription limits at any time".

## THE BETTER THE BRAIN, THE LESS YOU GET

One more line from the report deserves its own section. On a Claude plan, the newest and best brain, Fable 5.1, "can only be used for 50% of your limit". The thing you are paying the most for is the thing you are allowed the least of. That is the business model in one sentence: the cheaper brains are the gift, the best brain is the product, and the plan steers you from one to the other.

## WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

If you use AI, three things you can do on Monday. Ask the app which brain answered; OpenAI said ChatGPT "will continue to tell users which model is active when asked". Watch the usage meter, because the meter is the real price. And pick the plan for the job you actually do, then check it again in a month, because the deal moved twice between June and October.

## WHAT IT MEANS IF YOU BUILD ON AI

Never price your product on a subsidised price. The gift ends, as OpenAI's $200 buyers found out this month. Pick the brain per task yourself. Cognition's Scott Wu described why: for "a lot of boilerplate work", the dull same-again jobs, companies can get five to 10 times better cost efficiency from brains that are still good enough. And build so you can switch, because a company that can change your limits on a Tuesday can change your margins too.

## HOW THE OLD WAY IS CHANGING

Software used to be a price for a product. Now the price is fixed and the product underneath it moves, up with a launch and down with a quiet A/B test. CNBC's conclusion still holds: "Pricing power is shifting from the companies selling premium AI toward the companies buying it." For now, the companies' investors are paying part of everyone's bill. The creative shift is this. People building with these tools now treat the brain as a part, chosen per job. The people selling them now meter it like electricity.

## THREE WAYS THIS COULD GO

**The Vending Machine.** Every AI company makes the switching automatic and hides it. Winners: the AI companies. Losers: people who wanted to pick the smart brain on purpose. First sign: the menu of brains disappears from the app for everyone, paying customers included.

**The Secret Menu.** The switching stays automatic, but a hidden menu opens for people who know to ask, and picking your own brain costs extra. First sign: a "choose your model" switch buried three menus deep.

**The Group Project.** Several brains answer the same question at once and something compares the answers. Slower, pricier, harder to get a "way dumber" day. First sign: your answer takes visibly longer to arrive, on purpose.

Our bet: the Vending Machine.
