I've been using AI apps every day for years now, and I've started to notice something odd about how they're sold. Every launch is about a name. A new model, a new number, a livestream, a chart. So I wanted to break down whether the name you pick actually changes the answer you get.

Let me start by saying this: some of the new models really are better, and the people building them aren't lying to you. But I think the name is the least important thing on the screen. The setting that changes your answer most has a boring label, it's set to off by default, and almost nobody touches it.

## THE NAME GAME

Open Claude Code and the help page lists more than a dozen models you can pick by name. Open ChatGPT and you'll find GPT-6, powered by something called GPT-6 Sol, with GPT-6 Astra behind the Pro option. These names are built for launch days. Each one gets a headline, a benchmark chart and a week of people arguing about it online.

Even the people choosing the names think it's got out of hand. In February 2025, Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, wrote: "We hate the model picker as much as you do." In April 2025 he promised to "fix our model naming by this summer", and said everyone could keep making fun of them until then, "which we very much deserve".

So the name gets the headline. But a headline isn't an answer, and the question that matters is how much the name changes the answer you actually get. For most of what people ask, I don't think it changes much at all.

## WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY DO

The launches skip one awkward fact. Most people don't use AI the way the launches imagine.

A study by OpenAI and Harvard economists looked at 1.5 million ChatGPT conversations. Practical guidance, looking things up and writing make up "nearly 80%" of them. About half of all messages are people simply asking something. Only 4.2% of messages are about computer programming. By July 2025, people were sending more than 2.5 billion messages a day. That's not a lab. That's a search box.

And people asking like that don't open menus. In August 2025, after GPT-5 launched and the app began choosing for people, Altman wrote that the share of free users using a thinking model each day went "from <1% to 7%", and for paying Plus users "from 7% to 24%". The option had been sitting in the menu all along. Hardly anyone had pressed it. For an everyday question, which model answers barely matters. What would have mattered was the setting nobody touched.

## THE SWITCH THAT MATTERS

Picture a small business owner. Call her Priya. She pastes a supplier contract into her AI app and asks what she should worry about. It gives her a tidy, confident answer in four seconds. She signs. Six months later a clause bites her that a careful reader would have caught. (How many of us have signed something an AI waved through?)

Nothing was wrong with the name at the top of her screen. What was missing is that the AI never stopped to think. ChatGPT and Claude both now let you choose how hard the AI thinks. At the low end it answers straight away. Turn it up and it works through the problem first, checking itself before it replies. Think of the name as the badge on the car. The thinking setting is the gear you're in. We argue about badges, and most of us drive everywhere in first.

Here's how big that gap is. OpenAI tested its models on HealthBench Hard, a tough set of medical questions. The old GPT-4o scored 0.0%. The new GPT-5 answering without thinking, which OpenAI's report calls gpt-5-main, scored 25.5%. The same GPT-5 thinking first, gpt-5-thinking, scored 46.2%.

Anthropic says Claude's accuracy on maths questions "improves logarithmically" with the amount of thinking it's allowed. In plain words: the more it thinks, the more it gets right, with each extra bit of thinking adding a little less than the last.

And we've seen what happens when the switch fails. Soon after GPT-5 launched, Altman wrote that the "autoswitcher broke", the part that decided when to think, and that GPT-5 "seemed way dumber". Same name. Same app. Worse answers.

To be fair to the names, they're not nothing. When OpenAI took GPT-4o away at the GPT-5 launch, enough people missed its personality that OpenAI brought it back. But when OpenAI later announced it was retiring GPT-4o, it said "only 0.1% of users" still chose it each day. People missed it. Very few went back to it.

## WHO SETS THE DEFAULT

So who decides how hard your AI thinks? I went looking, and the honest answer took me a while to see. My answer: mostly the app, through its default setting.

In 2025, OpenAI's answer was a router: a hidden part of ChatGPT that reads your question and decides which model answers. OpenAI says the router is "continuously trained on real signals", and that once you hit your usage limit, a smaller "mini" model takes over.

On 14 September 2026, OpenAI retired automatic switching to thinking for its Plus and Pro users. Now the picker is literally a thinking slider: Instant, Medium, High, Extra High. On the Plus and Pro plans, its help page says Instant won't step up on its own, and even asking it to "think harder" won't change the level. So the choice is back with you, and Instant, the quickest level, won't step up for you.

On Claude, each model has an effort level marked "Default", and the help page is honest about the trade: higher effort means more thorough answers, but "you'll reach your usage limits faster".

That's the real reason the default matters. Thinking uses more of the AI's work, which is why it eats your limit faster. My read is that it costs the company more too. The quick setting is the one you get unless you go looking. Remember Altman's figure: among free users, daily use of thinking models had started from under 1%. I don't think that's a conspiracy. It's just what defaults do.

## WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT

For quick, everyday stuff, leave it alone. If you're asking for a recipe or a summary of an email, the fast setting is fine, and the name really doesn't matter.

For anything you'll act on, change the gear. A contract, a medical question, money, a decision you can't take back. On ChatGPT, pick Medium or High. On Claude, turn the effort up. On ChatGPT's free plan, use Think. It costs you a few more seconds, and you'll hit your limit sooner. That's the trade, and for the questions that matter, it's worth it.

If you build products on AI, don't choose a model by its name or its launch chart. Run your own hard questions through each setting, check the answers, and pay for thinking only where it changes the result.

There's a better way the apps could do this. They could show you, under every answer, whether the AI actually stopped to think. A small label, like the gear showing on a car's dashboard. Then you'd know when to ask again.

My prediction. By 30 June 2027, OpenAI will make thinking the starting setting for paying ChatGPT users, instead of Instant. Claude has already gone that way: on its newest top models, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1, thinking is always on. How sure we are: [Krish to set] I'd be wrong if speed keeps winning, and people keep choosing the quick answer over the careful one.

We've spent two years learning the badges. Nobody showed us where the gearstick was.
