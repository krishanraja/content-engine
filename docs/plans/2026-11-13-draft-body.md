In 1902, the French rulers of Hanoi had a rat problem, so they paid a bounty of one cent for every rat tail handed in. The tails poured in. Then people started seeing rats in the streets with no tails, very much alive. Hunters were snipping off the tails and letting the rats go, so they could breed and grow more tails. Some set up rat farms on the edge of the city.

Hanoi wanted dead rats, and it paid for tails, so tails are what it got.

Over the past 18 months, some of the biggest companies in the world ran that experiment again with AI. They told their staff to use it, counted how much they used it, ranked them on it and then backed off without much fuss. This piece is about why counting how much AI your people use makes things worse, and what to count instead.

## A CENT A RAT TAIL

Historian Michael Vann pieced the Hanoi story together from the colonial archives, in a 2003 paper for the journal French Colonial History. Hanoi had just built modern sewers, and they turned out to be a superb home for rats (nobody had asked the rats). Plague was the fear. Paid hunters went down into the tunnels, and when that wasn't enough, the city opened the bounty to everyone.

A tail was the proof because it was small, easy to carry and easy to count. A whole dead rat was none of those things. And that's the trap. Whatever you can count is rarely what you actually want, and once money rides on the count, people get very good at producing it. Hanoi learned that with rats. In 2025, big companies started learning it with AI.

## THE MANDATES

It began sensibly enough. In April 2025, Shopify's chief executive, Tobi Lütke, sent his staff a memo that he then posted in public. "Reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation at Shopify," it said, and it added questions about AI use to performance reviews.

That same month, Duolingo's chief executive, Luis von Ahn, told staff the company would look at AI use when it judged performance. At Coinbase, chief executive Brian Armstrong later said he had fired engineers who would not start using the company's AI coding tools.

Each of these leaders had a fair point. AI tools only help if people actually pick them up, and plenty of staff were ignoring them. But the moment AI use went into a performance review, it turned into a score. And people know what to do with a score.

## THE LEADERBOARDS

Once something is a score, someone builds a chart of it.

At Meta, the owner of Facebook, one employee built an internal leaderboard nicknamed "Claudeonomics". Fortune reported in April 2026 that it ranked around 85,000 staff by how many tokens they had used. Tokens are the small chunks of words an AI reads and writes, and every one of them costs money. It handed out titles such as "Token Legend" and "Cache Wizard" (somebody had fun naming those), and staff got through 60 trillion tokens in 30 days. It came down about two days after the story broke.

Amazon had its own version. Kiro is Amazon's AI coding tool, and an unofficial board called KiroRank ranked staff by how much they used it. CIO reported in May 2026 that some staff set AI agents, programs that keep working on a task by themselves, running on unnecessary tasks just to push up their scores.

One engineer's post caught the mood: "Whenever my PM says something ridiculous I launch 10 sub-agents to analyse him." It was a joke. But the leaderboards meant the joke paid.

That's the rat tail again. Each board counted AI use, and AI use is what it got: lots of it, and no way to tell how much of it did anything.

## THE U-TURNS

Then the companies running the boards turned round.

KiroRank was taken offline, and a senior Amazon vice president said the computing costs of all that usage were too high. In September 2026, Meta said it would no longer use AI adoption dashboards or token counts to judge performance.

Amazon made the clearest statement in October. CNBC reported new guidance telling its engineers that token counts and similar usage numbers are "deeply flawed" measures of success. That guidance also says that when an engineer tries AI on a job and it doesn't help, that is "a finding, not a failing".

That last line matters more than it looks. Once failing to use AI counts against you, nobody admits the AI didn't help. They just keep it running. Usage goes up, and the information you actually needed, where AI works and where it doesn't, disappears.

## HALF THE TARGET, MORE SALES

A study posted to arXiv this autumn shows what happens when a company counts less, and its answer is unusually clean.

Its authors looked at one large medical-device company with about 5,000 staff. It had set a target of 200 AI queries a month per person. When the researchers looked at what people were actually asking, 31% of the queries were repeats or had nothing to do with work. People were hitting their number.

So the company cut the target in half, to 100. Total AI use fell by about 30%, and about 90% of that drop was the junk. The real use stayed. And monthly sales went up 7%.

It rolled the change out branch by branch, so the researchers could compare branches that had the lower target with branches that didn't yet. That's what lets them say the cut did it, rather than a good month.

Read that again from a manager's seat. It asked for less AI and got more of what it was actually paying for. Those junk queries had cost money and time, and they had buried the useful work in noise.

## THE COBRA THAT NEVER WAS

There's a twist. Ask anyone for the famous story about counting the wrong thing, and you'll get cobras. Under British rule in Delhi, so it goes, the government paid for dead cobras, so people bred cobras for the bounty, and when the bounty ended they let them go, leaving the city with more snakes than before.

It's a great story. It is also very hard to prove. The Juggernaut, which went looking, found little evidence to support it. "Cobra effect", the phrase, was coined in 2001 by the German economist Horst Siebert, and the tale has been repeated ever since, mostly by people warning others to check their numbers.

So even the warning got believed without anyone checking it. Hanoi's rat tails, meanwhile, are in the archives.

Both stories point the same way. A count feels like proof, so we stop asking what's behind it. That's as true of an AI usage dashboard as it is of a bag of tails.

For the business, this is the practical bit. A usage number tells you people did more of the thing you counted, and nothing else. What matters is what got done: more sales, faster answers to customers, fewer hours on the monthly report. If a team's AI use doubles and none of those moves, you've bought a lot of rat tails.

**Our prediction.** By 30 June 2027, Shopify will say in public that it no longer uses AI usage as a measure in its performance reviews. Meta and Amazon have already backed away, and a company that put usage questions into its reviews in April 2025 is unlikely to keep them once its peers call the measure flawed. How sure we are: 65%.

It isn't higher because Shopify can change its reviews without saying so, and a change nobody announces is a change we can't check.

## 3 things you can do tomorrow

1. **Scrap any target or ranking for how much AI people use.** If a dashboard, a review form or a team goal counts prompts, tokens or logins, take that line out.
2. **Make "AI didn't help here" a good answer.** Amazon now calls it "a finding, not a failing". Say the same to your team, so nobody fakes their usage to look keen.
3. **Judge AI on a number you already track.** Pick one, such as sales, tickets closed or invoices sent. If it hasn't moved, the AI isn't working yet, whatever the usage says.
