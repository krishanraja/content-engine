## The arc

Five checkpoints, in order. Each passes only when a manager hearing it once could say it back.

1. **The bottom rung is going, and it's measured.** At Anthropic, juniors fell from 27% of technical hires to 9%, and
   from 21% to 14% of technical staff, since early 2025; by August its staff's AI agents out-worked them six to one.
   Outside Anthropic, a study of US firms finds junior employment falling at firms that adopt AI, through slower hiring.
   Pass: the reader can say the two numbers that matter (27% to 9%).
2. **We've seen this before, and the job came back bigger.** ATMs cut the tellers a branch needed from 20 to 13, but
   banks opened 43% more branches and tellers moved to selling. Spreadsheets took 400,000 bookkeeping clerk jobs and
   added 600,000 accountant jobs. Pass: the reader can say why those jobs grew back (the work got cheaper, so people
   bought more of it). The "came back bigger" is our read, labelled as such.
3. **This time the rung that goes is the one people learned on.** The report says juniors are "increasingly removed
   from the object-level work that originally built their skills", and the cut shows up as people never hired
   ("Employment protections for incumbents will not benefit those not hired to begin with"). Pass: the reader can say
   what is different this time.
4. **So who trains your 2030 seniors?** Hiring managers now want "autonomy and judgment", which people used to learn in
   their junior years. Pass: the reader sees the gap as theirs, not Anthropic's.
5. **What to do on Monday.** Before you sign off the next role AI could fill, name who on each team will be a senior in
   2030, and what real work they'll learn on now that AI does the first draft. Pass: the reader names one person.

His story, in his words: "I was at Microsoft in 2008, and I was asked to manually change the format of a 500,000-row spreadsheet. I was just doing the same actions over and over again. I had a hunch that this wasn't supposed to be done manually and looked online for how to loop things. I found Excel macros, which could record a simple action that I can add variables to. Obviously, this turned three weeks' worth of work into a few hours, but it took me a few days to build it. I really threw the dice on that first few days, which I could have completely wasted. I think the lesson there was identifying a repeatable task and then having the perseverance to fix the initial flaws in the system I built. Most people give up at the first system failure and call it the technologies fault." Then: "I dont want this example to feel unrelatable or arrogant though, it can easily." Told humbly: Microsoft as a joke on himself ("Yes, the people who make Excel"), the risk as the point, the lesson credited to the dull job, and no line judging others.
