Disney asked a dying AI company to go bust first, so it could hire the team and leave the lawsuits behind.

The company is Character.AI, the app where millions of people chat with made-up characters. It filed for bankruptcy in Delaware on 12 October. Two of the biggest companies on earth have now paid for pieces of it, two years apart, for two very different reasons. The people who used it, and the people suing it, are left with what remains.

So I spent the week in the court papers with one question. Why did Google and Disney both pay for Character.AI, and what does it mean for any business that runs on someone else's AI?

## GOOGLE PAID FOR THE BRAINS

In August 2024, Google reportedly paid $2.7 billion to use Character.AI's technology. Google told Wired that the deal covered the technology as it stood then, and that it also hired the founders and members of their research team. The "vast majority" of the staff stayed behind, and the app kept running.

Google's reason is easy to read. It was in a race to build the strongest AI, and the people who built Character.AI were some of the best in the world at it. Google didn't want the app. It wanted the brains, and it got them without buying the company.

## DISNEY TRIED RENTING FIRST

Disney's reason starts somewhere else. In December 2025, Disney agreed to put $1 billion into OpenAI and to let more than 200 of its characters appear in Sora, OpenAI's video app. Marvel, Pixar and Star Wars would all be there. It was a big bet, but the characters would be living on someone else's app.

Then on 24 March 2026, OpenAI said it would shut Sora down. The Disney deal ended with it, and reports say no money had changed hands. Disney said it respected the decision, which is the corporate way of saying it had just been left standing in the rain.

## NOW DISNEY IS BUYING THE TEAM

So Disney changed its approach. In September it hired Character.AI's chief executive as its first chief technology officer. Then, as the court papers show, it went for the rest.

Disney has offered jobs to 66 of Character.AI's staff, most of the workforce. Their pay comes to about $60 million a year for three years, around $180 million in all, according to the filings reported by Gizmodo. On top of that, Disney has made the opening $20 million bid for rights to Character.AI's technology, which other buyers can still beat. Wired reports that the list covers its code, its trust and safety tools, its training systems and its age-checking technology.

Here is the part that matters most. The filings say Disney required the deal to go through the bankruptcy court, because of Character.AI's "diminishing cash reserve and significant litigation and regulatory risks". A sale through the court lets a buyer take what it wants, free of the old company's legal claims. Disney gets the people and the code. Character.AI keeps the lawsuits.

My read on why: Disney owns the world's best-known characters, and Character.AI's whole business was letting people talk to characters. After Sora, Disney seems to have decided it would rather own the team that does this than rent space on someone else's app.

## WHO IS LEFT WITH THE SHELL

Two groups stay behind.

The first is the people who used the app. The list of what Disney is bidding for does not include users' data or the app itself, according to Wired. The users get no money from Google's deal or Disney's.

The second is the people suing. Families and dozens of US states have brought claims that Character.AI's chatbots harmed children. Those claims stay with a company that is being sold for parts.

So the money followed the people. Google paid billions for the founders. Disney is paying millions for the rest of the team. Everyone else gets the empty box.

## OUR PREDICTION

Disney is not alone. This year Netflix bought InterPositive, an AI filmmaking start-up, in a deal reported at $587 million. Warner Music bought Sureel AI in June. WildBrain, the company behind the Teletubbies, bought Personality AI in August. The big media companies are moving from renting AI to owning it.

Three of the biggest have not made that move yet. Skydance, which now owns Paramount and Warner Bros after its merger closed on 6 October, says it wants to run like a tech company. Universal Music has signed partnerships with Nvidia, Udio and ElevenLabs but has not bought an AI company, while its rival has. NBCUniversal has made no AI purchase that we could find.

**Our prediction.** By 1 March 2027, Skydance, Universal Music Group and Comcast's NBCUniversal will each have announced buying an AI company or its team. How sure we are: 75%.

## 3 things you can do tomorrow

1. **Write down the three AI tools your business would struggle without, and who makes each one.** Most leaders know the tools. Fewer know whether the maker is a giant, a start-up or a giant's side project.
2. **For the one you lean on most, answer four questions about its maker.** Are its founders still there? Is your tool its main product or a side bet? Is it short of cash? Is it collecting lawsuits? Google took Character.AI's founders two years before the end, Sora was a side bet that vanished, and the court papers warned about cash and lawsuits.
3. **If two of those answers worry you, pick your plan B now.** Choose the tool you would move to, and try it on one real task this month, while you still have time to switch calmly.
