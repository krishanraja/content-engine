# How Krish writes cover titles (26 Nov 2026)

Krish, 26 Nov 2026: "Learn from my old cover titles because I write them myself."

Sources: his picked and rejected lines in content-engine `config/title-verdicts.json` (127 verdicts with his reasons) and the covers live on home.makeyourmindup.ai. Every cover and thumbnail template in this Library gets its words this way. The words are always his pick from a few options; templates never invent final titles.

## The shapes he picks

| Shape | His lines | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Who is doing what to whom** (the most dramatic true claim) | "Claude is eating the jobs of its own humans." "Disney is eating the scraps of bankrupt Character.AI." | A named company, a physical verb, a victim. He rejected the correct-but-flat statistic versions ("Anthropic junior hires: 27% to 9%"). |
| **Statement, then the twist** (two beats) | "Meta staff were ranked by AI use. They gamed it." "AI used to reply. Now it acts first." | The first beat sets up a fact everyone accepts; the short second beat flips it. |
| **Repetition, then the question** | "New Claude. New ChatGPT. Does it even matter?" "Another Claude?" | The pattern is the story; the question points at the reader. |
| **A reveal that flips the assumed fight** | "Muse's real rival: Siri." | Two names you know, the surprise last, short. |
| **The big binary** | "AI music or human music?" | One question the whole piece answers; the reader picks a side before clicking. |
| **What happens next, for you** | "What will AI cost next?" "AI finds the skeletons in your closet." | The payoff lands on the reader, not the companies. |
| **The order of events, with the real number** | "Google paid $2.7B first." | A named company, the real figure, a timeline that implies a story. |
| **Taken apart / reviewed** | "Higgsfield, taken apart." "Meta's Muse AI, reviewed." | Says the format up front, names the thing exactly. |

## Rules that come from his verdicts

1. **Name the real thing exactly as people search it** (Meta's Muse, Claude Duet, Character.AI). Never "they", "it" or "this" standing in for a name ("They Faked AI Use" was rejected for that). "They" is fine only after the name is on the cover ("Meta staff ... They gamed it.").
2. **Lead with the most dramatic true claim**, never the neat summary or the statistic. If the claim is not literally true as written, it does not run (the Fortune correction on the Meta line).
3. **Physical, ordinary verbs**: eating, gamed, acts, finds, paid, locked out, let in.
4. **A stranger understands it in one second.** No insider shorthand from the script ("You're on low", "Old beat new" were called stupid and offensive).
5. **Never** a textbook taxonomy, a straw man, a quiz that gives the answer away, or a line that singles out one country.
6. **Cut to fit only by keeping the claim words.** If it will not fit, use his shorter line that keeps the claim, never a cut that changes it.
7. **Thumbnail words are not the title.** On a thumbnail beside a title, use the shortest version of the claim or the question (often under 4 words: "Another Claude?", "AI now acts first", "What will AI cost next?").
8. **Verdict chips carry the twist on logo covers**: each named company gets one short chip saying what it did ("PAYS", "PAID", "SUING"; "BLOCKED IT", "WELCOMED IT"; "DOES MOST", "MOST PRIVATE").
9. **One word in capitals for emphasis** is fine in a title ("OPPOSITE reasons"); on covers everything is set in Anton capitals anyway.

## Applied to the running example

"Why did pop songs get shorter?" is the article question. In his shapes the cover options become:

- Who did what to whom: "Spotify's 30-second rule is shrinking song intros."
- Statement, then twist: "A play counts at 30 seconds. So intros shrank."
- Big binary: "Skip the intro or lose the stream?"
- Thumbnail words: "30 SECONDS." beside the Spotify logo.

These are review samples only. The live cover words for any piece are his pick.
