Two years ago, picking an AI brain was like picking an ice cream flavour. Chocolate or vanilla. You chose. Now it's an ice cream shop with forty flavours and three sizes. There's a "thinking" scoop that costs extra. And there's a guy behind the counter who sometimes just picks for you without asking. That guy behind the counter is the whole story. Here's how he got there, and where he's taking you next.

## WHAT IT USED TO BE

Quick jargon check: OpenAI and Anthropic call their AIs "models". We'll call them brains. In September 2024, OpenAI gave two new brains, o1-preview and o1-mini, to people on its paid ChatGPT Plus and Team plans, and you picked them yourself from a menu called the model picker. They were built to spend more time thinking before they answered, like a kid who reads the whole maths question before scribbling. OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT, even said it was "working to" get ChatGPT to automatically choose the right model for you. It hadn't yet. You were the one holding the menu.

Anthropic, which makes Claude, sold its brains in three sizes: small, middle and big (Claude 3 Haiku, Sonnet and Opus). That way you could pick your own balance of brains, speed and price. A bit like choosing your bike gear yourself instead of the bike doing it.

## WHAT IT IS NOW

On 7 August 2025, OpenAI made GPT-5 the new default brain in ChatGPT. OpenAI called it "a single auto-switching system". Translation: you stopped picking. The app decided which brain answered each question: a quick one for easy stuff, or one that took extra time to "think" for hard stuff.

It broke: the next day, OpenAI's boss, Sam Altman, said the "autoswitcher was out of commission for a chunk of the day". The result, he said, was that "GPT-5 seemed way dumber". People lobbied hard to get the old brain back, and by 12 August 2025 OpenAI had put GPT-4o back on the menu for paying users. The guy behind the counter had scooped the wrong flavour, and half the shop sent it back.

Then OpenAI said it would "soon begin" sending some sensitive chats to a reasoning brain, the kind that stops and thinks first. One example: chats showing "signs of acute distress", from someone in a really bad way. On 3 October 2025, OpenAI's release notes said: "we've been using our real-time router to direct sensitive parts of conversations" that way. The router is the switcher. By March 2026, paying ChatGPT users had a limit on its thinking brain, GPT-5.4 Thinking. Hit the limit, and you get moved down to a smaller one, GPT-5.4 mini. And big companies can set ChatGPT's automatic mode to use that small brain by default.

Over at Anthropic, the docs say Claude "evaluates each request and decides for itself whether to think and how much". People building apps with Claude can turn its effort setting up to "max", or down to save time and money.

Here's the part that actually matters: the money. On 5 June 2026, CNBC's headline read: "Model routing is a fix for AI overspending. That's a problem for OpenAI and Anthropic." In plain English, letting software pick the brain stops companies overspending on AI, and that's bad news for the companies selling it. Scott Wu runs Cognition, which makes an AI coder called Devin. He said that for a lot of boilerplate work, the dull same-again jobs, companies can get five to 10 times better cost efficiency from brains that are still good enough for the task. That means more work for the money. His example: ask a model to name the third US president. Each one, no matter how expensive, will tell you it was Thomas Jefferson. Paying top price for that is like hiring a professor to tell you what day it is.

Yet Glean's boss, Arvind Jain, has estimated that roughly 95% of company AI use still runs on the most expensive brains. That includes jobs cheaper ones could easily handle. Jeetu Patel, Cisco's chief product officer, did the sums: about $200 of tokens per worker per week comes to about $10,000 a year each. As CNBC explains, tokens are "blocks of data that models use to generate information", and you pay for every one processed. With 90,000 employees, a company is looking at $900 million a year.

CNBC's own conclusion: "Pricing power is shifting from the companies selling premium AI toward the companies buying it." In plain English, the power to set prices is moving to the buyers.

On 22 September 2026 both big AI companies launched cheaper brains. OpenAI added two GPT-6 brains, Sol and Luna. CNBC reported that it also cut the prices it charges app builders by 50%, compared with its special-offer prices for GPT-5.6. Anthropic said its newest Claude, Opus 5.5, "costs 40% less to run than Opus 5".

The price list is now the length of a takeaway menu. OpenAI charges by the token. GPT-6 Luna, the brain OpenAI calls its "most efficient model", costs $0.50 for a million tokens of writing. GPT-6 Astra, its "most capable model", costs $50 for the same. Then you pick a lane. OpenAI's price list says Fast mode costs double, and two cheaper lanes, called Batch and Flex, cost half.

Put it all together and our answer to who picks your brain is, more and more, a switcher. TechCrunch called it "a real-time router" that "decides which model to use for a particular prompt". In plain English, it decides which brain answers each question.

## WHERE IT COULD GO

From here on, this is our guess. For now, you can still see behind the counter. In October 2025, OpenAI said ChatGPT "will continue to tell users which model is active when asked". The bigger shift would come if nobody tells you anything. Then the brain you get depends on what's cheapest that day. That goes for the free user, the student, and the person asking for a recipe.

You would never know if you got the smart brain or the bargain-bin brain. You would just notice, some days, that the answers feel a bit dumber, like people did in August 2025. Only this time, nobody would tell you why, say sorry or bring back the old one.

## THREE WAYS THIS COULD GO

Each one has a silly name, and a first sign that you're living in it.

**The Vending Machine.** Every AI company makes the switching automatic and hides it. Everywhere, all the time. You press a button, something comes out, you never see the machinery. Winners: the AI companies, whose pitch would shift from "our brain is smarter" to "our machine never jams". Losers: people who wanted to pick the smart brain on purpose and can't any more. First sign: the menu of brains disappears from the app for everyone, paying customers included.

**The Secret Menu.** The switching stays automatic for everyone. But a hidden menu opens back up for people who know to ask, and picking your own brain costs extra. Winners: professionals who need a specific brain for a specific job. Losers: everyday users who never knew the secret menu existed. First sign: a "choose your model" switch buried three menus deep, clearly built for people who go looking for it.

**The Group Project.** Several brains, cheap and expensive, answer the same question at once. Something compares the answers before showing you one. It would be slower and pricier per question, but much harder to get a "way dumber" day. Winners: anyone doing something that really matters, like medical questions, legal questions, or code that runs a real business. Losers: the AI companies' profits, because running three brains costs more than running one. First sign: your answer takes visibly longer to arrive than it used to, on purpose.

Our bet: the Vending Machine.

## OUR PREDICTION

By 30 September 2027, at least two of OpenAI, Anthropic and Google will have their software pick the brain automatically, by default, for businesses that build apps on their AI.
